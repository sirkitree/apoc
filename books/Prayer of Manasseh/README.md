# The Prayer of Manasseh

Fifteen verses put into the mouth of the worst king Judah ever had, kneeling in a Babylonian prison: the most concentrated statement of repentance in the biblical literature, and one of the shortest books ever to enter a canon.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**The gap it was written to fill.** 2 Chronicles 33 tells how **Manasseh**, king of Judah, filled Jerusalem with idols, set a carved image in the Temple itself, practised divination, and "made his sons pass through the fire" -- and then, taken with hooks to Babylon by the Assyrians, "besought the Lord his God, and humbled himself greatly," and was restored to his kingdom. The Chronicler then adds, at 33:18--19, that Manasseh's prayer "and how God was intreated of him" are "written in the book of the kings of Israel" and "among the sayings of the seers." That is an explicit reference to a document the reader does not have. The Prayer of Manasseh is what somebody wrote to supply it.

**Original Language:** **Greek**, on the evidence of its vocabulary and style, though a Hebrew original has been argued for and cannot be excluded. It shows no sign of being a translation.

**Key Manuscripts and Witnesses:**

- **Didascalia Apostolorum** (3rd century CE, Syriac and Latin) -- the earliest witness. The Prayer appears there embedded in the text as an illustration of the power of penitence, which is almost certainly how it first circulated: as a liturgical and homiletic piece rather than as a book.
- **Apostolic Constitutions** (Book II, late 4th century) -- transmits it in Greek.
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- includes it among the **Odes**, the collection of biblical canticles appended to the Psalter. This, not a place among the historical books, is its normal home in Greek Bibles.
- **Codex Turicensis** (7th century) -- another Odes witness.
- **Latin** -- it does not appear in the earliest Vulgate manuscripts. It entered the Latin tradition later and was printed in the appendix to the Clementine Vulgate.

**Date of Composition:** Usually placed in the **2nd or 1st century BCE**, though estimates range from the 2nd century BCE to the 1st century CE. It belongs to the same Hellenistic-Jewish penitential milieu as the prayers in Daniel 9, Baruch 1--3, and the Prayer of Azariah.

**Place of Composition:** Unknown; Alexandria and Palestine are both proposed.

## Historical Context

**The historical Manasseh** reigned in Judah for fifty-five years (roughly 687--642 BCE), the longest reign of any Judahite king. The account in 2 Kings 21 is unrelievedly hostile and says nothing whatever about repentance -- it holds Manasseh's sins responsible for the eventual destruction of Jerusalem, a judgement 2 Kings repeats even after Josiah's reforms. The Chronicler's story of exile, repentance and restoration has no parallel in Kings, and its historicity is debated; Assyrian records do list Manasseh as a vassal of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, and a summons to Nineveh or Babylon is plausible enough, but the repentance is the Chronicler's own theological contribution.

**Why the Chronicler needed it.** Chronicles operates on a strict principle of retribution: long reigns are rewards, disasters are punishments. Manasseh's fifty-five years were a problem. The repentance narrative resolves it, and the Prayer -- written centuries later by someone else -- makes the resolution audible.

**Why the tradition kept it.** Manasseh is the limiting case. If *he* could be forgiven, the argument runs, then no one is beyond mercy. The Prayer's entire rhetorical strategy depends on the reader knowing exactly whose voice this is.

## Canonical Status

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Eastern Orthodox** | Canonical or deuterocanonical, depending on the local tradition. Included in the **Odes** appended to the Psalter in Greek Bibles, and used liturgically at Great Compline. |
| **Roman Catholic** | Not canonical. Excluded at Trent (1546); printed in an appendix to the Clementine Vulgate together with 3 and 4 Esdras, "lest they perish altogether." It is nonetheless used in the Liturgy of the Hours. |
| **Anglican / Episcopal** | Not canonical, but in the Apocrypha -- and given a liturgical prominence it has nowhere else. It is appointed as **Canticle 14, "A Song of Penitence,"** in the 1979 American *Book of Common Prayer*, for use in Morning Prayer in Lent. |
| **Lutheran** | In the Apocrypha; Luther included it in his Bible. |
| **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo** | Canonical, in the broader canon. |
| **Protestant (Reformed)** | Not canonical. Apocrypha. |
| **Jewish** | Not canonical. A different Hebrew "Prayer of Manasseh" was found at **Qumran (4Q381)**, but it is not this text. |

## Summary

The Prayer is a single unbroken movement of fifteen verses, and it follows the classical shape of a penitential prayer exactly.

**Invocation (1--7).** It opens not with the sin but with God: "O Lord, Almighty God of our fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and of their righteous seed." God is named as the one who made heaven and earth, who bound the sea by the word of his commandment, who shut up the deep and sealed it. Then comes the pivot on which the whole prayer turns -- God's anger is unbearable, "for the majesty of thy glory cannot be borne, and thine angry threatening toward sinners is importable," **but** "thy merciful promise is unmeasurable and unsearchable." Wrath is finite; mercy is not.

**The theology of repentance (7--8).** God is "of great compassion, longsuffering, very merciful, and repentest of the evils of men." He appointed repentance not for the righteous -- not for Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, who did not sin against him -- but **for sinners, that they may be saved.** "Thou hast appointed repentance unto me that am a sinner."

**Confession (9--10).** "For I have sinned above the number of the sands of the sea. My transgressions, O Lord, are multiplied: my transgressions are multiplied, and I am not worthy to behold and see the height of heaven for the multitude of mine iniquities." The image that follows is the prayer's most vivid: "I am bowed down with many iron bands, that I cannot lift up mine head, neither have any release" -- the literal chains of the prisoner and the metaphorical weight of guilt in one figure. He names his offences: "I have provoked thy wrath, and done evil before thee: I did not thy will, neither kept I thy commandments: I have set up abominations, and have multiplied offences."

**Petition (11--13).** "Now therefore I bow the knee of mine heart, beseeching thee of grace." The phrase *the knee of mine heart* -- κάμπτω γόνυ καρδίας -- is the most quoted line in the book. "I have sinned, O Lord, I have sinned, and I acknowledge mine iniquities: wherefore, I humbly beseech thee, forgive me, O Lord, forgive me, and destroy me not with mine iniquities. Be not angry with me for ever, by reserving evil for me; neither condemn me to the lower parts of the earth. For thou art the God, even the God of them that repent."

**Doxology (14--15).** "And in me thou wilt shew all thy goodness: for thou wilt save me, that am unworthy, according to thy great mercy. Therefore I will praise thee for ever all the days of my life: for all the powers of the heavens do praise thee, and thine is the glory for ever and ever. Amen."

## Key Themes

### Repentance as a Divine Institution
The Prayer's central theological claim is that repentance is not a human achievement but something God *appointed* -- and appointed specifically for sinners, not for the righteous who have no use for it. This is a considerable idea compressed into two verses, and it anticipates the logic of "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance."

### No One Beyond Mercy
The choice of speaker is the argument. Manasseh is the man the biblical historians hold responsible for the destruction of Jerusalem. Putting the model penitential prayer in his mouth is a deliberate statement about the reach of forgiveness, and it is why the Prayer became a standard text for pastoral use with people who believed themselves past help.

### The Knee of the Heart
Verse 11's image insists that the outward posture of penitence is worth nothing without the inward one -- a theme running from the prophets ("rend your heart, and not your garments") through the Psalms to the whole later tradition of interior devotion.

### Wrath Measured, Mercy Unmeasured
The prayer's structural asymmetry is its theology: God's anger toward sinners is real and unbearable, and it is nevertheless the smaller of the two quantities. Everything after verse 6 depends on that comparison.

## Influence & Legacy

### Liturgy
The Prayer's liturgical career far exceeds its canonical standing:
- **Eastern Orthodox** -- read at **Great Compline**, especially during Lent, as one of the Odes.
- **Anglican / Episcopal** -- **Canticle 14, "A Song of Penitence,"** in the 1979 *Book of Common Prayer*, appointed for Morning Prayer in Lent. This is the appearance most English-speaking Christians encounter.
- **Roman Catholic** -- in the Liturgy of the Hours, and formerly among the responsories of the Roman Breviary.

### Music
The Prayer has been set repeatedly. The best known settings are the Orthodox chant traditions and, in the West, settings of the *Song of Penitence* for Anglican use; individual verses -- especially "I bow the knee of mine heart" -- have been set independently many times.

### Devotional and Pastoral Use
From the *Didascalia* onward, the Prayer was used as the standard example in Christian teaching on penance -- the demonstration case that God receives the worst of sinners. It occupied that role in catechesis and in the penitential handbooks of the medieval West, and it retains it in Orthodox pastoral practice.

### Literary Afterlife
The image of the man in irons who cannot lift his head, and the phrase "the knee of my heart," passed into English devotional writing after 1611 and recur in Puritan and Anglican prose throughout the seventeenth century.
