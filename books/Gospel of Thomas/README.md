# The Gospel of Thomas

A collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, hidden for nearly 1,600 years in the Egyptian desert, and one of the most debated texts in modern biblical scholarship.

---

## Source and Manuscripts

### The Nag Hammadi Discovery (1945)

In December 1945, near the town of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, a local farmer named Muhammad Ali al-Samman unearthed a sealed clay jar containing thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices. Among them, in Codex II, was the only complete surviving copy of the Gospel of Thomas -- written in Coptic, the late form of the Egyptian language. The manuscript itself dates to around 340 CE, but the text it preserves is considerably older.

This collection, now known as the Nag Hammadi Library, contained over fifty texts and became one of the most significant archaeological finds of the twentieth century, transforming our understanding of early Christianity and its diversity.

### The Oxyrhynchus Papyri

Remarkably, fragments of the Gospel of Thomas had already been found decades earlier -- scholars just did not know what they had. Three Greek papyrus fragments (P.Oxy. 1, P.Oxy. 654, and P.Oxy. 655), discovered at Oxyrhynchus, Egypt, between 1897 and 1903, were originally catalogued simply as *Logia Iesu* ("Sayings of Jesus"). It was only after the Nag Hammadi discovery that scholars recognized these fragments as portions of the Gospel of Thomas. These Greek fragments date to between 130 and 250 CE, providing crucial evidence that the text circulated in Greek well before the Coptic translation was made.

### Coptic vs. Greek: Which Came First?

The scholarly consensus holds that the Gospel of Thomas was originally composed in Greek. However, the relationship between the Greek and Coptic versions is not straightforward. The wording of the Coptic text sometimes diverges significantly from the earlier Greek Oxyrhynchus fragments, suggesting that the Gospel of Thomas may have circulated in more than one form and passed through several stages of editing. Some scholars have also proposed a Syriac origin, pointing to wordplay and idioms in the text that work better in Syriac than in Greek.

### Date of Composition

When the Gospel of Thomas was originally written remains one of the most contested questions in the field. Proposals range widely:

- **Early dating (50-80 CE):** Some scholars argue that Thomas preserves very early oral traditions, possibly independent of and even predating the canonical Gospels. Helmut Koester of Harvard and members of the Jesus Seminar have championed versions of this view.
- **Mid-range dating (80-140 CE):** Many scholars place composition in the late first or early second century, roughly contemporary with the latest canonical Gospels.
- **Late dating (140-250 CE):** Others argue that Thomas shows dependence on the canonical Gospels and reflects second-century Gnostic theology, making it a later composition.

The majority of scholars settle somewhere in the early to mid-second century, though the debate remains very much alive.

## Historical Context

### A Window into Early Christian Diversity

The Gospel of Thomas offers a glimpse into a form of Christianity that looked quite different from what eventually became orthodox. In the first and second centuries, there was no single "Christianity" -- there were many competing movements, each with its own scriptures, practices, and understandings of who Jesus was and what he taught. Thomas represents one strand of that diverse tapestry.

The text is attributed to "Didymos Judas Thomas" -- *Didymos* being Greek for "twin" and *Thomas* being the Aramaic equivalent. In some early Syrian Christian traditions, Thomas was regarded as the twin brother of Jesus and held special authority. Most scholars, however, do not consider the apostle Thomas to be the actual author; the attribution is a common ancient literary convention.

### The Q Source Hypothesis

One of the most intriguing aspects of the Gospel of Thomas is its format: a list of sayings with no narrative framework, no birth story, no crucifixion, no resurrection account. This is strikingly similar to what scholars believe the hypothetical "Q source" looked like -- a lost sayings collection that the authors of Matthew and Luke are thought to have drawn upon alongside the Gospel of Mark.

The existence of Thomas as a real, physical sayings gospel bolsters the plausibility of Q. Approximately 37 percent of the reconstructed Q source has parallels in Thomas. Some scholars, notably John Dominic Crossan, have explored the idea that Thomas and Q share a common oral or written tradition. Others caution that these parallels may simply reflect the widespread circulation of Jesus' sayings in the early church.

### Gnostic or Not?

Whether the Gospel of Thomas is "Gnostic" is itself a major scholarly debate. The text was found alongside clearly Gnostic works at Nag Hammadi, and some of its sayings do resonate with Gnostic themes -- the emphasis on hidden knowledge, the devaluation of the material world, the idea of a divine spark within each person. However, many of its sayings contain nothing distinctively Gnostic and closely parallel the canonical Gospels. Some scholars argue that Thomas is better understood as an early "wisdom gospel" that was later adopted (and perhaps edited) by Gnostic communities rather than a Gnostic composition from the start.

## Canonical Status

### Why It Was Excluded

The Gospel of Thomas was never accepted as canonical scripture by any major Christian tradition. Several factors contributed to its exclusion:

- **Late attribution and uncertain authorship.** While attributed to the apostle Thomas, the text was widely regarded as pseudepigraphal (falsely attributed).
- **Theological divergence.** Thomas lacks the narrative of Jesus' death and resurrection -- the very core of orthodox Christian proclamation. Its emphasis on secret knowledge and self-discovery did not fit the emerging creedal framework.
- **Association with Gnosticism.** As proto-orthodox Christianity defined itself in opposition to Gnostic movements, texts associated with those movements were viewed with deep suspicion.
- **Timing.** By the mid-second century, the four canonical Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John) were already broadly accepted as authoritative. Thomas arrived too late to the table, or at least was perceived to have done so.

### Early Church Responses

The early Church Fathers were not silent about the Gospel of Thomas. Several explicitly condemned it:

- **Hippolytus of Rome (c. 222-235 CE)** identified it as a heretical text in his *Refutation of All Heresies*.
- **Origen of Alexandria (c. 233 CE)** referenced it critically in a homily, distinguishing it from accepted scripture.
- **Eusebius of Caesarea (before 326 CE)** rejected it as an "absurd, impious, and heretical fiction" in his *Church History*.
- **Cyril of Jerusalem (347-348 CE)** warned followers to avoid it as heretical in his *Catechesis*.
- **Pope Gelasius I (5th century)** included it on a list of prohibited books.

These condemnations, paradoxically, are part of the reason we knew the Gospel of Thomas existed long before the Nag Hammadi discovery. The church fathers preserved its name and reputation even as they tried to suppress its content.

## Summary

The Gospel of Thomas opens with a prologue:

> *"These are the hidden sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down."*

What follows is not a story but a list -- 114 sayings, each typically introduced with "Jesus said," occasionally set within a brief dialogue between Jesus and his disciples. There is no birth narrative, no miracles, no crucifixion, no resurrection. The text is entirely focused on the *words* of Jesus, presented as bearers of hidden meaning that the reader must actively interpret.

The sayings range widely in form and content:

- **Parables** familiar from the canonical Gospels (the mustard seed, the sower, the pearl of great price) appear here, often in shorter, sometimes strikingly different versions.
- **Wisdom sayings** challenge the reader to look beyond appearances: *"If your leaders say to you, 'Look, the kingdom is in the sky,' then the birds of the sky will precede you"* (Saying 3).
- **Dialogues** with named disciples -- Peter, Matthew, Mary -- sometimes reveal tensions within the early Christian community.
- **Cryptic declarations** push toward mystical territory: *"I am the light that is over them all. I am the All; the All has come forth from me, and the All has attained unto me. Cleave a piece of wood: I am there. Raise up the stone, and you shall find me there"* (Saying 77).

Roughly half of the 114 sayings have parallels in the canonical Gospels, though often with notable differences. The other half are unique to Thomas, and some of these are among the most provocative sayings attributed to Jesus in any ancient text.

## Key Themes

### The Kingdom Within

Perhaps the most distinctive theme in Thomas is its radical reimagining of the "Kingdom of God." Where the canonical Gospels often speak of the Kingdom as a future event -- something that will come with dramatic, apocalyptic force -- Thomas insists it is already here, spread across the earth, waiting to be recognized:

> *"The kingdom of the Father is spread out upon the earth, and people do not see it."* (Saying 113)

> *"The kingdom is inside you and it is outside you. When you know yourselves, then you will be known, and you will understand that you are the children of the living Father."* (Saying 3)

This is not a kingdom of thrones and armies but a state of awareness -- present, immanent, available to anyone who learns to see.

### Self-Knowledge as Salvation

Thomas places extraordinary emphasis on knowing oneself as the path to knowing God. This is not the introspection of modern self-help but a radical theological claim: that the divine is not merely "out there" but also within each person, and that recognizing this is the key to liberation.

> *"Whoever discovers the meaning of these sayings won't taste death."* (Saying 1)

### Synoptic Parallels and Divergences

For readers familiar with the New Testament, one of the most fascinating aspects of Thomas is encountering familiar sayings in unfamiliar forms. Several scholars have argued that in some cases, Thomas preserves a version of a saying that is closer to its original form than the canonical version. For example, the Parable of the Wicked Tenants (Saying 65) appears in a shorter, less allegorized form than its counterparts in Mark, Matthew, and Luke, leading some to suggest Thomas reflects an earlier layer of the tradition.

These parallels and differences make Thomas an invaluable resource for understanding how Jesus' teachings were transmitted, adapted, and interpreted across different communities.

### Seeking, Finding, and Being Troubled

The very first saying sets the tone for the entire text: the path to understanding is not comfortable. It requires persistent seeking, and what one finds may be disturbing before it becomes illuminating:

> *"Whoever seeks shouldn't stop until they find. When they find, they'll be disturbed. When they're disturbed, they'll be amazed, and reign over the All."* (Saying 2)

This theme of transformative discovery runs throughout the gospel, casting the reader not as a passive recipient of doctrine but as an active seeker.

## Influence and Legacy

### Impact on Historical Jesus Research

The Gospel of Thomas has become one of the most important non-canonical texts for scholars studying the historical Jesus. The Jesus Seminar, founded in 1985, controversially treated Thomas as a "fifth gospel," arguing that some of its sayings might be more authentic than material in the Gospel of John. While this position remains contested, it brought Thomas into the center of mainstream biblical scholarship.

Helmut Koester at Harvard and his students initiated research in the 1970s and 1980s examining how Jesus' oral teachings came to be written down. Their work suggested that some sayings in Thomas belong to the earliest recoverable stratum of the Jesus tradition. Even scholars who dispute an early date for the text acknowledge that Thomas provides a unique window into how Jesus' words were remembered and transmitted outside the canonical tradition.

### Modern Scholarship

The Gospel of Thomas continues to generate a remarkable volume of academic work. Key areas of ongoing research include:

- The relationship between Thomas and the Synoptic Gospels (dependence vs. independence)
- The nature and extent of Thomas's "Gnosticism"
- The social setting of the community that produced and used the text
- The text's implications for understanding early Christian diversity

Major scholarly contributions include works by Marvin Meyer, Stephen Patterson, April DeConick, Simon Gathercole, and Elaine Pagels, among many others.

### Popular Culture

The Gospel of Thomas crossed from academic circles into popular awareness largely through Elaine Pagels' 2003 bestseller *Beyond Belief: The Secret Gospel of Thomas*, which introduced the text to a wide general audience. The broader cultural fascination with "lost gospels" -- amplified by Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* and related media -- kept Thomas in the public eye throughout the 2000s and beyond.

For many modern readers, especially those drawn to contemplative or mystical spirituality, Thomas offers an image of Jesus as a wisdom teacher and guide to inner awakening rather than a doctrinal authority -- a figure who says, in effect, "the answers are within you, if you learn to look."
