# 1 Esdras

A Greek retelling of the return from Babylonian exile and the rebuilding of the Temple, running parallel to Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah -- but interrupted by a story found nowhere else in the Bible: three young bodyguards debating what is the strongest thing in the world.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original Language:** 1 Esdras survives in Greek, and the Greek is notably better -- freer, more idiomatic, less wooden -- than the Greek of the canonical Ezra-Nehemiah in the Septuagint. Most of the book corresponds to material that exists in Hebrew and Aramaic in the Masoretic Text (2 Chronicles 35--36, Ezra, Nehemiah 7:73--8:12), so the translator was working from a Semitic source. The one long section without any Hebrew parallel, the Debate of the Three Guardsmen (3:1--5:6), may itself be a translation from Aramaic, or may have been composed in Greek.

**A note on names:** the naming of the Esdras books is a genuine tangle, and it is the single most common source of confusion about this text.

| Tradition | This book is called | Canonical Ezra-Nehemiah is called |
|-----------|--------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Septuagint (Greek)** | Esdras A (1 Esdras) | Esdras B |
| **Vulgate / Latin** | 3 Esdras | 1 and 2 Esdras |
| **King James Apocrypha** | 1 Esdras | Ezra, Nehemiah |
| **Slavonic Bible** | 2 Esdras | 1 Esdras, Nehemiah |

This collection follows the King James Apocrypha convention, which is also the convention of most English-language scholarship: **1 Esdras** is the Greek parallel history in this directory, and **2 Esdras** is the apocalypse of Ezra found in the [2 Esdras](../2%20Esdras/) directory.

**Key Manuscripts:**

- **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century CE) -- a principal witness to the Greek text.
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- the other principal witness; it differs from Vaticanus in a number of readings.
- **Old Latin** version -- an important secondary witness, since Jerome declined to translate the book afresh for the Vulgate.
- **Josephus** -- the *Jewish Antiquities* (Book 11) follows 1 Esdras rather than canonical Ezra in retelling this period, which makes Josephus an indirect first-century witness to the text and shows it was the version in circulation among Greek-speaking Jews.

**Date of Composition:** The Greek translation is usually dated to roughly **200--150 BCE**, on the strength of its vocabulary and its use by Josephus. The underlying Semitic material is of course older, being a form of the Chronicles-Ezra tradition.

**Place of Composition:** Egypt, most likely Alexandria, is the usual proposal, on the same grounds that place the rest of the Septuagint translation work there.

## Historical Context

**Historical Setting:** The book covers roughly two centuries, from the Passover celebrated by **King Josiah** in Jerusalem (c. 622 BCE) through the destruction of the city by Nebuchadnezzar (586 BCE), the Persian conquest, the decree of **Cyrus** permitting the exiles to return (538 BCE), the rebuilding of the Temple under **Zerubbabel** and **Jeshua**, and finally the mission of **Ezra the scribe** and his reading of the Law to the assembled people.

**The scholarly problem:** 1 Esdras is not simply a loose translation of Ezra-Nehemiah. It begins earlier (with Josiah), ends earlier and more abruptly (mid-sentence, in the middle of Nehemiah 8:13), omits almost all of Nehemiah, and places some material in a different order. Scholars have argued about what this means for more than a century, and the debate falls into two broad camps:

1. **The fragment hypothesis** -- 1 Esdras is what survives of a once-larger Greek work, perhaps a complete Chronicles-Ezra-Nehemiah, and its abrupt ending is simply damage.
2. **The compilation hypothesis** -- 1 Esdras is a deliberate composition, excerpted and rearranged from existing material to tell a particular story: the story of the Temple, from its last great Passover before the exile to its restoration and the restoration of the Law.

The second view has gained ground, in part because the book's structure is so coherent when read as a Temple history. A third and more radical position holds that 1 Esdras preserves an *older* arrangement of the material than the Masoretic Ezra-Nehemiah, in which case it is a witness to the literary history of the canonical books themselves. On any of these views the book matters for how Ezra-Nehemiah came to have the shape it now has.

**Purpose:** To tell the story of the Second Temple as an unbroken thread -- worship interrupted by sin and exile, then restored by God working through foreign kings -- and to make the case that the restoration of correct worship and the restoration of the Law are a single act.

## Canonical Status

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Eastern Orthodox** | Canonical, under the name **1 Esdras** (Greek Ἔσδρας Αʹ). Included in the Greek and Slavonic Bibles. |
| **Roman Catholic** | Not canonical. The Council of Trent (1546) excluded it, and in the Clementine Vulgate it was printed as **3 Esdras** in an appendix after the New Testament, "lest they perish altogether," along with 4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasseh. |
| **Anglican / Lutheran** | Not canonical, but included in the Apocrypha and appointed for reading. Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles lists "The Third Book of Esdras" and "The Fourth Book of Esdras" among the books the Church reads "for example of life and instruction of manners." |
| **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo** | Canonical, in the broader canon. |
| **Protestant (Reformed)** | Not canonical. Classified as apocrypha and generally omitted from printed Bibles after the 19th century. |
| **Jewish** | Not canonical. Never part of the Hebrew Bible, though the material it retells is. |

## Summary

### The Last Passover and the Fall of Jerusalem (1:1--58)

The book opens on a high note. **Josiah** keeps a Passover in Jerusalem of a magnificence unmatched since the days of Samuel -- the priests in order, the Levites in their courses, the singers in their places, the whole apparatus of correct worship functioning as it should. It is the book's picture of what Israel is supposed to look like.

It does not last. Josiah goes out to fight **Pharaoh Neco** at Megiddo despite a warning delivered in God's name, is wounded by archers, and dies. What follows is a rapid, grim procession of kings -- Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, Zedekiah -- each worse than the last, each a puppet of Egypt or Babylon. The people mock the prophets. Finally **Nebuchadnezzar** takes the city, burns the Temple, breaks down the walls, carries off the sacred vessels, and deports the survivors to Babylon. The land, the narrator says, keeps its sabbaths at last: it lies desolate for seventy years.

### The Decree of Cyrus and the First Return (2:1--30)

**Cyrus of Persia**, in the first year of his reign, is stirred by God to proclaim that the God of heaven has charged him to build a house at Jerusalem. He releases the exiles and returns the Temple vessels -- counted out, item by item, into the hands of **Sheshbazzar**. But the neighbouring peoples write to **Artaxerxes** denouncing the returned Jews as a rebellious city that will withhold tribute, and the king orders the work stopped. Construction halts "until the second year of the reign of Darius."

### The Debate of the Three Guardsmen (3:1--5:6)

Here 1 Esdras departs from every other biblical account, and the book becomes something else entirely.

**King Darius** holds a great feast and then goes to bed. Three young men of his personal bodyguard, keeping watch, propose a contest: each will write down what he believes is the strongest thing in the world, and the king will judge, and the winner will be clothed in purple, drink from gold, and be called kinsman of the king.

- The first writes: **wine is strongest**. It makes kings and orphans, slaves and free men, think alike; it turns every mind to mirth and makes men forget sorrow and debt; and when they are done they remember nothing.
- The second writes: **the king is strongest**. Men labour, sail, fight and die at his word; he says kill, and they kill; he says release, and they release; the whole earth bends to one man.
- The third -- **Zerubbabel** -- writes: **women are strongest, but above all things truth bears away the victory.**

Zerubbabel's speech is the finest piece of writing in the book. Women, he argues, are stronger than kings, because kings are born of women, raised by women, and undone by women; he describes a king sitting in state while his concubine takes the crown off his head and puts it on her own and slaps his face, and the king sits there with his mouth open, watching her, and laughs when she laughs. Then Zerubbabel turns: yes, women are strong, and wine is strong, and the king is strong -- but they are all unrighteous and they all perish. **"Great is truth, and mighty above all things."** Truth endures, is strong for ever, lives and conquers for evermore. The whole assembly shouts, *Great is truth, and mighty above all things!*

The Latin of that acclamation -- ***Magna est veritas, et praevalet*** -- became one of the most widely quoted tags in Western literature.

Darius offers Zerubbabel whatever he asks. Zerubbabel asks not for purple or gold but for the king to keep the vow he made when he came to the throne: **to rebuild Jerusalem and restore the Temple vessels.** Darius grants it, with letters, escorts, timber, and tax exemptions, and Zerubbabel leads a second return.

### The Rebuilding of the Temple (5:7--7:15)

A long register of the returning families follows -- names, numbers, and the priests who could not prove their descent and were therefore barred from the priesthood until a priest should arise "with Urim and Thummim." The altar is set up, the foundation of the Temple laid. When it is laid, the people shout for joy, but the old men who had seen the first house weep aloud, and the noise of the weeping and the noise of the joy cannot be told apart.

The Samaritan neighbours offer to help build and are refused; opposition and legal obstruction follow. The prophets **Haggai** and **Zechariah** urge the work forward. **Tattenai**, the Persian governor, challenges the builders' authority and writes to Darius; a search of the royal archives at Ecbatana turns up the original scroll of Cyrus's decree, and Darius orders that the work be funded from the royal revenue and that anyone who interferes be impaled on a beam from his own house. The Temple is finished in the sixth year of Darius and dedicated with sacrifices, and the Passover is kept.

### Ezra and the Reading of the Law (8:1--9:55)

**Ezra**, priest and scribe, "learned in the law of Moses," comes up from Babylon with a further company of exiles and with a commission from Artaxerxes granting him money, vessels, and authority to appoint judges. He proclaims a fast at the river Theras rather than ask the king for a military escort, being ashamed to admit he did not trust God to protect them.

On arrival he learns that the returned community, including priests and Levites, have intermarried with the surrounding peoples. Ezra tears his clothes, pulls out his hair, and prays a long public prayer of confession. A great assembly is called in the rain; the foreign marriages are dissolved, and the men involved are listed by name.

The book closes with Ezra standing on a wooden platform before the whole people -- men, women, and "all who could hear with understanding" -- reading the Law from dawn until noon, with Levites moving through the crowd explaining it. The people weep. Ezra tells them not to weep, for the day is holy: **"Go your way, eat the fat and drink the sweet, and send portions to them that have nothing."** And the Levites quiet the people, saying, "This day is holy; be not sorrowful."

The Greek text then stops mid-sentence: *"And they came together..."* The corresponding passage in Nehemiah 8:13 continues into the keeping of the Feast of Booths. Whether the ending was lost or the compiler simply stopped is the oldest unsettled question about the book.

## Key Themes

### Truth Above Power
The Debate of the Three Guardsmen is a wisdom contest in the mould of the Egyptian and Persian court tales, and its verdict is the theological centre of the book. Wine, kings and women are real powers in the world; the book does not pretend otherwise. But they are transient and morally compromised, and truth is neither. Everything that follows -- the archive search that vindicates the builders, the reading of the Law that binds the community -- is the working out of that verdict in political life.

### Right Worship as the Thread of History
1 Esdras begins with a perfect Passover and ends with a perfect reading of the Law, and the whole intervening ruin is framed as the interruption of correct worship. The book is arguably the most temple-centred narrative in the biblical tradition: the vessels are counted out of the Temple in chapter 1 and counted back in chapter 2, and the reader is meant to notice.

### God Working Through Foreign Kings
Cyrus, Darius and Artaxerxes are not converts, and the book does not claim they are. They are instruments. The decree of a Persian king, retrieved from a Persian archive, is what makes the house of God possible. This is a distinctly post-exilic theology of history, and it stands in deliberate contrast to the theology of the monarchy that the opening chapter has just watched collapse.

### The Restored Community and Its Boundaries
The book's treatment of the foreign marriages is uncomfortable to modern readers and was meant to be uncomfortable to its first ones -- the men named in the final list are named because naming was the point. The community that returns is small, vulnerable, and preoccupied with the question of who belongs to it. The genealogical registers, the priests who cannot prove their descent, and the dissolved marriages are all facets of one anxiety.

## Influence & Legacy

### "Great is truth, and mighty above all things"
Zerubbabel's acclamation (4:41) is the book's lasting contribution to the Western vocabulary. In its Latin form, ***Magna est veritas, et praevalet***, it became a proverb, a motto, and a stock quotation. It appears on the arms and mottoes of institutions, in Sir Thomas Browne, in Coventry Patmore's poem of that title, and -- as the ironic epigraph to a story about the impossibility of telling the truth -- in Aldous Huxley. The Latin is quoted far more often than the book it comes from is read.

### Josephus and the Shape of the Story
Because Josephus followed 1 Esdras rather than canonical Ezra in the *Jewish Antiquities*, this book's version of the restoration -- including the guardsmen's debate -- was the version that reached a great many later readers of Jewish history, Christian and Jewish alike, through him.

### The Textual History of Ezra-Nehemiah
For modern biblical scholarship, 1 Esdras's chief importance is technical but considerable: it is independent evidence about how Chronicles-Ezra-Nehemiah reached its final form. Any reconstruction of the composition of Ezra-Nehemiah has to explain 1 Esdras, and different explanations produce quite different histories of the canonical text.

### Liturgical and Devotional Afterlife
Ezra's instruction to the weeping people -- **"eat the fat and drink the sweet, and send portions to them that have nothing"** -- passed into Jewish observance of Rosh Hashanah through its Nehemiah parallel, and into Christian preaching on almsgiving and festival joy. It is one of the warmest sentences in the restoration literature.
