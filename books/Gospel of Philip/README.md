# The Gospel of Philip

A Valentinian Gnostic anthology of mystical sayings, sacramental theology, and esoteric reflections on the nature of truth, marriage, and spiritual transformation -- one of the most theologically rich texts recovered from the sands of Upper Egypt.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Gospel of Philip survives in a single Coptic manuscript, discovered in December 1945 near the town of **Nag Hammadi** in Upper Egypt. A local farmer named Muhammad Ali al-Samman unearthed a sealed clay jar containing thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices -- a cache that would become known as the **Nag Hammadi Library**, one of the most significant manuscript discoveries of the twentieth century.

The Gospel of Philip is the third text in **Codex II** (Nag Hammadi Codex II, tractate 3), bound alongside the better-known *Gospel of Thomas*. The manuscript itself dates to the **4th century CE** and is now held at the **Coptic Museum in Cairo** (Inv. 10544). However, the text was almost certainly composed earlier -- most scholars place its original composition in **Greek** during the **late 2nd or 3rd century CE**, after the initial activity of the Gnostic teacher Valentinus (who was active in Rome around 138-158 CE).

The Coptic text suffers from significant lacunae (gaps) at the bottom of nearly every page, which means some of the most provocative passages -- including the famous description of Jesus and Mary Magdalene -- are frustratingly incomplete.

The text is not a "gospel" in the narrative sense of Matthew or Mark. It contains no birth story, no Passion narrative, no linear account of Jesus's ministry. Instead, it is best understood as a **florilegium** -- an anthology or collection of excerpts. The German scholar Hans-Martin Schenke divided it into approximately **127 independent sayings**, drawn from various Valentinian sources including catechetical instruction, biblical interpretation, and liturgical reflection. Bentley Layton described it as "a Valentinian anthology containing some one hundred short excerpts taken from various other works." Despite this patchwork character, scholars have noted organizational principles and thematic clustering that give the text a coherent, if meditative, flow.

## Historical Context

The Gospel of Philip belongs to the stream of early Christianity known as **Valentinian Gnosticism**, named after the Egyptian-born teacher **Valentinus** (c. 100-180 CE). Valentinus studied in Alexandria and later taught in Rome, where he was reportedly a candidate for the position of Bishop of Rome. His followers developed an elaborate mythological and sacramental system that rivaled proto-orthodox Christianity in sophistication and popularity during the 2nd and 3rd centuries.

Valentinian Christians were not marginal cultists. They attended the same churches as other Christians, read many of the same scriptures, and participated in the same sacraments -- but they understood all of these through a radically different theological lens. Where proto-orthodox Christians saw a fallen world redeemed through Christ's sacrifice, Valentinians saw a cosmos born from ignorance and error, in which the fundamental human problem was not sin but **lack of knowledge** (*agnosis*). Salvation came through **gnosis** -- direct, experiential knowledge of one's divine origin and ultimate destiny.

The Gospel of Philip is the most **sacramentally oriented** text in the Nag Hammadi collection. It describes what appear to be **five sacraments** or ritual stages:

1. **Baptism** -- initiation into the community
2. **Chrism** (anointing) -- described as superior even to baptism, and the source of the name "Christian"
3. **Eucharist** -- the sacred meal
4. **Redemption** -- a ritual of liberation
5. **Bridal Chamber** -- the highest and most mysterious sacrament

This sacramental framework suggests the text may have functioned, at least in part, as a **catechetical manual** -- a guide for initiates being led through progressive stages of spiritual understanding.

## Canonical Status

The Gospel of Philip was never a serious candidate for inclusion in the New Testament canon. Several factors placed it firmly outside the boundaries drawn by the emerging orthodox church:

- **Late composition**: Written at least 150-200 years after the events it discusses, the text could not claim apostolic authorship in any credible sense. While it is titled "The Gospel of Philip," there is no evidence the apostle Philip had any connection to it.

- **Gnostic theology**: The text's cosmology -- in which the world was created through a "mistake" and the creator deity is flawed -- directly contradicted the proto-orthodox doctrine that the God of the Hebrew Bible and the Father of Jesus Christ were one and the same.

- **Heresiological condemnation**: The Valentinian tradition to which this text belongs was vigorously denounced by influential early church writers. **Irenaeus of Lyon** attacked Valentinianism extensively in *Against Heresies* (c. 180 CE). **Tertullian** wrote a dedicated treatise, *Against the Valentinians* (early 3rd century). **Epiphanius of Salamis** catalogued Valentinian beliefs among his list of eighty heresies in the *Panarion* (c. 374-377 CE).

- **Sacramental secrecy**: The text's insistence that the highest mysteries were reserved for a spiritual elite ("A bridal chamber is not for the animals, nor is it for the slaves, nor for defiled women; but it is for free men and virgins") ran counter to the increasingly universal and institutional character of orthodox Christianity.

The Gospel of Philip was effectively lost to history after the suppression of Gnostic communities in the 4th and 5th centuries. The Nag Hammadi manuscripts appear to have been buried around 367 CE, possibly in response to Archbishop Athanasius's famous Easter letter ordering the destruction of non-canonical texts.

## Summary

The Gospel of Philip reads less like a story and more like a **stream of contemplation** -- a sequence of meditations, parables, paradoxes, and theological reflections loosely organized around recurring themes. For a first-time reader, the experience can feel disorienting. There is no plot. Ideas surface, submerge, and resurface in new forms. Yet the text rewards patience with passages of startling beauty and intellectual power.

### The Nature of Names and Deception

The text opens with reflections on spiritual identity and inheritance, then moves into one of its most distinctive themes: **the deceptiveness of names**. Words like "God," "Father," "Son," "Holy Spirit," "resurrection," and "Church" are declared to be misleading -- they "divert our thoughts from what is correct to what is incorrect." The archons (cosmic rulers) use names to enslave humanity, binding people to shadows instead of realities. True knowledge requires moving beyond names to the realities they only dimly reflect.

### Sacramental Instruction

Extended passages explore the five Valentinian sacraments, building from baptism to the culminating mystery of the bridal chamber. The text uses the **Jerusalem Temple** as an architectural metaphor: baptism corresponds to the outer Holy place, redemption to the Holy of the Holy, and the bridal chamber to the innermost Holy of Holies -- the sacred space where only the high priest may enter.

### The Mary Magdalene Passage

Perhaps the most famous section appears in saying 55 (by Schenke's numbering), where three women named Mary are identified as walking with Jesus: "his mother, and her sister, and Magdalene, the one who was called his companion." Later, in an even more provocative passage:

> *"As for the Wisdom who is called 'the barren,' she is the mother of the angels. And the companion of the [...] Mary Magdalene. [...] loved her more than all the disciples, and used to kiss her often on her mouth. The rest of the disciples [...]. They said to him, 'Why do you love her more than all of us?' The Savior answered and said to them, 'Why do I not love you like her? When a blind man and one who sees are both together in darkness, they are no different from one another. When the light comes, then he who sees will see the light, and he who is blind will remain in darkness.'"*

The word translated "companion" is the Coptic loan-word *koinonos* (from Greek), which can mean companion, partner, or consort. The manuscript is damaged precisely at the most tantalizing points -- the word after "kiss her often on her" has a lacuna, and "mouth" is a scholarly reconstruction. Jesus's response reframes the question entirely: the issue is not romantic favoritism but spiritual perception. Mary sees; the others are still blind.

### Resurrection as Present Experience

The text contains a striking reversal of mainstream Christian eschatology: "Those who say they will die first and then rise are in error. If they do not first receive the resurrection while they live, when they die they will receive nothing." Resurrection is not a future event but a present spiritual transformation available to those with gnosis.

### The Separation and Reunification of Adam and Eve

The text returns repeatedly to the Genesis narrative, reading it as a story of catastrophic division: "When Eve was still with Adam, death did not exist. When she was separated from him, death came into being." Christ's mission is understood as the **repair of this primal separation** -- reuniting the divided halves of humanity in the sacred space of the bridal chamber.

## Key Themes

### The Bridal Chamber Sacrament

The bridal chamber (*nymphon*) is the supreme mystery of the Gospel of Philip -- the sacrament that completes and transcends all others. It represents the reunification of the human soul with its angelic counterpart, the restoration of an original spiritual wholeness that was shattered when the world came into being. The text describes it as the "Holy of the Holies," the innermost sanctum. Those who enter it "will kindle the light" and "will no longer be separated." Whether this sacrament involved an actual ritual practice or was purely symbolic remains one of the great debates in Nag Hammadi scholarship.

### Names and Deception

The text develops a sophisticated **philosophy of language**, arguing that worldly names are instruments of bondage wielded by the archons (cosmic powers). The name "God" does not point to God; "resurrection" does not point to resurrection. Only gnosis -- direct experiential knowledge -- can break through the prison of language to reach the unnamed realities beyond. This theme anticipates modern philosophical concerns about the relationship between language, power, and reality.

### Gnosis over Sin

"Ignorance is the mother of all evil." The Gospel of Philip redefines the human predicament: the fundamental problem is not moral failure but spiritual blindness. "He who has knowledge of the truth is a free man." Yet the text balances this with a striking teaching on love: "He who is really free, through knowledge, is a slave, because of love for those who have not yet been able to attain to the freedom of knowledge."

### Resurrection in the Present

Against the mainstream Christian expectation of a future bodily resurrection, the Gospel of Philip insists that resurrection must be experienced *now*, in this life, through sacramental transformation and gnosis. "If they do not first receive the resurrection while they live, when they die they will receive nothing."

### Male-Female Reunification

Death entered the world through the separation of Eve from Adam. Christ came "to repair the separation, which was from the beginning, and again unite the two." The bridal chamber is the locus of this reunification -- not merely the joining of husband and wife, but the restoration of humanity's original androgynous wholeness. The "undefiled marriage" described in the text is "not fleshly, but pure. It belongs not to desire, but to the will."

### Truth and Image

"Truth did not come into the world naked, but it came in types and images." The Gospel of Philip develops an elaborate theology of symbolic mediation -- truth cannot be grasped directly in this world but only through images, sacraments, and ritual enactments that point beyond themselves. Yet the goal is always to pass through the image to the reality it represents.

## Influence & Legacy

### The Dan Brown Effect

The Gospel of Philip languished in relative scholarly obscurity until Dan Brown's 2003 novel *The Da Vinci Code* catapulted it into global awareness. Brown's character Leigh Teabing quotes the Mary Magdalene passage to argue that Jesus and Mary were married, that their union produced a royal bloodline, and that the Church suppressed this truth for centuries. The novel sold over 80 million copies and sparked a worldwide debate about early Christianity, the formation of the canon, and the role of women in the early church.

Scholars of Gnosticism were quick to note that Brown's interpretation stripped the passage of its theological context. The Gospel of Philip is not interested in biological marriage or literal descendants. The "companion" language and the kissing passage operate within a Valentinian framework of spiritual communion and the transmission of gnosis. Jesus's answer to the jealous disciples -- the parable of the blind man and the sighted man -- redirects attention from physical intimacy to spiritual perception.

Nevertheless, the controversy had a lasting positive effect: it drew millions of readers to the Nag Hammadi texts for the first time and stimulated public interest in the diversity of early Christianity.

### Gnostic Studies

The Gospel of Philip is a cornerstone text for the academic study of Valentinian Christianity. Its detailed references to sacramental practice provide the most extensive surviving window into how Valentinian communities may have actually worshipped. Scholars including Wesley W. Isenberg, Hans-Martin Schenke, Bentley Layton, Marvin Meyer, and April DeConick have produced important translations and studies. The text remains central to ongoing debates about the relationship between "Gnostic" and "orthodox" Christianity, and whether these categories are even meaningful.

### Feminist Theology and the Recovery of Mary Magdalene

The Gospel of Philip, along with the *Gospel of Mary* and the *Gospel of Thomas*, has been important to feminist theologians and historians seeking to recover the suppressed roles of women in early Christianity. The text's portrayal of Mary Magdalene as Jesus's closest spiritual companion -- the one who "sees" when others are blind -- challenges the later Western tradition that reduced her to a repentant prostitute (a conflation with no basis in the canonical gospels, formalized by Pope Gregory I in 591 CE and not officially corrected by the Catholic Church until 1969). Scholars such as Karen King, Elaine Pagels, and Ann Graham Brock have drawn on these texts to argue that women held leadership roles in some early Christian communities, roles that were systematically erased as institutional orthodoxy consolidated its power.
