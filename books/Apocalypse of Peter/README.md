# The Apocalypse of Peter

**The earliest Christian text to describe the afterlife in vivid, terrifying detail -- a guided tour of heaven and hell that shaped Western imagination for centuries.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Apocalypse of Peter was composed in Greek during the first half of the second century CE, likely between 100 and 150 CE. Clement of Alexandria (c. 200 CE) treated it as holy scripture, confirming that it was already well established by that time. The original text is lost, but it survives through several manuscripts and fragments:

### The Akhmim Fragment (Greek)
In 1886-87, a fragmentary Greek manuscript was discovered during excavations led by Gaston Maspero in a desert necropolis at Akhmim in Upper Egypt. Found in the same codex as a fragment of the Gospel of Peter, the manuscript itself dates to the late sixth century (based on paleographic analysis by Guglielmo Cavallo and Herwig Maehler). This was the first major recovery of the text after centuries of being known only through quotations in early Church Fathers.

### The Ethiopic Text
In 1910, the English scholar M. R. James recognized that an Ethiopic version embedded within a collection known as the "Books of Clement" -- a writing purporting to contain revelations dictated by Peter to Clement -- was in fact the Apocalypse of Peter. This Ethiopic manuscript (the d'Abbadie manuscript, estimated 15th-16th century) preserves a fuller version of the text. A second independent Ethiopic manuscript was discovered in 1968 on the island of Kebran in Lake Tana, Ethiopia.

### The Rainer and Bodleian Fragments (Greek)
Two additional short Greek fragments exist: a 5th-century leaf held in the Bodleian Library at Oxford (discovered in 1895), and the Rainer fragment held in the Austrian National Library in Vienna (discovered in the 1880s). Though brief, scholars consider these fragments the most reliable guide to the original Greek wording.

### Which Version is More Original?
Most scholars today believe the Ethiopic text is closer to the original composition, while the Akhmim Greek fragment represents a later, edited version. The evidence for this includes: the Ethiopic text matches the line count given in the ancient Stichometry of Nicephorus (270-300 lines); patristic quotations align more closely with the Ethiopic; the Ethiopic agrees better with the Rainer and Bodleian fragments; and the Akhmim version appears to have been revised to integrate with the Gospel of Peter, which was found in the same manuscript.

One key structural difference: in the Ethiopic version, Peter sees hell first and then heaven, while the Akhmim fragment reverses this order. In the Ethiopic, the vision is granted by the risen Christ after the resurrection; in the Akhmim, it occurs during Jesus's earthly ministry.

## Historical Context

The Apocalypse of Peter emerged during a formative period in early Christian thought, when believers were grappling with fundamental questions: What happens after death? How will the righteous be rewarded? How will the wicked be punished? The canonical New Testament offers only sparse and often metaphorical answers. This text stepped into that gap with startling specificity.

The work belongs to the genre of apocalyptic literature -- revelatory visions typically granted to a biblical figure -- and specifically to the tradition of the *katabasis* or *nekyia*, the descent into the underworld familiar from Greek and Roman literature (Homer's *Odyssey*, Virgil's *Aeneid*). The Apocalypse of Peter Christianized this tradition, becoming the first known Christian text to offer a detailed, systematic tour of the afterlife.

Its influence was immediate and widespread. The text is paraphrased extensively in Book 2 of the Sibylline Oracles (c. 150 CE). It shaped the Acts of Thomas, the Martyrdom of Perpetua, the so-called Second Epistle of Clement, and possibly the Shepherd of Hermas. Clement of Alexandria quoted it by name in his *Prophetical Extracts*.

The Muratorian Fragment -- one of the earliest surviving lists of authoritative Christian texts, dating to the late second century -- includes the Apocalypse of Peter alongside the Revelation of John. But even there, a note of hesitation appears: "We receive only the apocalypses of John and Peter, though some of us are not willing that the latter be read in the church." This ambivalence would prove decisive.

## Canonical Status

The Apocalypse of Peter came remarkably close to being part of the New Testament. Along with the Shepherd of Hermas, it is one of the texts that scholars identify as having the strongest claim to near-canonical status in the early centuries of Christianity.

Several factors worked in its favor: it bore the name of Peter, the chief apostle; it was quoted as scripture by Clement of Alexandria; it appeared in the Muratorian Fragment's canon list; and its vivid moral teachings had obvious pastoral value.

So why was it ultimately excluded? Scholars point to several converging reasons:

- **Pseudonymity became suspect.** As the church developed stricter criteria for canonicity, the growing recognition that Peter did not actually write the text weakened its authority.
- **Theological discomfort.** The Rainer fragment contains a remarkable passage (chapter 14) suggesting that the righteous in heaven could pray for the damned, who would then receive "a fine baptism in salvation from the Acherousian lake." This implies that hell could eventually be emptied -- a position close to the doctrine of *apokatastasis* (universal restoration), which was associated with Origen and would later be condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council of Constantinople in 553 CE. M. R. James argued that the original text may well have endorsed universal salvation after a period of purifying suffering.
- **Competition from the Apocalypse of John.** As the Book of Revelation gained acceptance (itself controversial for centuries), there was diminishing appetite for a second apocalypse in the canon.
- **Declining use.** By the fourth and fifth centuries, the text had fallen out of regular liturgical use in most churches, and practical usage was a significant factor in canonical decisions.

## Summary

The text takes the form of a revelation granted by Jesus Christ to the apostle Peter, in which Peter is shown the fates awaiting the dead.

### The Vision of Hell
Jesus leads Peter to witness the place of punishment, where sinners suffer torments specifically designed to match their sins -- a principle scholars call *contrapasso* (the same term later used for Dante's system of divine justice). The angel Uriel resurrects the dead into new bodies so they can experience physical torment. Among the punishments:

- **Blasphemers** are hanged by their tongues over flames.
- **Those who denied justice** are cast into a pit of fire.
- **Adulterous women** hang by their hair over a lake of bubbling mire, while the men who sinned with them hang by their feet beside them.
- **Murderers** are placed in a narrow space filled with venomous creatures and gnawing worms.
- **Women who procured abortions** sit in a pit of excrement up to their throats, while the spirits of their unborn children shoot sparks of fire into their eyes.
- **Those who falsely claimed righteousness** are struck blind and dumb and placed on burning coals.
- **Persecutors of the righteous** have their entrails eaten by worms.
- **Sorcerers and sorceresses** are bound to spinning wheels of fire.
- **Usurers** stand in a lake of foul matter up to their knees.
- **Those who dishonored their parents** are hung over a pit and tormented by beasts.

The catalog continues at length, each punishment a dark mirror of the sin it answers.

### The Vision of Heaven
Peter then beholds a vast, luminous country beyond this world, bathed in light brighter than daylight. The earth blooms with imperishable flowers, and the fragrance reaches even to the place where Peter stands. The righteous dwell there in radiant bodies "whiter than any snow and ruddier than any rose," with long, curling hair interwoven with flowers resembling a rainbow. They are clothed in garments like those of angels and sing praises to God with one voice. Moses and Elijah appear among them. It is a paradise of beauty, peace, and unending worship.

### The Possibility of Mercy
In the Rainer fragment, a striking passage suggests that the righteous may intercede for the damned, praying to God for mercy on their behalf. The sinners would then be baptized and saved from the Acherousian lake. This tantalizing hint of universal restoration -- that even hell might not be permanent -- sets the Apocalypse of Peter apart from later, more rigid accounts of eternal damnation.

## Key Themes

### Afterlife Geography
The Apocalypse of Peter is the earliest Christian text to map the afterlife as a physical landscape with distinct regions. Hell is a place of darkness, fire, mire, and narrow spaces; heaven is a luminous garden of supernatural beauty. This spatial conception of the afterlife -- hell below, heaven above, each with its own terrain -- became foundational for Christian eschatology.

### Moral Retribution and Contrapasso
The governing principle is that punishment fits the crime. Blasphemers suffer through their tongues; the lustful through the body parts involved in their sin; the unjust through their eyes, which refused to see righteousness. This system of poetic justice makes the text not merely a vision but a moral argument: the body that sins is the body that suffers.

### The Fate of the Righteous
Heaven in this text is not merely the absence of suffering but a place of active, radiant joy. The righteous are physically transformed -- their bodies glow, their hair blooms with flowers. They exist in perpetual worship, surrounded by angels. The text insists that goodness is not merely rewarded but transfigured.

### The Tension Between Justice and Mercy
Perhaps the most theologically interesting feature of the text is the tension between its elaborate system of punishments and its hint (in the Rainer fragment) that these punishments may not be permanent. If the righteous can pray sinners out of hell, then the purpose of punishment is corrective rather than retributive -- a position closer to Clement of Alexandria's view that "God does not punish, but corrects." This tension may ultimately have cost the text its place in the canon.

### The Authority of Apostolic Witness
By placing these visions in Peter's mouth, the text claims the highest possible authority for its eschatological teachings. Peter is not merely a theologian speculating about the afterlife; he is an eyewitness, shown these things directly by the risen Christ.

## Influence & Legacy

### The Apocalypse of Paul and Its Descendants
The most direct literary descendant is the Apocalypse of Paul (Visio Pauli), composed in the late fourth century, which expands and elaborates the tour-of-hell genre that Peter's Apocalypse inaugurated. Jan Bremmer called the Apocalypse of Paul "the most important step in the direction that would find its apogee in Dante." But the step before that was Peter's vision.

### Dante's Divine Comedy
The structural and thematic parallels between the Apocalypse of Peter and Dante's *Inferno* (1314) are striking: a guided tour of the afterlife, punishments designed to fit specific sins, a geography of moral meaning. Dante did not read the Apocalypse of Peter directly (it was lost to Western readers by his time), but he inherited its traditions through intermediary texts -- the Apocalypse of Paul, the Vision of Tungdal, the Vision of Adamnan, and other medieval visionary works that carried the Petrine tradition forward. The *contrapasso* principle that structures the *Inferno* has its earliest Christian expression here.

### Medieval Visions of Hell
Throughout the Middle Ages, the tradition launched by the Apocalypse of Peter proliferated in visionary literature across languages and cultures. In the ninth century, the Greek "Apocalypse of the Virgin" drew on memories of both the Apocalypses of Peter and Paul for another circuit of hell. The Vision of Tungdal (1149), the Vision of Adamnan, and numerous other medieval texts continued to elaborate the afterlife geography that Peter's Apocalypse first mapped.

### Islamic Eschatology
Scholars have noted parallels between the Christian visionary tradition originating in the Apocalypse of Peter and Islamic accounts of the afterlife, particularly the *Mi'raj* (the Prophet Muhammad's Night Journey through heaven and hell). The shared features -- guided tours of the afterlife, encounters with angels and prophets, vivid depictions of both paradise and torment -- have been the subject of extensive scholarly debate. Whether these represent direct literary influence, shared Near Eastern eschatological traditions, or independent development remains an open question, but the structural similarities are unmistakable.

### The Concept of Hell Itself
Perhaps the most far-reaching legacy of the Apocalypse of Peter is its role in shaping the very concept of hell in the Western and Christian imagination. The canonical scriptures offer relatively little detail about the nature of post-mortem punishment. It was this text -- and its many descendants -- that filled in the picture: the fire, the darkness, the demons, the specific torments for specific sins. When people in the Western world imagine hell, they are imagining, whether they know it or not, a tradition that begins here.
