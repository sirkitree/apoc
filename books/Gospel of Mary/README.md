# The Gospel of Mary

**A fragmentary early Christian text that places Mary Magdalene at the center of Jesus' teaching circle -- and ignited one of the earliest recorded debates about women's spiritual authority.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Gospel of Mary survives in three fragmentary manuscripts, all discovered in Egypt:

1. **Papyrus Berolinensis 8502 (BG 8502,1)** -- A fifth-century Coptic translation and the most complete surviving copy, though even it is missing more than half the text. The codex was purchased in Cairo in 1896 by German diplomat Dr. Carl Reinhardt from an antiquities dealer in Achmim. It was brought to Berlin, but a series of obstacles -- including two world wars -- delayed its publication until Walter C. Till's 1955 German edition. An English translation by George W. MacRae and R. McL. Wilson followed, included in James M. Robinson's *The Nag Hammadi Library in English* (1978). The codex also contains the *Apocryphon of John* and *The Sophia of Jesus Christ*, both typically classified as Gnostic texts.

2. **Papyrus Rylands 463 (P.Ryl. 463)** -- A small Greek fragment dating to the third century, published in 1938. It coincides with BG 8502 at 17:5-19:5.

3. **Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 3525 (P.Oxy. 3525)** -- Another third-century Greek fragment, published in 1983. It coincides with 9:5-10:13.

The existence of three independent copies -- in two languages, spread across several centuries -- is remarkable for a text of this era. It demonstrates that the Gospel of Mary was widely copied and circulated in the ancient world. Scholars broadly agree that the original was probably composed in **Egypt or Syria in the first half of the second century CE**, making it roughly contemporary with some of the latest books of the New Testament. Karen L. King dates it to the early second century; Christopher Tuckett concurs with the first half of the second century.

### What Is Missing

Of the original Coptic manuscript's nineteen pages, ten are lost: **pages 1 through 6** (the entire opening, likely containing conversations between Jesus and his disciples) and **pages 11 through 14** (the heart of Mary's private vision of Jesus and his teachings to her). Less than half of the text survives. The two Greek fragments, while confirming the text's early circulation, do not fill in the gaps.

## Historical Context

The Gospel of Mary emerged from a period of intense diversity and debate within early Christianity. In the first and second centuries, there was no single, unified "church" -- instead, scattered communities across the Mediterranean held widely varying beliefs about Jesus' nature, the path to salvation, and who had the authority to teach.

### Gnostic Communities

The Gospel of Mary is typically associated with **Gnostic Christianity**, a loose family of movements that emphasized inner spiritual knowledge (*gnosis*) over faith, institutional authority, or ritual. Gnostic communities often valued direct revelation and personal visionary experience. In this text, salvation comes not through external rites or hierarchies but through turning inward: "the Son of Humanity exists within you," the Savior says. God is called simply "the Good," without gender. There is no hell, no original sin -- only ignorance and the possibility of inner transformation.

### Women's Authority in the Early Church

The Gospel of Mary directly addresses one of the most contentious issues of the period: whether women could serve as teachers, apostles, and recipients of divine revelation. Mary is presented as a figure who received private teachings from the risen Christ and who rallied the fearful male disciples when they faltered. This portrait stands in sharp tension with texts like 1 Timothy 2:12 ("I do not permit a woman to teach") and Tertullian's *On Baptism* (chapter 17), which explicitly prohibit women from teaching or baptizing. The confrontation between Mary and Peter depicted in this gospel -- also echoed in *The Gospel of Thomas*, *Pistis Sophia*, and *The Gospel of the Egyptians* -- reflects real second-century struggles over the role of women in Christian communities.

Early Church Father Irenaeus (ca. 140-203 CE) noted with alarm that women were especially drawn to Gnostic groups; in his *Against Heresies*, he complained that a Gnostic teacher named Marcus had attracted "many foolish women" from his own congregation. Tertullian (ca. 160-225 CE) mocked "those Magdalenians who dream of spiritual marriages." These hostile reactions suggest that communities which honored Mary Magdalene as a spiritual authority were visible enough to provoke sustained opposition from the emerging orthodox establishment.

## Canonical Status

The Gospel of Mary was never included in the New Testament canon. Several factors contributed to its exclusion:

- **Theological incompatibility.** The text's teachings -- that sin does not truly exist, that salvation is found within, that matter will dissolve back into its roots -- diverged sharply from the doctrines that became orthodox Christianity. The emphasis on inner gnosis over institutional authority was threatening to bishops seeking to consolidate church structure.

- **Women's leadership.** The gospel's central claim -- that Mary Magdalene received private revelation from Christ and was qualified to teach the male apostles -- directly contradicted the patriarchal hierarchy that was solidifying in the second through fourth centuries. As Karen King has argued, the early image of Mary as a trusted apostle "proved to be a major obstacle to establishing male dominance," and that image had to be recast or suppressed.

- **Esoteric revelation.** Peter and Andrew's objections within the text itself -- that Mary received teachings "privately" and "without our knowledge" -- mirror the orthodox position that authentic revelation must be public, apostolic, and verifiable through institutional succession. Secret or private visions were regarded with deep suspicion.

- **The canonization process.** By the fourth century, when church councils and influential bishops (Athanasius, Eusebius) were defining which texts were authoritative, the Gospel of Mary and texts like it had already been marginalized by centuries of polemical writing. The text was excluded, its manuscripts ceased to be copied, and it vanished from the historical record until its rediscovery in 1896.

The early Church Fathers had acknowledged Mary Magdalene as the "Apostle to the Apostles" -- the first witness to the resurrection. This title survived in Eastern Christianity, but in the Western tradition, Pope Gregory I conflated her in 591 CE with the unnamed "sinful woman" of Luke 7, redefining her for over a thousand years as a repentant prostitute rather than a spiritual leader.

## Summary

### What Survives

The surviving text falls into four main sections:

**1. The Savior's Teaching (pages 7-9)**
The text picks up mid-conversation. The Savior (Jesus) teaches his disciples about the nature of matter and sin. His teaching is radical: "Sin doesn't exist, but you're the ones who make sin when you act in accordance with the nature of adultery, which is called 'sin.'" Matter will dissolve back into its own roots. Sickness and death arise from attachment to illusion. He instructs the disciples to preach the gospel of the kingdom, but warns them not to lay down rules or laws beyond what he has given, "lest you be bound by it." Then he departs.

**2. Mary Rallies the Disciples (pages 9-10)**
The disciples are devastated and afraid. If the authorities did not spare the Savior, how will they spare his followers? Mary stands up, comforts them, and "turned their hearts toward the Good." Peter acknowledges that the Savior loved Mary "more than all other women" and asks her to share teachings they have not heard.

**3. Mary's Vision (pages 10-17)**
Mary describes a vision in which the Savior appeared to her. He praises her for not wavering and teaches that it is the *mind* -- not the soul or spirit, but something between the two -- that perceives visions. Here the text breaks off (pages 11-14 are lost). When it resumes, the soul is ascending through hostile powers. It confronts **Desire**, which claims ownership over it. The soul replies: "I saw you, but you didn't see me or know me." It overcomes **Ignorance**, then faces a fourth power that takes **seven forms**: Darkness, Desire, Ignorance, Zeal for Death, the Kingdom of the Flesh, the Foolish "Wisdom" of Flesh, and the "Wisdom" of Anger -- collectively called **the seven powers of Wrath**. The powers challenge the soul: "Where do you come from, you murderer, and where are you going, conqueror of space?" The soul answers: "What binds me has been killed, what surrounds me has been overcome, my desire is gone, and ignorance has died." It then attains rest and silence.

**4. The Conflict Over Authority (pages 17-19)**
Andrew and Peter challenge Mary. Andrew says: "These teachings seem like different ideas." Peter is more blunt: "He didn't speak with a woman without our knowledge and not publicly with us, did he? Did he prefer her to us?" Mary weeps. Then **Levi** intervenes, rebuking Peter: "Peter, you've always been angry. Now I see you debating with this woman like the adversaries. But if the Savior made her worthy, who are you then to reject her?" The disciples resolve to go out and preach, and the text ends.

## Key Themes

### Mary as Spiritual Leader
Mary is not merely a passive recipient of teaching. She is the one who holds the community together when the male disciples fall apart in grief and fear. Peter himself acknowledges the Savior's special love for her. She possesses knowledge the others lack, and it is her vision that provides the culminating revelation of the text.

### The Conflict with Peter
The confrontation between Mary and Peter is the dramatic heart of the gospel. Peter's objections are twofold: that she is a woman, and that she received private teachings. This mirrors real historical debates in second-century Christianity about who held authority -- and on what basis. Levi's defense of Mary ("if the Savior made her worthy, who are you then to reject her?") is a direct challenge to the idea that gender determines spiritual capacity.

### Inner Vision and the Mind
The Gospel of Mary introduces a distinctive idea: that visions are perceived not through the soul or the spirit, but through the *mind* that exists between them. This intermediary faculty -- tragically, its full explanation falls in the missing pages -- suggests a sophisticated psychology of spiritual perception. The treasure is found "where the mind is."

### The Nature of Sin
The Savior's teaching on sin is startling: sin does not inherently exist. Rather, people *create* sin by acting contrary to their own nature. Evil is not a cosmic force but a product of ignorance and misdirected desire. The path to liberation is not penance or atonement but *recognition* -- seeing through the illusions that bind the soul. This stands in stark contrast to the doctrine of original sin that would later dominate Western Christianity.

### The Soul's Ascent
The surviving vision describes the soul's journey upward past hostile powers -- a motif common in Gnostic literature. Each power attempts to claim ownership over the ascending soul, and each is overcome through knowledge and self-recognition. The seven powers of Wrath represent the forces that keep human beings trapped: darkness, desire, ignorance, death-obsession, attachment to the flesh, false wisdom, and anger. Liberation means recognizing that these powers have no ultimate claim.

## Influence & Legacy

### Scholarship
The Gospel of Mary remained virtually unknown until the mid-twentieth century. Its publication sparked intense scholarly interest, particularly after the discovery of the Nag Hammadi library in 1945 provided a broader context of early Christian diversity. **Karen L. King's** landmark study, *The Gospel of Mary of Magdala: Jesus and the First Woman Apostle* (Polebridge Press, 2003), provided the definitive scholarly treatment and argued that the text presents "the most straightforward and convincing argument in any early Christian writing for the legitimacy of women's leadership." King, the Winn Professor of Ecclesiastical History at Harvard Divinity School, has been central to the gospel's modern reception.

### Feminist Theology
The Gospel of Mary has become a foundational text for feminist theologians and historians seeking to recover women's voices in early Christianity. It provides direct ancient evidence that some Christian communities affirmed women's authority to teach, receive revelation, and lead -- countering the narrative that male leadership was universal and uncontested from the beginning.

### Popular Culture
Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* (2003) -- which sold over 80 million copies and was adapted into a major film -- brought enormous public attention to Mary Magdalene and the Gnostic gospels, though scholars have noted that Brown's sensationalized treatment (claiming Mary was Jesus' wife and bore his child) has no basis in the Gospel of Mary or any other ancient text. The actual gospel is far more interesting than the conspiracy theories: it depicts Mary not as a wife or mother, but as an intellectual and spiritual equal whose authority rested on the quality of her understanding, not on a romantic relationship.

The text has also influenced contemporary spiritual movements, interfaith dialogue, and ongoing debates within Christianity about women's ordination and leadership. The Vatican officially corrected Gregory I's conflation of Mary Magdalene with the unnamed sinner in 1969, and in 2016 Pope Francis elevated her feast day to a full liturgical celebration, recognizing her as the "Apostle to the Apostles."
