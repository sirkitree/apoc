# Additions to Esther

**The six Greek passages that transformed Esther from a secular tale of court intrigue into a story of divine deliverance.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original language:** Greek. The six additions (totaling 107 verses) were composed in Greek and have no counterpart in the Hebrew Masoretic Text. Most scholars agree that nearly all the additions were originally written in Greek, though some argue that Addition D (Esther's audience with the king) may have a Semitic original. The two royal edicts (Additions B and E) are widely recognized as Greek compositions based on their rhetorical style, which echoes Hellenistic bureaucratic prose.

**Relationship to Hebrew Esther:** The canonical Hebrew Book of Esther tells the story of how the Jewish queen of Persia and her cousin Mordecai thwarted Haman's plot to annihilate the Jewish people. The Greek translators of the Septuagint (LXX) did not simply translate this Hebrew text -- they expanded it dramatically, weaving in six substantial passages that appear at contextually appropriate points throughout the narrative. These additions roughly double the theological content of the book.

**Key manuscripts:** The Greek Esther survives in two distinct recensions:

- **The Septuagint (o') text**, preserved in major uncial manuscripts including Codex Vaticanus (B) and Codex Alexandrinus (A), though scholars note that Vaticanus may not represent the earliest ("Old Greek") form of the text in this particular book.
- **The Alpha (L) text**, sometimes called the "Lucianic" recension, preserved in a handful of minuscule manuscripts. This version differs significantly from the o' text and may reflect an independent, and possibly earlier, translation tradition.

**Date of composition:** The additions were not all composed at the same time or by the same author. Most scholars date them broadly to the 2nd or 1st century BCE, based on literary style, theology, and their strongly anti-Gentile sentiment. A crucial piece of evidence is the colophon (Addition F / 11:1), which states:

> "In the fourth year of the reign of Ptolemy and Cleopatra, Dositheus, who said that he was a priest and a Levite, and his son Ptolemy brought to Egypt the preceding Letter about Purim, which they said was authentic and had been translated by Lysimachus son of Ptolemy, one of the residents of Jerusalem."

The identity of "Ptolemy and Cleopatra" is debated. The two leading candidates are Ptolemy VIII Soter II (placing the translation around 114-113 BCE) and Ptolemy XII Auletes (placing it around 78-77 BCE). The latter date, championed by the scholar Elias Bickerman, is widely accepted.

**Place of composition:** The additions were likely composed in a Jewish diaspora community, probably in Alexandria, Egypt, or possibly Jerusalem. The colophon describes the finished Greek text being brought *to* Egypt, suggesting the translation work may have been done in Jerusalem by Lysimachus.

## Historical Context

The Hebrew Book of Esther posed a problem for ancient readers -- and not just any problem. It is the only book in the Hebrew Bible that never once mentions God. There are no prayers, no references to the Temple, no appeals to the covenant, no dietary laws, no acknowledgment of divine providence. The deliverance of the Jewish people appears to unfold through nothing more than clever politics, personal bravery, and fortunate timing.

For Jewish communities living in the Greek-speaking diaspora, surrounded by Hellenistic culture and needing to articulate their faith in terms that resonated with both their own tradition and the wider intellectual world, this was a significant gap. How could a book about the salvation of Israel not mention the God of Israel?

The Additions to Esther are, at their core, an answer to that question. By inserting prayers, divine visions, explicit references to God's intervention, and official decrees that frame the conflict in theological terms, the Greek translators transformed Esther from a book that might be read as a purely human drama into one that explicitly credits God with every turn of the plot. Where the Hebrew text trusts the reader to sense divine providence behind the scenes, the Greek additions make that providence impossible to miss.

The additions also served a secondary purpose: they enhanced the book's literary and historical credibility by providing the "full text" of the royal edicts mentioned but not quoted in the Hebrew. Ancient readers valued documentary evidence, and the inclusion of these elaborate decrees lent the narrative an air of archival authenticity.

## Canonical Status

The canonical status of the Additions to Esther has been a point of division among religious traditions for centuries:

- **Roman Catholic Church:** The Council of Trent (1546) definitively affirmed the entire Greek Esther, additions included, as deuterocanonical scripture. Catholic Bibles (such as the New American Bible and the Jerusalem Bible) integrate the additions into the body of the text, though they are often printed in italics or marked to distinguish them from the Hebrew portions.

- **Eastern Orthodox Churches:** The Greek and Russian Orthodox churches accept the expanded Septuagint version of Esther as canonical. Since the Orthodox Old Testament is based on the Septuagint, the additions are a natural part of their scriptural text.

- **Protestant Churches:** Following the lead of Martin Luther and the Reformers, who restricted the Old Testament canon to books found in the Hebrew Bible, Protestant traditions classify the Additions to Esther as apocryphal. They appear in some Protestant Bibles (such as editions of the King James Version that include the Apocrypha) but are not considered authoritative for doctrine.

- **Judaism:** The additions have no place in the Jewish canon. The Hebrew Megillat Esther, read during the festival of Purim, is the only recognized form of the text. The Greek additions are seen as later Hellenistic expansions with no basis in the Hebrew tradition.

**A note on numbering:** The chapter-and-verse numbering of the additions is notoriously confusing. When Jerome translated the Bible into Latin (the Vulgate) in the 4th century, he recognized that these passages had no Hebrew equivalent. He removed them from their positions within the narrative and gathered them into an appendix at the end of the book, marking them with an obelus. When Stephen Langton added chapter numbers in the 13th century, he numbered the appendix as chapters 10-16 -- creating a system where the additions are out of narrative order and bear chapter numbers that do not correspond to their place in the story. Modern scholars use the letter designations A through F to avoid this confusion.

## Summary of the Six Additions

### Addition A -- Mordecai's Dream (11:2-12:6)

The Greek Esther opens not with the banquet of Ahasuerus, as the Hebrew does, but with a prophetic dream. In the second year of the reign of King Artaxerxes (the Greek name used for Ahasuerus), Mordecai -- described as a Jew living in the city of Susa, a member of the tribe of Benjamin -- has a terrifying vision:

Two great dragons come forward, ready for battle, roaring terribly. At their roar, every nation prepares for war against "the nation of the righteous." It is a day of darkness and gloom, of tribulation and distress. The whole righteous nation is troubled, fearing their own destruction, and they cry out to God. From their cry, a tiny spring grows into a great river, overflowing with water. Light and sun appear, and the lowly are exalted and devour the powerful.

This dream, which will not be interpreted until Addition F at the end of the book, frames the entire narrative as the unfolding of a divine plan -- something the Hebrew text never does. The passage also recounts how Mordecai discovers a plot by two eunuchs (Gabatha and Tharra) to assassinate the king, and is rewarded for exposing them. This episode links to the canonical narrative's subplot about Mordecai's unrewarded loyalty.

### Addition B -- The First Royal Edict (13:1-7)

After Haman persuades the king to authorize the destruction of the Jews (Esther 3:13 in the Hebrew), the Greek text provides the full text of the decree. Written in the elaborate style of Hellenistic royal correspondence, the edict is addressed from "the great king Artaxerxes" to the governors of the 127 provinces. It accuses the Jews of being a hostile people whose laws are "contrary to those of every nation," who "continually disregard the ordinances of kings," and who threaten the stability of the empire. It orders that all Jews -- men, women, and children -- be destroyed on the fourteenth day of Adar, "so that those who have long been hostile and remain so may in a single day go down in violence to Hades, and leave our government completely secure and untroubled hereafter."

The edict is chilling in its bureaucratic precision and its language of dehumanization. For modern readers, it carries uncomfortable echoes of later historical documents.

### Addition C -- The Prayers of Mordecai and Esther (13:8-14:19)

This is the theological heart of the additions. After learning of the edict, Mordecai and Esther each offer extended prayers to God -- prayers that are completely absent from the Hebrew text.

**Mordecai's prayer** (13:8-18) is a passionate appeal to the sovereign Lord of all creation. He insists that his refusal to bow to Haman was not motivated by pride but by his conviction that worship belongs to God alone: "I would have been willing to kiss the soles of his feet to save Israel! But I did this so that I might not set human glory above the glory of God." He begs God to remember his covenant with Israel and not to abandon his chosen people.

**Esther's prayer** (14:1-19) is longer, more anguished, and more personally revealing. She strips off her royal garments, covers herself with ashes and dung, and tears out her hair. She confesses her loneliness and isolation in the Persian court. In a remarkable passage, she declares that she "abhors the bed of the uncircumcised" and the "sign of her high estate" (the royal crown), which she regards as a "menstruous rag." She insists she has never eaten at Haman's table, nor drunk the wine of pagan libations, nor found any joy in her position since the day she was brought to the palace. She begs God to give her courage and to "put eloquent speech in my mouth before the lion" (the king).

These prayers accomplish something extraordinary: they make explicit the inner spiritual lives of characters who, in the Hebrew text, are defined almost entirely by their actions.

### Addition D -- Esther Before the King (15:1-16)

In the Hebrew text, Esther's approach to the king's throne room is described in just three verses (5:1-3). The Greek expands this into a dramatic scene of sixteen verses that became one of the most frequently depicted episodes in Western art.

On the third day after her prayer, Esther removes her mourning garments and arrays herself in "splendid attire." She takes two maids with her -- leaning on one for support, while the other carries her train. She is "radiant with perfect beauty" and looks happy, "as if beloved," but her heart is "frozen with fear."

When the king sees her, he lifts his face "flushed with splendor" and looks at her "in fierce anger." Esther falters, turns pale, and faints, collapsing on the maid who walks before her. Then -- in the key theological moment -- "God changed the spirit of the king to gentleness." The king springs from his throne, takes Esther in his arms, and comforts her with soothing words. She faints a second time, and the king is distressed until she revives and speaks.

The explicit statement that God intervened to change the king's disposition is the addition's most direct assertion of divine control over events. Where the Hebrew text leaves the reader to wonder what moved the king's heart, the Greek text declares it outright.

### Addition E -- The Second Royal Edict (16:1-24)

Balancing Addition B, this passage provides the full text of Artaxerxes' counter-edict after Haman's downfall. It is the longest of the six additions (24 verses) and one of the most rhetorically polished.

The king begins by denouncing Haman as a Macedonian (not a Persian!) who conspired to transfer the kingdom to the Macedonians -- a remarkable detail that may reflect tensions between Jews and Greeks in the Hellenistic period. Haman is accused of having plotted not only against the Jews but against the king himself. The edict praises Mordecai and Esther, declares the Jews to be "children of the living God, most high, most mighty," and orders that the previous decree be nullified. The Jews are to be permitted to defend themselves and to "celebrate with all good cheer" their deliverance. Any city or province that fails to comply will be "destroyed in wrath with spear and fire" and left "not only untrodden by men but also most hateful to wild animals and birds for all time."

The edict's language makes explicit what the Hebrew text implies: the conflict between Haman and Mordecai is a cosmic struggle, and the deliverance of the Jews is the work of "the living God."

### Addition F -- Interpretation of the Dream and Colophon (10:4-11:1)

The book ends where it began -- with Mordecai's dream. Now, after the events of the story have unfolded, Mordecai provides the interpretation:

- The **two dragons** are Mordecai and Haman.
- The **tiny spring that became a great river** is Esther, whom the king married and made queen.
- The **two lots** are two destinies -- one for the people of God, one for the nations. These are the "lots" (purim) that give the festival its name.
- God remembered his people and vindicated his inheritance.

The interpretation turns the Purim festival itself into a theological statement: the "lots" are not the random casting of dice by Haman (as in the Hebrew), but the two fates that God set before the nations at the moment of judgment. Providence, not chance, governs history.

The colophon that follows provides the historical note about Dositheus, Lysimachus, and the translation being brought to Egypt -- the passage that scholars use to date the Greek text.

## Key Themes

### The Naming of God

The most consequential change the additions make is also the most obvious: they name God. The Hebrew Book of Esther is famous for its silence about the divine -- a silence so complete that some ancient rabbis questioned whether it belonged in the canon at all. The Greek additions mention God over fifty times. They transform a narrative that could be read as a story of human resourcefulness into one that is unmistakably about divine salvation.

### Providence vs. Chance

In the Hebrew text, the name "Purim" comes from the "pur" (lot) that Haman cast to choose the date of the massacre -- an act of random chance. The additions reinterpret this: the "lots" become God's two destinies for humanity, one for the righteous and one for the wicked, decided "before God among all nations." What looked like chance was always providence.

### Prayer and Piety

The additions insist that the characters are not merely clever political operators but devout Jews. Mordecai refuses to bow to Haman not out of stubborn pride but out of theological conviction. Esther is not merely a beautiful queen deployed as a strategic asset but a woman of deep faith who loathes her pagan surroundings, keeps kosher, and turns to God in desperate prayer before risking her life. The additions give both characters rich interior spiritual lives that the spare Hebrew narrative does not provide.

### Jewish Identity in the Diaspora

Esther's prayer is a window into the anxieties of diaspora Judaism. She lives in a foreign court, married to a Gentile king, eating food she finds suspect, wearing a crown she despises, isolated from her people. Her prayer is the prayer of every Jew forced to navigate between assimilation and faithfulness. The additions take a story that could be read as celebrating successful assimilation and reframe it as a story of anguished, hidden fidelity.

### Anti-Gentile Sentiment

The additions carry a sharply antagonistic edge toward non-Jews that goes beyond the Hebrew text. The royal edicts frame the conflict in terms of civilizational struggle. Esther's prayer expresses physical revulsion at her Gentile marriage. Mordecai's prayer frames the crisis as an attack by "the nations" against God's people. These passages likely reflect the real tensions of Jewish communities living under Hellenistic or early Roman rule.

### Apocalyptic Framing

Mordecai's dream, with its cosmic dragons and rivers of light, draws on the vocabulary of apocalyptic literature. By bookending the Esther story with a prophetic dream and its interpretation, the additions place a court intrigue within a cosmic framework. The events in Susa are not merely political -- they are part of God's eschatological plan.

## Influence & Legacy

### In Art

Addition D -- Esther's dramatic, fainting approach to the king's throne -- became one of the most beloved subjects of European painting from the Renaissance through the Baroque era. Major works include:

- **Tintoretto**, *Esther before Ahasuerus* (c. 1546-47), which captures the moment of Esther's collapse with Venetian dynamism.
- **Artemisia Gentileschi**, *Esther before Ahasuerus* (c. 1630s), now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, which brings Caravaggist intensity to the scene, dressing the characters in contemporary costume.
- **Guercino**, **Sebastiano Ricci**, and many other painters returned to this scene repeatedly. Notably, it is the Greek addition's version -- with the fainting, the maids, and the king leaping from his throne -- that painters depicted, not the restrained three-verse account in the Hebrew.

### In Theology

The additions fundamentally shaped how Catholic and Orthodox Christians read the Book of Esther for over a millennium. Until the modern era of critical scholarship, most Christians encountered Esther only in its expanded Greek form, and their understanding of the book's theology was profoundly influenced by the additions' explicit God-language, prayers, and providential framing.

The additions also contributed to wider theological discussions about divine providence, the problem of evil, and the relationship between hidden and revealed divine action. They represent an early example of what might be called "theological reception" -- the reworking of a sacred text to make its implicit theology explicit.

### In Liturgy and Literature

Esther's prayer (Addition C) and the account of her approach to the king (Addition D) have been used in various Christian liturgical traditions, particularly in Marian devotion, where Esther's intercession before the king is read as a type of Mary's intercession before God.

The additions also influenced later Jewish and Christian retellings of the Esther story, including midrashic expansions and medieval dramatic adaptations.

### In Translation History

Jerome's decision to separate the additions from the body of the text and append them at the end of Esther in the Vulgate created a textual puzzle that persisted for over a thousand years. The resulting chapter numbering system (10-16) remains a source of confusion. Modern Catholic translations have largely restored the additions to their original positions within the narrative, but the legacy of Jerome's arrangement endures in older translations and reference works.
