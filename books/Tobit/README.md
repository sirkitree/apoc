# The Book of Tobit

A deuterocanonical novella of exile, faith, and divine providence -- the story of a blind father, a demon-plagued bride, and the angel who secretly guides their deliverance.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

### Original Language

Scholarly consensus holds that Tobit was originally composed in **Aramaic**, with the possibility that parts circulated in Hebrew as well. This was confirmed in the mid-twentieth century by the discovery of Semitic fragments at Qumran. Before those discoveries, the book was known only through Greek, Latin, and other secondary translations, and the question of its original language had long been debated.

### Date and Place of Composition

The book is generally dated to the **3rd or early 2nd century BCE** (a commonly proposed range is 225--175 BCE). Although the narrative is set during the Assyrian captivity of the 8th century BCE, the theological concerns, ritual details, emphasis on almsgiving, endogamous marriage customs, and demonological beliefs all reflect **Second Temple Judaism** during the Persian or early Hellenistic period. The place of composition is uncertain, but many scholars favor the **eastern Jewish diaspora** (Mesopotamia or Persia), given the story's detailed knowledge of the geography between Nineveh and Ecbatana and its concern with preserving Jewish identity in exile.

### Key Manuscripts

**Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran Cave 4)**

Five fragmentary manuscripts were discovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls, catalogued as 4Q196 through 4Q200 and dating from roughly 100 BCE to 25 CE:

| Manuscript | Language | Approximate Date | Content Coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4Q196 (4QTob^a ar) | Aramaic | ca. 50--25 BCE | Tobit 1:17 through 14:7 (most extensive) |
| 4Q197 (4QTob^b ar) | Aramaic | ca. 25 BCE -- 25 CE | Tobit 3:6 through 9:4 |
| 4Q198 (4QTob^c ar) | Aramaic | ca. 50 BCE | Tobit 14:2--6, 14:10 |
| 4Q199 (4QTob^d ar) | Aramaic | ca. 100 BCE | Tobit 7:11, 14:10 |
| 4Q200 (4QTob^e) | Hebrew | ca. 30 BCE -- 20 CE | Fragments through 14:2 |

These fragments generally align with the longer Greek text tradition (GII/Sinaiticus) and confirmed the Semitic origin of the book.

**Greek Recensions**

The Book of Tobit survives in three distinct Greek recensions:

- **GI (Short Text):** Found in Codex Vaticanus (B) and Codex Alexandrinus (A). Written in more idiomatic Greek, shorter and more polished. This was the version most commonly used in scholarship before the Qumran discoveries.
- **GII (Long Text):** Found in Codex Sinaiticus (4th century CE). Approximately 1,700 words longer than GI, with a strongly Semitic flavor and many additional narrative details. The vast majority of modern scholars consider GII closer to the original because its readings align more closely with the Aramaic and Hebrew fragments from Qumran.
- **GIII:** A fragmentary recension preserved in manuscripts 44, 106, and 107, covering only Tobit 6:9 through 13:8.

**Latin Versions**

- **Vetus Latina (Old Latin):** Translated from a Greek text very close to GII/Sinaiticus and preserves the entire book. It is invaluable for reconstructing the longer text where Sinaiticus has lacunae (notably 4:7--18 and 13:7--9).
- **Vulgate:** Jerome's Latin translation, which he reportedly produced in a single day by dictating to a scribe while a rabbi translated orally from an Aramaic manuscript. Jerome's version is shorter and somewhat freer than the Old Latin.

Additional ancient translations exist in **Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Arabic**.

## Historical Context

### The Setting vs. the World of the Author

The narrative is set during the **Assyrian Empire's deportation of the northern tribes of Israel** (after the fall of Samaria in 722 BCE). The protagonist Tobit, of the tribe of Naphtali, lives as a deportee in the Assyrian capital of Nineveh under the reigns of Shalmaneser V, Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon. His kinswoman Sarah lives in Ecbatana, the capital of Media (modern-day Hamadan, Iran).

However, the book was actually composed centuries later, during the **Hellenistic period**, when Jewish communities were dispersed across the Near East. The real audience was the Jewish diaspora of the 3rd--2nd centuries BCE, navigating the challenge of maintaining religious identity, Torah observance, and communal solidarity while living as minorities under foreign rule.

### Community and Purpose

The book was produced by and for **diaspora Jews** who needed practical and theological guidance for life in exile. It addressed pressing questions: How does one remain faithful to God far from the Temple? How should one marry? What obligations does one owe to the dead, to the poor, and to one's kin? The story answered these questions through the example of Tobit's household -- a family that suffers unjustly, perseveres in piety, and is ultimately rewarded by God through the hidden agency of an angel.

The text also integrates the **Story of Ahikar**, a widely circulated Near Eastern wisdom tale. Ahikar appears as Tobit's nephew, and his proverbial wisdom influenced the ethical teachings woven throughout the narrative. This literary borrowing reflects the cosmopolitan intellectual environment in which the book was composed.

## Canonical Status

The canonical status of the Book of Tobit varies across religious traditions:

| Tradition | Status |
|---|---|
| **Roman Catholic Church** | Deuterocanonical (fully canonical). Affirmed by the Council of Rome (382), Council of Hippo (393), Councils of Carthage (397, 419), Council of Florence (1442), and definitively by the Council of Trent (1546). |
| **Eastern Orthodox Churches** | Deuterocanonical / Anagignoskomena (recognized as scriptural). Included in the Septuagint-based canon. |
| **Oriental Orthodox Churches** | Canonical. Included in the broader Old Testament canon. |
| **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** | Canonical. Part of the broader 81-book Ethiopian biblical canon. |
| **Anglican Communion** | Listed among the Apocrypha in the Thirty-Nine Articles; read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not used to establish doctrine. |
| **Protestant Churches** | Generally classified as Apocrypha. Not included in the canon, though historically printed between the Old and New Testaments in early Protestant Bibles (including early editions of the King James Version). Martin Luther included it in his German Bible's Apocrypha section, calling its contents useful. |
| **Judaism (Rabbinic)** | Not included in the Masoretic canon. Despite the Qumran evidence of early Jewish use, Tobit was not accepted into the rabbinic canon finalized in the early centuries CE. |

## Summary

### Chapters 1--2: Tobit's Piety and Affliction

Tobit, a devout Israelite of the tribe of Naphtali, introduces himself and recounts his life. Before the Assyrian conquest, he alone among his kinsmen remained faithful to the Temple in Jerusalem while others in his tribe worshipped the golden calves at Dan. After being deported to Nineveh, Tobit continues to observe the Law of Moses scrupulously. He gives alms to the poor, provides food and clothing to the needy, and -- at great personal risk -- buries the bodies of fellow Israelites who have been executed and left unburied by the Assyrian authorities.

Under King Shalmaneser, Tobit prospers and entrusts ten talents of silver to a relative named Gabael in the city of Rages in Media. But when Sennacherib comes to power, Tobit's practice of burying the dead brings him into danger; his property is confiscated and he must flee. After Sennacherib is assassinated, Esarhaddon restores Tobit's fortunes through the intercession of Tobit's nephew Ahikar.

One night, after burying another dead Israelite, Tobit sleeps outside in the courtyard. Sparrow droppings fall into his eyes, producing white films that gradually render him completely **blind**. Physicians fail to cure him. He becomes dependent on his wife Anna, who takes up weaving to support the family. When a goat is given to Anna as a bonus, Tobit accuses her of stealing it, and she rebukes him sharply. Humiliated and despairing, Tobit prays to God for death.

### Chapter 3: Sarah's Parallel Crisis and God's Response

Meanwhile, in the distant city of **Ecbatana** in Media, a young woman named **Sarah**, daughter of Raguel, endures her own agony. She has been married seven times, but each husband has been killed on the wedding night by the demon **Asmodeus** before the marriage could be consummated. Her father's maidservants mock her and accuse her of murdering her husbands. Sarah, too, prays for death -- or at least for God to deliver her from her shame.

God hears both prayers simultaneously and dispatches the archangel **Raphael** to heal them both: to cure Tobit's blindness and to free Sarah from the demon, binding her in marriage to Tobias, Tobit's son.

### Chapters 4--6: The Journey Begins

Remembering the money deposited with Gabael, Tobit instructs his son **Tobias** to travel to Rages to retrieve it. Before the journey, Tobit delivers a lengthy discourse of **fatherly wisdom**: give alms, avoid fornication, marry within the kindred, pay laborers promptly, do not drink to excess, seek counsel from the wise, and bless God at all times.

Tobias goes out to find a traveling companion and encounters a young man who is actually the angel Raphael in human disguise, calling himself **Azariah** ("God has helped"). Tobit interviews Azariah, is satisfied, and sends them on their way. Tobias's **dog** accompanies them on the journey -- a small but memorable detail, one of the earliest appearances of a pet dog as a companion in biblical literature.

While camping beside the **Tigris River**, a large fish leaps up and tries to swallow Tobias's foot. Raphael instructs him to catch it and to preserve its **heart, liver, and gall**, explaining that the heart and liver, when burned as incense, can drive away demons, and the gall can cure blindness.

### Chapters 7--8: The Marriage of Tobias and Sarah

Raphael leads Tobias to the house of Raguel, Sarah's father, in Ecbatana. Raguel recognizes Tobias as his kinsman and agrees to the marriage, though he is fearful, having buried seven of Sarah's previous husbands. He quietly begins digging a grave for Tobias, just in case.

On the **wedding night**, Tobias follows Raphael's instructions. He burns the fish's heart and liver on incense coals, and the odor drives the demon Asmodeus to **Upper Egypt**, where Raphael pursues and binds him. Tobias and Sarah then **pray together** before consummating their marriage, asking God for mercy and protection -- a prayer that has become one of the most celebrated passages in the book:

> "Blessed are you, O God of our ancestors... You made Adam, and for him you made his wife Eve as a helper and support. From the two of them the human race has sprung. You said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; let us make a helper for him like himself.' I now am taking this kinswoman of mine, not because of lust, but with sincerity. Grant that she and I may find mercy and that we may grow old together." (Tobit 8:5--7)

Raguel sends a servant to check if Tobias is still alive. Finding him safe, Raguel fills in the grave he had dug, and the household celebrates a fourteen-day wedding feast.

### Chapters 9--11: The Return and Healing

During the feast, Raphael travels to Rages alone, retrieves the money from Gabael, and brings Gabael back to join the celebration. Meanwhile, back in Nineveh, Tobit and Anna anxiously count the days. Anna watches the road daily for her son's return, weeping and fearing the worst.

After the feast, Tobias and Sarah depart for Nineveh with half of Raguel's wealth as a dowry. The **dog runs ahead** of them, an endearing detail noted by the narrator. Anna spots Tobias approaching from a distance and runs to tell Tobit.

Following Raphael's instructions, Tobias applies the **fish gall** to his father's eyes. The white films peel away, and **Tobit's sight is restored**. He weeps with joy, blesses God, and welcomes his new daughter-in-law Sarah.

### Chapter 12: Raphael's Revelation

Tobit and Tobias wish to pay their guide "Azariah" half of the recovered treasure. Instead, Raphael reveals his true identity: he is **Raphael, one of the seven angels who stand in the glorious presence of the Lord**. He tells them that he was sent by God in response to their prayers. He reveals that it was he who presented Tobit's prayers and his acts of burying the dead before the throne of God.

Raphael delivers a final exhortation -- praising prayer, fasting, and almsgiving -- and declares: **"It is better to give alms than to lay up gold"** and **"almsgiving delivers from death and purges away every sin."** He then ascends to heaven, and father and son fall to the ground in awe.

### Chapter 13: Tobit's Hymn of Praise

Tobit composes a magnificent **hymn of thanksgiving and prophecy**, praising God's justice and mercy. The hymn moves from personal gratitude to a vision of cosmic redemption: God scatters Israel among the nations but will gather them again. Jerusalem, though now in ruins, will be rebuilt with **sapphires, emeralds, and precious stones**, and all nations will come to worship the God of Israel. The hymn echoes themes from Isaiah, Psalms, and later apocalyptic literature, and it looks forward to a restored Temple and a glorified Jerusalem.

### Chapter 14: Tobit's Final Words and Death

In his old age, Tobit delivers a **deathbed prophecy** to Tobias. He foretells the **destruction of Nineveh** (which historically occurred in 612 BCE) and warns Tobias to leave the city before it falls. He prophesies the destruction and rebuilding of the Temple, the eventual return of all Israel from exile, and a final age in which all nations will turn to the true God.

Tobit dies at the age of 112 (or 158, depending on the manuscript) and is buried with honor. Anna dies and is buried beside him. Tobias and Sarah move to **Ecbatana** to live with Raguel. Before his own death, Tobias lives to hear of the **destruction of Nineveh** by Nebuchadnezzar and Ahasuerus (a literary conflation of historical figures), vindicating his father's prophecy. Tobias dies at the age of 117, having seen the fulfillment of God's justice.

## Key Themes

### Divine Providence and Hidden Agency

The central theological claim of Tobit is that **God is actively at work in the lives of the faithful, even when his presence is invisible**. Raphael walks alongside Tobias for the entire journey without ever being recognized as an angel. The story insists that suffering is not evidence of divine absence; rather, God's response may already be in motion before the prayer is even finished.

### Almsgiving and Righteousness

Tobit elevates **almsgiving** to the highest ethical imperative. Raphael's climactic revelation declares that almsgiving "delivers from death and purges away every sin" (12:9). Acts of charity -- feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, burying the dead -- are presented as the tangible expression of covenant faithfulness. This teaching profoundly influenced later Christian theology on the merit of good works.

### Burial of the Dead

Tobit's insistence on **burying executed Israelites** despite the danger to himself is the act of piety that sets the entire plot in motion. In a diaspora context where the dead could become socially expendable, the story treats burial as a declaration that no political regime gets the final word on a person's dignity. This theme resonated strongly with early Christians, who adopted the burial of the dead as one of the corporal works of mercy.

### Marriage, Family, and Endogamy

The marriage of Tobias and Sarah is presented as a **covenantal act rooted in faith and prayer**, not merely a social arrangement. Their wedding-night prayer (8:5--7) is a model of spiritual partnership. The book consistently advocates **marriage within the kinship group** (endogamy) as a means of preserving communal identity in exile. Family loyalty -- honoring parents, caring for in-laws, raising children in the faith -- is a pervasive concern.

### Testing, Suffering, and Faithfulness

Both Tobit and Sarah suffer innocently and unjustly. The book reframes suffering not as divine punishment but as a **test of faith** -- an idea that parallels the Book of Job, though Tobit resolves the tension far more optimistically. The righteous may suffer, but God hears their prayers and vindicates them in the end.

### Angels and Demons

Tobit offers one of the most developed **angelologies** in biblical literature. Raphael is one of seven archangels who stand before God's throne, serving as intercessor, guide, healer, and exorcist. The demon Asmodeus represents malevolent spiritual forces that can afflict human beings but are ultimately subject to divine authority. These ideas deeply influenced later Jewish and Christian angelology and demonology.

### Prayer

Nearly every major turning point in the narrative is preceded or accompanied by **prayer**: Tobit's lament, Sarah's plea, the wedding-night prayer, Tobit's hymn of praise. The book models prayer as the primary human response to both suffering and blessing, and portrays God as a deity who genuinely listens and acts.

## Influence & Legacy

### Visual Art

No apocryphal book has inspired more **visual art** than Tobit. The story was enormously popular among Renaissance and Baroque painters, and the motif of **"Tobias and the Angel"** -- the young man walking with his angelic companion, often carrying a fish -- became one of the most frequently depicted biblical scenes in Western art.

**Rembrandt van Rijn** had a particular fascination with the story, producing at least **55 paintings, drawings, and etchings** depicting scenes from Tobit across his career: Tobit sleeping beneath the swallow's nest, Anna and the blind Tobit at home, Tobias frightened by the fish, Tobias and Sarah praying, the departure of Raphael, and many others. Other major artists who depicted Tobit subjects include Andrea del Verrocchio, the workshop of which produced the famous *Tobias and the Angel* now in the National Gallery, London (sometimes attributed to the young Leonardo da Vinci), as well as works by Filippino Lippi, Giovanni Biliverti, and many others.

### Catholic and Christian Liturgy

The **prayer of Tobias and Sarah** (Tobit 8:4b--8) is one of the approved Old Testament readings for the **Catholic Rite of Marriage** and is also used in Anglican marriage services. Its portrayal of a couple praying together before God on their wedding night has made it a touchstone for sacramental theology of marriage. The book's teachings on almsgiving, prayer, and fasting shaped early Christian ethics and were cited by the Church Fathers, including Cyprian, Ambrose, and Augustine.

### Literature and Drama

The story has been adapted repeatedly for stage and screen. **James Bridie's** play *Tobias and the Angel* (1930) became one of the best-loved modern British dramas. The composer **Arthur Bliss** wrote an opera based on the story, and **Jonathan Dove** composed a community opera titled *Tobias and the Angel* (1999) with a libretto by David Lan. **Joseph Haydn's** oratorio *Il ritorno di Tobia* (1775) drew on the narrative. The novel *Tobit's Dog* by Michael Nicholas Richard reimagines the story in a modern American setting.

### Theological Influence

Tobit's theology of **almsgiving as redemptive** (12:9: "almsgiving delivers from death") was widely cited in early Christian debates about the relationship between faith and works. The book's angelology -- particularly the concept of seven archangels and the idea that angels present human prayers before God -- influenced Jewish mystical traditions, Christian liturgical theology, and Islamic angelology (Raphael appears as Israfil in some traditions).

### The Dog

A small but culturally resonant detail: Tobias's dog, which accompanies him on the journey and runs ahead on the return, is **one of the earliest positive depictions of a companion dog in ancient literature**. Scholars have noted parallels to the faithful dog Argos in Homer's *Odyssey*, who recognizes the returning Odysseus. The dog has no plot function but adds warmth and domesticity to the narrative, and it has captured the imagination of artists and readers across centuries.
