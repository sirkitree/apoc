# 1 Maccabees

**A stirring historical chronicle of the Jewish revolt against the Seleucid Empire, the liberation of Jerusalem, and the rededication of the Second Temple -- the foundational story behind the festival of Hanukkah.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original language:** 1 Maccabees was composed in Hebrew, though the original Hebrew text has been entirely lost. The work survives only in its Koine Greek translation within the Septuagint. The Greek, however, bears the unmistakable stamp of a rather literal translation from a Semitic source -- Hebraic idioms, syntax, and phrasing run throughout the text. Both Origen and Jerome claimed to have seen a Hebrew (or possibly Aramaic) version. Origen recorded the title of the Hebrew original as *Sarbeth Sarbanael*, translated variously as "the Book of the Prince of the House of Israel" or "the Book of the Dynasty of God's Resisters."

**Key manuscripts:** The Greek text is preserved in two of the three great uncial codices of the Septuagint:

- **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century CE) -- one of the oldest and most important biblical manuscripts in existence
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- a nearly complete copy of the Greek Bible
- **Codex Venetus** (8th-9th century CE) -- a later but significant witness to the text

Notably, 1 Maccabees is *absent* from the Codex Vaticanus. Additional witnesses include several minuscule (cursive) manuscripts. Werner Kappler published a critical edition of the Greek text in 1936, assembling an eclectic reconstruction from the surviving manuscript evidence.

**Date and place of composition:** The anonymous author almost certainly wrote during the reign of John Hyrcanus I (high priest from 135/134 to 104 BCE), likely from within the newly independent Hasmonean kingdom in Judea. The text must date to before the Roman general Pompey's conquest of Jerusalem in 63 BCE. Most scholars place composition roughly between 110 and 100 BCE, making it a near-contemporary account of events that had concluded only a generation or two earlier.

## Historical Context

To understand 1 Maccabees, you need to understand the crisis that provoked it.

### Alexander's Legacy and the Seleucid Empire

After Alexander the Great conquered the ancient Near East in the 330s BCE, his empire fractured into successor kingdoms. Judea eventually fell under the control of the **Seleucid Empire**, a Hellenistic dynasty ruling from Syria. For a time, Jewish life under Seleucid rule continued with relative autonomy. But tensions were building.

### The Hellenization Crisis

Greek culture -- its language, athletics, philosophy, religion, and civic institutions -- exerted a powerful pull on the peoples of the eastern Mediterranean. Some Jews embraced Hellenistic customs enthusiastically; others saw the adoption of Greek ways as a betrayal of the covenant with God. This was not simply an external conflict between Jews and Greeks. It was, at its heart, an internal Jewish struggle over identity: *What does it mean to be faithful to the God of Israel in a world dominated by Greek culture?*

The crisis came to a head under **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** (reigned 175-164 BCE). Struggles for control of the Jerusalem high priesthood -- which was not merely a religious office but the effective political leadership of Judea under Seleucid suzerainty -- gave Antiochus a pretext for intervention. After a humiliating forced withdrawal from Egypt by Rome in 168 BCE, Antiochus turned his frustrations on Judea.

### The Persecution

What followed was unprecedented in the ancient world: a systematic campaign to suppress a people's religion. Antiochus:

- Outlawed the observance of the Sabbath and Jewish festivals
- Prohibited circumcision under penalty of death
- Ordered the burning of Torah scrolls
- Compelled Jews to sacrifice to pagan gods and eat forbidden foods
- Desecrated the Jerusalem Temple by erecting an altar to Zeus on the altar of burnt offering -- what 1 Maccabees calls "the abomination of desolation"

The faithful faced a stark choice: abandon the covenant or die.

## Canonical Status

The status of 1 Maccabees varies significantly across religious traditions:

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Roman Catholic** | **Deuterocanonical** (fully canonical). Affirmed by the Council of Hippo (393), the Council of Carthage (397), the Council of Florence (1442), and definitively by the Council of Trent (1546). |
| **Eastern Orthodox** | **Canonical.** Accepted as part of the Old Testament from the early Church period, included in the longer canon received through the Septuagint. |
| **Oriental Orthodox** | **Canonical** in most traditions, though the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church does not include it. |
| **Protestant** | **Apocryphal.** Classified during the Reformation as useful for edification but not authoritative for doctrine. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1643) declared such books "of no authority in the Church of God." Many Protestant Bibles omit it entirely, though some include it in a separate Apocrypha section. |
| **Jewish** | **Non-canonical.** Never included in the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible). Despite its subject matter, later Jewish leaders viewed the Hasmonean dynasty critically for several reasons -- including their combination of royal and priestly authority -- and a text so openly celebratory of them was problematic. The stories of the Maccabees were preserved in Jewish tradition through the Talmud and other rabbinic literature, but the book itself was not transmitted. |

## Summary

### Prologue: The Shadow of Alexander (Chapter 1)

The book opens with Alexander the Great's conquests and the fracturing of his empire among his generals. The narrative then leaps forward to the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Renegade Jews, eager to assimilate, build a gymnasium in Jerusalem and "made themselves uncircumcised." Antiochus, emboldened, plunders the Temple, strips it of its gold furnishings, and installs a military garrison in Jerusalem. He then issues his devastating decrees against Jewish religious practice. The Temple is profaned with the "abomination of desolation." The chapter closes on a note of desolation: faithful Jews are executed, Torah scrolls are burned, and the land mourns.

### The Rise of Mattathias (Chapter 2)

In the village of Modein, northwest of Jerusalem, a priest named **Mattathias** -- father of five sons -- witnesses a fellow Jew about to offer a pagan sacrifice at the command of Seleucid officials. Mattathias is seized with righteous fury. He kills the apostate Jew and the king's officer, tears down the pagan altar, and cries out: *"Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out with me!"* He and his sons flee to the hills, launching a guerrilla campaign against both Seleucid forces and Jewish collaborators. On his deathbed, Mattathias charges his sons to continue the fight. He singles out **Simon** for his wisdom ("he shall be your father") and **Judas** for his warrior's strength. Mattathias dies in 166 BCE.

### Judas Maccabeus: Warrior and Liberator (Chapters 3-9)

**Judas**, nicknamed "Maccabeus" (probably meaning "the Hammer"), takes command and becomes the central hero of the book's middle section. Despite being vastly outnumbered, Judas wins a series of stunning victories against Seleucid armies:

- He defeats **Apollonius**, governor of Samaria, and takes his sword, which he uses for the rest of his life
- He routs the larger force of **Seron** at the pass of Beth-horon
- He defeats the massive armies of **Nicanor** and **Gorgias** through bold tactics and faith in God
- He overcomes **Lysias**, regent to the young Antiochus V, in a major engagement at Beth-zur

In **164 BCE**, Judas enters Jerusalem, cleanses the Temple of its pagan defilements, rebuilds the altar, and rededicates the sanctuary. The celebration lasts eight days. Judas and his brothers decree that this rededication should be commemorated annually -- the origin of **Hanukkah** (1 Maccabees 4:36-59).

The narrative continues with Judas's campaigns to rescue persecuted Jewish communities in Gilead, Galilee, and other regions. After the death of Antiochus IV on a failed expedition in Persia, the political situation grows more complex. Judas negotiates a treaty of friendship with Rome -- the first recorded diplomatic contact between the Jewish people and the Roman Republic. But the military tide turns. In 160 BCE, Judas faces an overwhelming Seleucid force under **Bacchides** at the Battle of Elasa. Refusing to flee, Judas fights and falls. His death is mourned in words echoing David's lament for Saul: *"How is the mighty fallen, the savior of Israel!"*

### Jonathan: Diplomat and High Priest (Chapters 9-12)

After Judas's death, his brother **Jonathan** ("Apphus," meaning "the wary") takes over in desperate circumstances. Jonathan begins as a guerrilla leader in the wilderness, gradually rebuilding Maccabean power. But Jonathan's genius lies less in the battlefield than in diplomacy and political maneuvering.

As rival claimants to the Seleucid throne compete for Jewish support, Jonathan plays them against each other with remarkable skill. He is appointed **high priest** in 152 BCE by the Seleucid pretender Alexander Balas -- the first time a Maccabee holds that office. He secures additional territorial concessions, military authority, and tax exemptions. Jonathan renews the alliance with Rome and establishes diplomatic ties with Sparta.

Jonathan's downfall comes through treachery. In 143 BCE, the Seleucid general **Diodotus Tryphon** lures Jonathan to a meeting at Ptolemais (modern Acre) with promises of friendship. Jonathan, despite his reputation for caution, walks into the trap with only a small escort. He is captured, and later executed.

### Simon: Independence and Betrayal (Chapters 13-16)

The last surviving son of Mattathias, **Simon** ("Thassi"), assumes leadership. Simon is presented as the culmination of the Maccabean project. He achieves what his brothers fought and died for: **full political independence**.

In 142 BCE, Simon secures formal recognition of Jewish autonomy from the Seleucid king Demetrius II. The yoke of foreign tribute is lifted from Israel for the first time in centuries. The book marks this as a momentous occasion: the people begin dating documents by "the first year of Simon the great high priest and commander and leader of the Jews." Simon expels the Seleucid garrison from the Akra, the fortress that had loomed over the Temple for decades. He fortifies Jerusalem, secures the port of Joppa (Jaffa), and presides over a period of peace and prosperity.

A hymn of praise in Chapter 14 celebrates Simon's reign in almost messianic terms: the people farm their land in peace, the old sit in the streets, the young dress in splendor, and no one makes them afraid.

But the story does not end in triumph. In 134 BCE, Simon's own son-in-law, **Ptolemy son of Abubus**, murders Simon and two of his sons at a banquet in Jericho -- a treacherous bid for power. Simon's surviving son, **John Hyrcanus**, escapes and succeeds his father. The book closes by noting that the rest of John's deeds are recorded elsewhere, pointing forward to the continuation of the Hasmonean dynasty.

## Key Themes

### Zeal for the Law

The driving force of 1 Maccabees. Mattathias's rallying cry -- "Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out with me!" -- defines the entire revolt. Fidelity to Torah is the measure of a righteous person. Compromise is apostasy. The contest, as the author frames it, is not simply between Jew and Gentile but between those who uphold the law and those, whether Jewish or pagan, who would destroy it.

### Divine Providence Through Human Agency

One of the most distinctive theological features of 1 Maccabees is what it does *not* say. The book almost never mentions God directly. There are no overt miracles, no angelic interventions, no prophetic visions. Yet divine guidance permeates the narrative. The author interprets military victories as evidence of God's hand working *through* the courage, strategy, and faithfulness of human agents. This is a theology of providence -- God acting in history through those who remain loyal to the covenant.

### The Centrality of the Temple

The desecration and restoration of the Jerusalem Temple is the emotional and theological heart of the book. The Temple represents God's presence among the people; its profanation is the ultimate catastrophe, and its rededication is the supreme triumph. The festival of Hanukkah, instituted to commemorate the rededication, gives the Temple theme permanent liturgical expression.

### Legitimate Leadership and Dynastic Succession

The book serves, in part, as a legitimation of the Hasmonean dynasty. Each brother -- Judas, Jonathan, Simon -- is presented as a worthy leader who arises at the hour of need. The narrative traces an arc from resistance fighter to high priest to independent ruler, culminating in Simon's formal investiture and the establishment of a hereditary leadership. The book is, in a real sense, a foundation narrative for the Hasmonean state.

### The Danger of Hellenization

The author draws a sharp line between Jewish faithfulness and Greek cultural assimilation. Hellenizers are portrayed as traitors who "sold themselves to do evil." Yet the book is not entirely anti-Greek in a simple sense -- Judas and his successors skillfully engage in Hellenistic diplomacy, forge alliances with Rome and Sparta, and operate within the political conventions of the Hellenistic world. The opposition is not to Greek culture as such but to the abandonment of Jewish religious identity.

### Literary Artistry: Biblical Imitation

The author consciously models his style on the historical books of the Hebrew Bible (especially Judges, Samuel, and Kings). The prose is terse, restrained, and dignified. Seven poetic sections -- four laments and three hymns of praise -- interrupt the narrative, imitating classical Hebrew poetic forms. Characters are compared to biblical heroes; battles echo the victories of Israel's past. The effect is to place the Maccabean revolt within the grand sweep of salvation history: these are not merely political events but the latest chapter in God's ongoing relationship with Israel.

## Influence & Legacy

### Hanukkah

The most direct and enduring legacy of 1 Maccabees is the festival of **Hanukkah** (Hebrew for "dedication"), commemorating Judas Maccabeus's cleansing and rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE. The eight-day celebration described in 1 Maccabees 4:56-59 became one of the most widely observed Jewish holidays. While later rabbinic tradition emphasized the miracle of the oil (found in the Talmud, not in 1 Maccabees), the historical narrative of religious resistance and Temple restoration originates here. Hanukkah's meaning has evolved over the centuries -- from a celebration of Temple renewal to a symbol of religious freedom, cultural survival, and resistance to oppression.

### Theology and Religious Thought

For Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, 1 Maccabees has served as a proof text for various doctrinal positions. The book's accounts of sacrifice and prayer on behalf of the dead (particularly in the companion text 2 Maccabees) influenced the development of Christian theology regarding purgatory and prayers for the deceased. The Maccabean martyrs became models of faithfulness under persecution, venerated as saints in several Christian traditions. The feast of the Holy Maccabean Martyrs (August 1) is one of the few pre-Christian observances in the Christian liturgical calendar.

### Political and National Identity

The Maccabees have served as a potent symbol of national liberation and resistance across many centuries and cultures:

- For **early modern Jews** in Europe and America, the Maccabees represented the capacity of a small people to resist a great empire
- For **Zionists** before and after 1948, the story provided a powerful narrative of Jewish national sovereignty in the ancestral homeland
- The name "Maccabee" has been adopted by Jewish athletic organizations (the Maccabiah Games), military units, and cultural institutions worldwide
- The story resonated with **American revolutionaries** and other national liberation movements who saw parallels to their own struggles

### Art, Music, and Literature

The Maccabean story has inspired some of Western civilization's most celebrated works:

- **George Frideric Handel** composed the oratorio *Judas Maccabaeus* (1746), one of his most popular works, originally conceived as a tribute to the Duke of Cumberland after the Battle of Culloden. The famous chorus "See, the Conqu'ring Hero Comes" originated in this oratorio before Handel transplanted it to *Joshua*
- **Peter Paul Rubens**, the great Flemish painter, depicted Judas Maccabeus in dramatic compositions
- **Henry Wadsworth Longfellow** wrote a five-act closet drama, *Judas Maccabaeus* (1872)
- The story has been adapted into numerous modern novels, films, children's books, and graphic novels
- Hanukkah itself has generated a vast body of liturgical music, visual art, and popular culture

### Historiography

1 Maccabees remains an indispensable primary source for historians of the Hellenistic period. Along with Josephus (who relied heavily on 1 Maccabees for his *Jewish Antiquities*), it provides the most detailed account of Seleucid-Jewish relations, the political dynamics of the Hellenistic successor states, and the emergence of an independent Jewish polity in the second century BCE.
