# 2 Maccabees

**A dramatic theological history of the Jewish struggle against forced Hellenization, renowned for its vivid martyrdom narratives and its pioneering articulation of resurrection, prayer for the dead, and creation from nothing.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original Language:** Koine Greek. Unlike 1 Maccabees (composed in Hebrew and surviving only in Greek translation), 2 Maccabees was written directly in Greek by an anonymous Jewish author living in the Hellenistic diaspora, most likely in Alexandria, Egypt.

**Date of Composition:** Approximately 150--100 BCE. The text itself is an abridgment (or "epitome") of a now-lost five-volume history written by Jason of Cyrene, who chronicled events from the death of the Seleucid king Seleucus IV (175 BCE) through the defeat of the general Nicanor (161 BCE).

**Key Manuscripts:**

- **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century CE) -- One of the oldest and most important witnesses for the Septuagint text, preserved in the Vatican Library.
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- A major Septuagint manuscript now housed in the British Library. Contains 2 Maccabees among its deuterocanonical books.
- **Codex Venetus** (8th century CE) -- Exists in two parts split between the Vatican Library and Venice; its Venetian portion includes 1--4 Maccabees among other deuterocanonical books.
- Numerous Greek minuscule manuscripts, as well as manuscript witnesses in **Syriac**, **Armenian**, and **Latin** translation traditions.

Notably, **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century), despite being one of the great biblical codices, does *not* contain 2 Maccabees -- though it does include 1 and 4 Maccabees.

## Historical Context

### The Maccabean Revolt

In the 160s and 170s BCE, the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes launched a campaign to forcibly Hellenize the Jews of Judea. He rededicated the Jerusalem Temple to Zeus, outlawed circumcision, Sabbath observance, and Torah study, and punished noncompliance with death. A Jewish family led by the priest Mattathias and his sons -- most famously Judas, nicknamed "Maccabeus" (the Hammer) -- rose in armed revolt. Their unlikely military victories against the Seleucid Empire culminated in the recapture and rededication of the Temple, the event commemorated as Hanukkah.

### Relationship to 1 Maccabees

Despite their shared subject matter, 1 and 2 Maccabees are independent works, not a sequence. They differ in almost every dimension:

| | **1 Maccabees** | **2 Maccabees** |
|---|---|---|
| **Original language** | Hebrew | Greek |
| **Author's location** | Palestine | Diaspora (likely Egypt) |
| **Timespan covered** | ~175--134 BCE | ~175--161 BCE |
| **Perspective** | Political/military chronicle; pro-Hasmonean court history | Theological interpretation; God as the true protagonist |
| **Emphasis** | Bravery, combat, dynastic succession | Martyrdom, divine intervention, resurrection |

Where 1 Maccabees reads like a sober dynastic chronicle written to legitimize the Hasmonean ruling family, 2 Maccabees is openly didactic and theological. Its anonymous epitomist tells us he aims at "brevity, clarity, and edification" rather than exhaustive detail, comparing himself to a decorator who adds beauty to an existing structure.

### A Theological Reading of History

For the author of 2 Maccabees, the revolt is not simply a political or military event -- it is a drama of sin, punishment, repentance, and divine deliverance. The persecutions under Antiochus are understood as God's discipline of a wayward people; the martyrs' faithfulness turns aside God's wrath; and the military victories that follow are signs of divine favor restored. God, not Judas, is the hero of the story.

## Canonical Status

The canonical status of 2 Maccabees has been debated for centuries:

- **Roman Catholic Church:** Canonical (deuterocanonical). The Council of Trent (1546) formally affirmed its inclusion in the biblical canon. It is regularly read in the liturgy and has been cited in doctrinal definitions, particularly regarding prayer for the dead and purgatory.
- **Eastern Orthodox Churches:** Canonical. The Orthodox Churches received the longer Septuagint canon from the earliest centuries and regard 2 Maccabees as fully part of the Old Testament.
- **Oriental Orthodox Churches:** Canonical. Accepted as scripture in the Ethiopian, Coptic, and other Oriental Orthodox traditions.
- **Protestant Churches:** Not canonical, but often included as "Apocrypha" -- books considered edifying and historically valuable, though not bearing the authority of scripture. Martin Luther and the Reformers rejected the book's canonical status in part because of its support for practices they opposed, especially prayer for the dead.
- **Judaism:** Not canonical. Although the book circulated among Greek-speaking Jews in antiquity, it was not included in the Masoretic Hebrew canon (the Tanakh) and was eventually set aside by the rabbinic tradition.
- **Church Fathers:** Many early Church Fathers -- including Augustine of Hippo (c. 397 CE) -- listed 1 and 2 Maccabees as canonical scripture, though the matter was not universally settled in the early Church.

## Summary

### Prefixed Letters (Chapters 1--2:18)

The book opens with two letters from Jews in Jerusalem to their brethren in Egypt, urging them to celebrate the feast of the purification of the Temple (Hanukkah). These letters anchor the book in a concrete communal purpose: the diaspora must remember and commemorate what God has done in Jerusalem.

### The Epitomist's Prologue (2:19--32)

The anonymous author introduces himself and explains his project: he is condensing Jason of Cyrene's massive five-volume history into a single, readable work. He asks the reader's indulgence for any shortcomings, likening his task to that of a painter who decorates a house already built by another.

### The Heliodorus Affair (Chapter 3)

During the peaceful high priesthood of Onias III, a Seleucid official named Heliodorus is sent by King Seleucus IV to confiscate treasure from the Temple. As he enters the sacred treasury, a terrifying supernatural vision -- a horse with a fearsome rider and two radiant young men -- strikes him down. Onias intercedes with prayer and sacrifice, and Heliodorus recovers, returning to the king to testify that the God of Israel truly guards the Temple. This dramatic opening establishes the book's central conviction: the Temple and its people are under divine protection.

### Corruption and Hellenization (Chapters 4--5)

The narrative darkens. Jason (brother of Onias) bribes Antiochus IV to appoint him high priest and begins a program of aggressive Hellenization, building a gymnasium in the shadow of the Temple and encouraging Jewish youth to adopt Greek customs. He is in turn displaced by the even more corrupt Menelaus, who arranges the murder of the righteous Onias III. Antiochus invades Jerusalem twice, plundering the Temple and massacring thousands.

### The Persecution (Chapter 6)

Antiochus decrees the abolition of Jewish religion. The Temple is rededicated to Olympian Zeus. Copies of the Torah are destroyed. Women who circumcise their infants are executed with their babies hung around their necks. Amid this horror, the elderly scribe **Eleazar** becomes the book's first great martyr. Rather than eat pork -- or even pretend to comply with the decree -- he willingly goes to his death, declaring that it would be unworthy of his age and dignity to set a false example for the young. His execution is described with great pathos: he is a man choosing integrity over survival.

### The Seven Brothers and Their Mother (Chapter 7)

This is the emotional and theological heart of 2 Maccabees, and one of the most celebrated martyrdom narratives in all of religious literature. Seven brothers are arrested along with their mother and ordered by the king to eat pork. One by one, each refuses and is tortured to death in escalating brutality -- tongues cut out, scalps torn off, hands and feet severed, bodies fried in pans.

What makes the scene extraordinary is not just the graphic suffering but the theological declarations the brothers make as they die:

- **The second brother** (7:9): "You accursed fiend, you are depriving us of this present life, but the King of the universe will raise us up to live again forever."
- **The third brother** (7:11): He stretches out his hands to be cut off, saying, "From Heaven I received these; for the sake of His laws I disregard them; from Him I hope to receive them again."
- **The fourth brother** (7:14): "It is my choice to die at the hands of men with the hope that God will restore me to life; but for you, there will be no resurrection to life."

The **mother**, watching all seven of her sons die, encourages each in turn. She invokes God as Creator, articulating one of the earliest known statements of *creatio ex nihilo* -- creation from nothing: "I beg you, child, to look at the heavens and the earth... and recognize that God did not make them from things that existed" (7:28). She then willingly goes to her own death.

### The Rise of Judas Maccabeus (Chapters 8--10)

Judas Maccabeus organizes resistance, rallying the faithful and leading a guerrilla campaign. The author emphasizes that Judas's victories are accomplished through divine aid -- angelic horsemen appear on the battlefield; enemies are routed by heavenly terror. Judas defeats the generals Nicanor, Gorgias, and Timothy.

Antiochus IV, campaigning in the east, is struck by a gruesome illness that the author portrays as divine punishment: his body is consumed by worms, and his flesh rots while he still lives. Before dying, he is said to have acknowledged the God of Israel, though this confession comes too late.

Judas and his followers recapture the Temple, cleanse it of pagan defilement, and rededicate it. They celebrate for eight days "in the manner of the festival of booths" -- the origin of the **eight-day Hanukkah festival**. The author notes that just shortly before, they had been "wandering in the mountains and caves like wild animals" (10:6).

### Further Campaigns (Chapters 10--12)

Under the new Seleucid king Antiochus V Eupator, the wars continue. Judas campaigns against hostile neighbors -- Idumeans, Ammonites, and others. At one point, after a battle, Judas discovers that some of his fallen soldiers had been wearing pagan amulets -- a violation of the Torah. He takes up a collection and sends it to Jerusalem as a sin offering on behalf of the dead, "taking into account the resurrection" (12:43). The author comments: "It is therefore a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead, that they may be loosed from sins" (12:46). This passage became one of the most consequential in the history of Christian theology.

### The Defeat of Nicanor (Chapters 13--15)

The final act revolves around the Seleucid general Nicanor, who is sent to subdue Judea. Nicanor initially attempts diplomacy with Judas, but is overruled by the treacherous high priest Alcimus, who denounces him to King Demetrius I. Nicanor turns hostile and threatens to destroy the Temple.

Before the decisive battle, Judas receives a dream-vision in which the murdered high priest Onias III and the prophet Jeremiah appear and hand him a golden sword from God. Emboldened by this sign, Judas leads his forces into battle at Adasa. Nicanor is killed -- the first to fall -- and his army is routed. Judas orders Nicanor's head and his blasphemous right hand displayed beside the Temple.

The book closes with the institution of **Nicanor's Day** (the 13th of Adar) as an annual celebration, and the narrator signs off with a literary flourish, noting that if his account has been well told, that is what he wished; and if it is mediocre, "that is the best I could do" (15:38).

## Key Themes

### Resurrection of the Dead

2 Maccabees provides the clearest and most developed statement of bodily resurrection in any Jewish text written before the Common Era. The seven brothers go to their deaths professing absolute confidence that God will raise them to new life. The fourth brother draws a sharp theological line: the righteous will be resurrected, but the wicked will not. This theology of resurrection would profoundly shape later Jewish and Christian thought, including the Pharisaic tradition that Jesus of Nazareth inherited.

### Martyrdom as Witness

The deaths of Eleazar and the seven brothers establish a paradigm of martyrdom -- suffering and death freely accepted as a testimony of faithfulness to God -- that would become foundational for both Judaism and Christianity. The early Church Fathers regarded the Maccabean martyrs as forerunners of the Christian martyrs, and they were venerated as saints. August 1 was observed as their feast day in many Christian calendars. Some scholars even suggest that the English word "macabre" derives from *Machabaeorum* (Latin for "of the Maccabees"), by way of medieval mystery plays dramatizing their deaths.

### Prayer for the Dead

Judas's offering on behalf of fallen soldiers who had sinned (12:39--45) is the earliest known textual witness to the practice of praying and making atonement for the dead. The author explicitly endorses the practice as pious and salutary. This passage became the primary scriptural basis for the Catholic doctrine of purgatory and the practice of offering Masses for the deceased.

### Creation from Nothing (*Creatio ex Nihilo*)

The mother of the seven brothers, in her final exhortation (7:28), urges her youngest son to "look at the heavens and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed." This is widely regarded as one of the earliest articulations of *creatio ex nihilo* -- the idea that God created the universe not from preexisting matter but from absolute nothing. Early Church Fathers such as Hilary of Poitiers cited this verse when formulating the Christian doctrine of creation.

### Divine Sovereignty and Intervention

Throughout the book, God is the true mover of history. Angelic warriors appear on battlefields, divine illness strikes down persecutors, and visions guide the faithful. Human action matters -- Judas's courage is real -- but it is always God who delivers the final victory.

### The Sanctity of the Temple

The Temple stands at the center of the narrative, from the Heliodorus affair in chapter 3 to Nicanor's threats in chapter 15. The author's message is clear: the Temple belongs to God, and those who profane it will be punished. The Temple's fate mirrors the fate of the people -- when they are faithful, the Temple is inviolable; when they stray, it falls into enemy hands.

## Influence & Legacy

### Impact on Christian Theology

2 Maccabees has had an outsized influence on Christian doctrine relative to its length:

- **Purgatory:** The passage on prayer for the dead (12:39--46) became the cornerstone scriptural text for the Catholic doctrine of purgatory, which holds that the faithful who die with venial sins undergo purification before entering heaven. The Council of Trent explicitly cited 2 Maccabees in its decree on purgatory.
- **Resurrection:** The book's vivid articulation of bodily resurrection shaped early Christian understanding of the afterlife and influenced New Testament theology.
- **Intercession of saints:** The dream-vision in which the deceased Onias III and Jeremiah intercede for the living (15:12--16) provided scriptural grounding for the doctrine of the intercession of saints.
- **Creation theology:** The mother's statement in 7:28 influenced patristic articulations of *creatio ex nihilo*.

### The Maccabean Martyrs in Christian Veneration

The early Church honored Eleazar, the seven brothers, and their mother as saints and martyrs. Church Fathers including Gregory of Nazianzus (who preached a homily "In Praise of the Maccabees" in 362 CE), John Chrysostom, and Augustine wrote admiringly of their courage. Their feast day, August 1, was observed for centuries. They were among the few pre-Christian figures honored with a liturgical feast in the Christian calendar, and their story served as a model for the theology of Christian martyrdom during the Roman persecutions.

### The Protestant Reformation

2 Maccabees became one of the most contested books of the Reformation era. Martin Luther and other Reformers rejected its canonical status in part because it supported Catholic doctrines they opposed -- especially prayer for the dead and the efficacy of intercessory sacrifice. The book's removal from the Protestant canon had far-reaching consequences for how different Christian traditions understand the afterlife.

### Hanukkah

2 Maccabees provides the earliest surviving explanation for why Hanukkah is celebrated for eight days: the newly liberated Jews celebrated "in the manner of the festival of booths" (Sukkot), which lasts eight days, because they had been unable to observe Sukkot while hiding in the mountains (10:6--8). The two letters prefixed to the book are themselves invitations to diaspora Jews to observe the festival.

### Nicanor's Day

The book closes by instituting Nicanor's Day on the 13th of Adar as an annual celebration of Judas's victory. Though the holiday eventually fell out of Jewish observance, it was once listed among the festivals recorded in the ancient *Megillat Taanit* (Scroll of Fasting). Its date, the day before Purim, ensured its eventual eclipse by that more enduring festival.

### Literature, Art, and Culture

- **Dante Alighieri** placed Judas Maccabeus in the Heaven of Mars in the *Divine Comedy*, among the great warriors of the faith.
- **George Frideric Handel** composed the oratorio *Judas Maccabaeus* (1746), one of his most popular works, celebrating the hero of the Maccabean revolt.
- Medieval and Renaissance artists depicted the martyrdom of the seven brothers and their mother in paintings, manuscript illuminations, and mystery plays. Notable works include paintings by Wojciech Stattler (1830s--1840s), Julius Schnorr von Carolsfeld, and Giustino Menescardi.
- The word **"macabre"** may derive from medieval dramatizations of the Maccabean martyrdoms (*danse Macabre* / *chorea Machabaeorum*), though this etymology is debated.
- In the modern era, the Maccabean story has been invoked in contexts of Jewish national identity and resistance, most notably in Zionist discourse and in artistic responses to the Holocaust.
