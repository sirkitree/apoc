# Additions to Daniel

**Three ancient Jewish narratives and hymns that expand the Book of Daniel with tales of faith under fire, justice against corruption, and the folly of idol worship.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Additions to Daniel are three compositions absent from the Hebrew-Aramaic Masoretic Text of Daniel but present in all surviving Greek manuscripts of the book. They were never part of the Jewish Hebrew Bible and exist today because they were incorporated into the Greek Septuagint (LXX) during the Hellenistic period.

### Two Greek Textual Traditions

The additions survive in two distinct Greek versions, each with its own history:

- **The Old Greek (OG) / Original Septuagint** (c. 100 BCE): The earlier translation, which fell out of use by the late 4th century CE. For a long time it survived only in Syriac translation and in a single medieval Greek manuscript, **Codex Chisianus 45** (also known as Codex Chisianus 88, Rahlfs siglum 88). The 1931 discovery of **Papyrus 967**, a 3rd-century CE manuscript from Egypt, dramatically expanded our access to the Old Greek Daniel, including the additions. Notably, in Papyrus 967 the story of Susanna follows Bel and the Dragon rather than preceding the main text of Daniel.

- **Theodotion's Revision** (c. 2nd century CE): A Hellenistic Jewish redactor named Theodotion produced a revised Greek text that eventually replaced the Old Greek version in nearly all Greek manuscripts. This is the version found in the great uncial codices: **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century), **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century), and **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century). Theodotion's text became the standard form used in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles to this day.

### Original Language

The original language of all three additions is debated:

| Addition | Likely Original Language | Notes |
|----------|------------------------|-------|
| **Prayer of Azariah & Song** | Hebrew or Aramaic | The high density of Semitisms strongly suggests a Semitic original. Moses Gaster claimed in the 1890s to have identified an Aramaic source. However, Bruce Metzger found evidence suggesting Greek composition for the Prayer. |
| **Susanna** | Debated (Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek) | Greek wordplay in the trial scene (the puns linking tree names to punishments) has been cited as evidence of Greek composition, but scholars have proposed plausible Hebrew and Aramaic word-pairs that could have served the same function. |
| **Bel and the Dragon** | Possibly Hebrew or Aramaic | Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome all stated that no Semitic form was known in their time. However, comparison of the OG and Theodotion versions reveals divergences characteristic of independent translation from a shared Semitic source. No Semitic original has been discovered to date. |

### Date and Place of Composition

- **Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men**: Most likely composed in the 2nd or 1st century BCE. The Prayer's references to national suffering under a "lawless" and "unjust king" may point to the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (168--165 BCE), while the Song's references to temple worship may suggest a date after the rededication of the Temple (c. 164 BCE). The Prayer and the Song likely originated independently and were combined with the narrative bridge before being inserted into the Daniel text. Probable origin: Palestine.

- **Susanna**: Composed between approximately 200 and 100 BCE. The setting in the Babylonian diaspora and focus on legal procedure in the Jewish community suggest a diaspora origin, though Palestinian composition cannot be ruled out.

- **Bel and the Dragon**: Composed between the 3rd and 1st centuries BCE. Some scholars suggest the Hebrew text may have originated in Palestine around 146 BCE or later, while the Septuagint version was produced in Egypt around 100 BCE. The three additions were likely united with the Greek Daniel no later than 100 BCE.

## Historical Context

Each of the three additions addresses distinct concerns of the Jewish community during the Hellenistic period, when Jews lived as minorities under foreign empires and faced pressure to assimilate into the dominant Greco-Roman culture.

### Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men

This addition fills what ancient readers apparently saw as a narrative gap in Daniel 3. The canonical Hebrew text describes the three young men -- Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah (known by their Babylonian names Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego) -- being thrown into a furnace for refusing to worship Nebuchadnezzar's golden image, then jumps immediately to the king's astonished observation that four figures walk unharmed in the flames. The addition supplies what happens inside the furnace: prayer, divine intervention, and praise. It served the liturgical needs of Jewish and later Christian communities who valued the hymnic material for worship.

### Susanna

Set among the Jewish community in Babylon, this story addresses the corruption of the justice system and the vulnerability of the innocent when those in power abuse their authority. It likely reflects real anxieties about corrupt judges within the Jewish diaspora communities, where elders held enormous legal authority over community members. The story also functions as a "Daniel origin story," depicting the young prophet's first act of divinely inspired wisdom, which is why in the Septuagint it precedes the main book of Daniel (though in the Vulgate it appears as chapter 13).

### Bel and the Dragon

These two satirical episodes -- set during the reign of Cyrus the Great of Persia -- function as anti-idolatry polemic, a genre deeply important to Jewish communities surrounded by Greco-Roman polytheism. The stories ridicule the notion that idols are living gods and assert the supremacy of the God of Israel. The tone is humorous and mocking, closer to popular storytelling than to the apocalyptic visions of canonical Daniel. The narrative reflects Jewish confidence in the intellectual superiority of monotheism and the power of rational investigation to expose religious fraud.

## Canonical Status

The canonical status of the Additions to Daniel has been contested since antiquity and remains a point of divergence among Christian traditions:

| Tradition | Status | Details |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| **Roman Catholic** | **Deuterocanonical** (fully canonical) | Affirmed at the Council of Rome (382 CE), the Council of Hippo (393 CE), and the Council of Trent (1546 CE). The additions are integrated into the Book of Daniel: the Prayer and Song within chapter 3, Susanna as chapter 13, and Bel and the Dragon as chapter 14. |
| **Eastern Orthodox** | **Canonical** | Accepted since the earliest centuries. The Song of the Three Young Men forms the basis of the seventh and eighth odes in the Canon of Biblical Odes during Orthros (Matins). The full passage is read during the Vespers of Holy Saturday. |
| **Oriental Orthodox** | **Canonical** | Included in the scriptural canon of Armenian, Coptic, Ethiopian, and Syriac Orthodox churches. |
| **Anglican** | **Apocryphal but liturgically valued** | Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles classifies the additions as non-canonical for doctrine, yet the Benedicite (from the Song) has appeared in the Book of Common Prayer since 1549 as an alternative canticle for Morning Prayer. |
| **Lutheran** | **Apocryphal but edifying** | Not considered canonical but historically included in Lutheran Bibles between the Old and New Testaments as "useful and good to read." |
| **Reformed / Protestant** | **Apocryphal** | Excluded from the canon entirely. Most modern Protestant Bibles omit these texts. |
| **Judaism** | **Non-canonical** | Never part of the Hebrew Bible. Not cited in any extant early Jewish writings. |

The early Church Father Jerome, who translated the Latin Vulgate, noted the additions were not in the Hebrew text and marked them accordingly, but they were retained in the Vulgate and eventually affirmed as canonical by the Catholic Church.

## Summary

### 1. The Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men

This addition is inserted into Daniel 3, between verses 23 and 24 of the Hebrew text (numbered as Daniel 3:24--90 in Catholic Bibles). It consists of four distinct sections:

**The Prayer of Azariah (vv. 1--22):** Standing in the midst of the blazing furnace, Azariah (whose Babylonian name is Abednego) offers a prayer on behalf of the entire nation of Israel. He begins by praising God's righteousness and justice, then confesses the communal sins that led Israel into exile and subjugation. He acknowledges that their suffering under foreign rule -- including their delivery into the hands of "lawless enemies, most hateful rebels, and an unjust king, the most wicked in all the world" -- is a just consequence of their disobedience. Yet the prayer pivots from confession to supplication: Azariah appeals not to Israel's merit but to God's covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He asks that God accept their contrition as a substitute for the burnt offerings they can no longer perform (since the Temple is inaccessible), and pleads for deliverance so that God's name may be glorified among the nations.

**The Narrative Bridge (vv. 23--27):** A brief prose section describes how the king's servants kept stoking the furnace with naphtha, pitch, tow, and brushwood until the flames blazed forty-nine cubits above the furnace, killing nearby Chaldeans. But the angel of the Lord descended into the furnace and drove the flames outward, making the interior feel like "a moist whistling wind," so that the fire did not touch the three young men at all.

**The Song of the Three Young Men / The Benedicite (vv. 28--68):** Delivered from the flames, all three young men join in a magnificent hymn of praise that calls upon the entire created order to "bless the Lord, praise and exalt him forever." The Song moves systematically through creation -- from the heavenly realm (angels, heavens, waters above the heavens, powers of the Lord) through the celestial bodies (sun, moon, stars) to atmospheric phenomena (rain, dew, winds, fire, heat, cold, frost, ice, lightning) to the earth itself (mountains, hills, springs, seas, rivers) to living creatures (whales, birds, beasts, cattle) and finally to humanity, particularly the people of Israel and the three young men themselves. Each element of creation is summoned to join the chorus of praise. The refrain -- "Bless the Lord... praise and exalt him forever" -- repeats throughout, creating a rhythmic, litany-like structure that lends itself powerfully to liturgical use.

### 2. Susanna (Daniel 13 in Catholic Bibles)

The story of Susanna is a compact courtroom drama set among the Jewish community in Babylon, and it reads like a masterfully crafted short story.

**The Setup:** Susanna is the beautiful and devout wife of Joakim, a wealthy and respected member of the Jewish community in Babylon. Her parents raised her "according to the law of Moses," and she is pious in both conduct and character. Two elders have been appointed as judges over the community that year, and they frequent Joakim's house, where legal cases are heard. Both elders independently develop an obsessive lust for Susanna, whom they observe walking in the garden each day when the household empties at midday.

**The Confrontation:** One hot day, Susanna enters the garden to bathe, sending her maids to fetch oil and ointments and instructing them to shut the garden gates. The two elders, who have been hiding, emerge and confront her with a devastating ultimatum: submit to their sexual demands, or they will publicly testify that they caught her in adultery with a young man -- a charge carrying the death penalty.

**Susanna's Choice:** Facing an impossible situation, Susanna makes a courageous moral decision. She declares: "I choose not to do it; I will fall into your hands, rather than sin in the sight of the Lord." She cries out, and the elders cry out against her.

**The Trial and Condemnation:** The elders present their fabricated testimony before the assembly. Because of their status as respected judges, the community believes them without question, and Susanna is condemned to death. As she is led to execution, she prays to God, declaring her innocence.

**Daniel's Intervention:** God stirs "the holy spirit of a young lad named Daniel," who cries out that he will not be party to the shedding of innocent blood. He demands a retrial and separates the two elders for cross-examination. He asks each one a simple but devastating question: Under what tree did you see Susanna with her alleged lover?

**The Unmasking:** The first elder says "under a mastic tree" (Greek: *schinos*); the second says "under an evergreen oak" (Greek: *prinos*). Their contradictory testimony exposes the lie. In the Greek text, Daniel's responses contain sharp wordplays -- the angel will "split" (*schizein*) the first elder as the mastic (*schinos*) splits, and "saw" (*prizein*) the second as the oak (*prinos*) is sawn -- linking each tree to the punishment awaiting the liar who named it.

**Justice Restored:** The assembly turns on the elders. Condemned by their own contradictions, the two men are executed according to the law of Moses -- receiving the punishment they had intended for their victim. Susanna is vindicated, her family rejoices, and "from that day onward Daniel had a great reputation among the people."

### 3. Bel and the Dragon (Daniel 14 in Catholic Bibles)

This addition consists of two satirical episodes and a dramatic conclusion, all serving as polemic against idolatry.

**Episode One -- The Priests of Bel (vv. 1--22):** King Cyrus of Persia asks Daniel why he does not worship Bel (the Babylonian deity Marduk), whose magnificent idol consumes vast daily offerings: twelve great measures of fine flour, forty sheep, and six vessels of wine. Daniel laughs and replies that he worships only the living God -- this idol is merely clay covered with bronze and "never ate or drank anything." The king, offended, summons Bel's seventy priests and issues a challenge: the offerings will be set out, the temple sealed with the royal signet ring, and if Bel has not consumed everything by morning, the priests will die; if the food is gone, Daniel dies.

The priests are confident, for they have a secret: a hidden passage beneath the temple through which they, their wives, and their children enter each night to feast on the offerings. But Daniel has a trick of his own. After the priests leave, he orders his servants to scatter fine ashes across the entire temple floor. The next morning, the seals are intact and the food is gone -- but the floor is covered with footprints leading to the secret entrance. Confronted with the evidence, the priests confess. They, their wives, and children are put to death. Daniel destroys the idol and the temple.

**Episode Two -- The Dragon (vv. 23--27):** The Babylonians also worship a great living dragon (or serpent), which they revere as a god because, unlike the idol Bel, it is a living creature that eats and drinks. The king challenges Daniel: "You cannot say that this is not a living god; so worship him." Daniel replies that he will worship only the Lord God and asks permission to slay the dragon without sword or club. He mixes a concoction of pitch, fat, and hair, boils it into cakes, and feeds them to the dragon. The creature swallows them and bursts open, proving it to be an ordinary -- if large -- animal, not a god. "See what you have been worshiping!" Daniel exclaims.

**The Lion's Den (vv. 28--42):** The Babylonians are furious that Daniel has destroyed both their idol and their sacred dragon. They demand that the king hand Daniel over or face a revolt: "The king has become a Jew!" Cyrus, fearing for his throne, reluctantly surrenders Daniel to the mob, who cast him into a den with seven hungry lions. For six days Daniel sits among the lions, who do not touch him.

Meanwhile, in Judea, the prophet Habakkuk has just prepared a pot of stew and bread for field workers. An angel of the Lord seizes Habakkuk by the hair of his head and carries him through the air to Babylon, setting him down above the lion's den. "Daniel, Daniel!" Habakkuk calls. "Take the lunch that God has sent you." Daniel eats and gives thanks. Habakkuk is immediately returned to Judea. On the seventh day, the king comes to mourn Daniel but finds him alive. He cries out: "You are great, O Lord, the God of Daniel, and there is no other besides you!" Daniel is released, and those who had conspired against him are thrown into the den, where the lions devour them instantly.

## Key Themes

Though composed independently and differing in genre, the three additions share a constellation of interconnected theological and literary themes:

### Divine Deliverance of the Faithful
All three stories culminate in God's rescue of the innocent. The three young men are shielded from the flames by an angel. Susanna is saved from a death sentence by God's stirring of Daniel's spirit. Daniel survives the lion's den through divine protection and miraculous provision. The message is consistent: God does not abandon those who remain faithful, even when all human hope is exhausted.

### The Sovereignty of God Over Pagan Powers
Each addition asserts the supremacy of the God of Israel over the religious, political, and judicial systems of the surrounding empires. Babylonian and Persian gods are exposed as frauds. Corrupt judges are overthrown. Foreign kings are brought to acknowledge the God of Daniel. This theme would have been profoundly encouraging to Jewish communities living under Hellenistic and Roman rule.

### Justice and the Vindication of the Innocent
Susanna presents the most developed exploration of justice, dramatizing how institutional authority can be weaponized against the powerless. But even in Bel and the Dragon, justice is a central concern: the deceitful priests are punished, and Daniel's accusers meet the fate they intended for him. The additions affirm that divine justice ultimately corrects the failures of human justice.

### The Futility of Idolatry
Bel and the Dragon is an extended satire on idol worship, but the theme runs through all three additions. The Prayer of Azariah implicitly contrasts true worship (prayer to the living God) with the forced worship of Nebuchadnezzar's golden image. Susanna's faithfulness to the law of Moses stands against the moral corruption of elders who have abandoned their religious duties. The overarching message is that idolatry -- whether of statues, dragons, or human authority -- is both foolish and dangerous.

### Courage and Moral Integrity Under Pressure
Susanna's refusal to submit to the elders, the three young men's refusal to worship the golden image, and Daniel's refusal to worship Bel or the Dragon all present models of moral courage. Each protagonist faces death as the consequence of faithfulness and chooses integrity over survival. These are stories for communities under pressure to compromise.

### The Wisdom of the Young
Daniel appears in two of the three additions as a young man whose divinely inspired wisdom surpasses that of his elders and social superiors. In Susanna, he is explicitly described as a "young lad" who outwits corrupt judges. In Bel and the Dragon, he uses rational investigation to defeat the schemes of seventy priests. This theme inverts social hierarchies and suggests that God's wisdom operates outside conventional channels of authority.

### Creation Praising the Creator
The Song of the Three Young Men presents a theology of creation as inherently worshipful. Every element of the natural world -- from angels to whales, from frost to fire -- participates in an unbroken chorus of praise. This cosmic liturgy suggests that faithfulness to God is not merely a human obligation but the fundamental orientation of all existence.

## Influence & Legacy

### Liturgical Impact

The Song of the Three Young Men has had an outsized impact on Christian worship. Known in Latin as the *Benedicite, omnia opera Domini* ("Bless the Lord, all you works of the Lord"), it became one of the most widely used canticles in Western Christianity. Jerome included it in the Vulgate in the late 4th century, and it entered the liturgies of virtually every major Christian tradition:

- **Anglican**: Included in the Book of Common Prayer from 1549 onward as an alternative to the Te Deum at Morning Prayer -- a remarkable status for a text that Anglicanism officially classifies as non-canonical.
- **Roman Catholic**: Incorporated into the Liturgy of the Hours and sung during the Lauds of Sunday.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: Forms the basis of the seventh and eighth odes in the Canon of Biblical Odes during Orthros (Matins). The full passage is chanted during the Vespers of Holy Saturday, where it prefigures Christ's Resurrection -- the three young men's deliverance from the furnace being read as a type of Christ's victory over death.

The Prayer of Azariah similarly influenced penitential liturgies, providing a model for communal confession that acknowledges both the justice of divine punishment and the hope of divine mercy.

### Susanna in Art

Few biblical narratives have generated as much visual art as the story of Susanna and the Elders. The subject has been depicted continuously from the earliest Christian art to the present day, making it one of the most enduring subjects in Western visual culture.

**Early Christian Art (3rd--5th centuries):** Susanna appears in catacomb paintings and on early Christian sarcophagi, typically depicted fully clothed as a symbol of faith, virtue, and marital chastity. She was understood as a figure of the Church persecuted by false accusers.

**Renaissance and Baroque (15th--17th centuries):** As interest in the nude revived, artists increasingly depicted the bathing scene, making Susanna one of the few female figures in the biblical canon shown unclothed. Major works include paintings by:
- **Lorenzo Lotto** (1517)
- **Tintoretto** (1555--1556), whose dramatic composition bridges Mannerism and the Baroque
- **Peter Paul Rubens** (multiple versions), emphasizing the sensuality of the scene
- **Rembrandt van Rijn** (1636), who brought psychological depth to Susanna's vulnerability
- **Guido Reni**, **Guercino**, and many others

**Artemisia Gentileschi's Revolution (1610):** The most critically significant treatment is arguably Artemisia Gentileschi's *Susanna and the Elders* (1610), painted when the artist was only seventeen. Gentileschi departed radically from the tradition of depicting Susanna as coy, passive, or complicit in the voyeuristic scene. Her Susanna twists away from the elders in visible distress and revulsion. Art historians have connected this unflinching depiction to Gentileschi's own experience of sexual assault, and the painting is now considered a landmark in the history of women's art and feminist reinterpretation of biblical narrative.

**Modern and Contemporary Art:** The story continues to inspire artists exploring themes of gender, power, surveillance, and the male gaze. Contemporary feminist artists have revisited the subject to interrogate centuries of artistic tradition that aestheticized Susanna's violation.

### Literary Influence

**Bel and the Dragon as Proto-Detective Fiction:** Literary scholars have noted that the story of Daniel and the priests of Bel contains the first known example of a "locked room" mystery. Daniel's method -- sealing the room, scattering ashes to reveal hidden footprints, and logically deducing the truth -- anticipates the techniques of detective fiction by nearly two millennia. Similarly, Daniel's cross-examination of the elders in Susanna employs the classic detective technique of separating witnesses and exposing contradictions in their testimony.

**Influence on Later Literature:** The stories of Susanna and of Daniel in the lion's den have been retold, adapted, and alluded to in countless literary works. The motif of the falsely accused woman vindicated by divine intervention became a staple of medieval romance and hagiography. Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice* (with Portia's courtroom intervention) and *Measure for Measure* (with its themes of corrupt authority and sexual coercion) have been compared to the Susanna narrative.

### Theological Legacy

The Additions to Daniel contributed significantly to the development of several theological ideas:

- **Substitutionary Prayer:** Azariah's prayer, offered on behalf of the entire nation from within the furnace, provided an influential model for intercessory and penitential prayer, particularly the idea that a contrite heart can substitute for temple sacrifice.
- **Anti-Idolatry Polemic:** Bel and the Dragon's satirical approach to pagan religion influenced later Jewish and Christian apologetic literature, including the mockery of idol worship found in the Church Fathers.
- **Typology:** The deliverance of the three young men from the furnace became one of the most important Old Testament "types" in Christian theology -- a prefiguration of the Resurrection. This reading shaped Christian art, liturgy, and homiletics for centuries.
- **Habakkuk Tradition:** Bel and the Dragon's portrayal of Habakkuk being carried through the air by an angel enriched the prophetic tradition surrounding this figure and influenced later Jewish and Christian legends about miraculous transport.

### Cultural Legacy

The story of Susanna has taken on renewed significance in modern discussions of sexual harassment, the credibility of accusers versus the powerful, and the failures of institutional justice. The #MeToo movement brought fresh attention to the narrative as one of the oldest literary depictions of a woman's word being dismissed in favor of powerful men's testimony -- and of eventual vindication.

They gave the world one of its greatest hymns of creation, one of its oldest courtroom dramas, and one of its first detective stories.*
