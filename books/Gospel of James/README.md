# The Gospel of James (Protoevangelium of James)

**The most influential non-canonical text about the Virgin Mary, shaping centuries of Christian doctrine, art, and liturgy.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Protoevangelium of James was composed in Greek, most likely in the second half of the second century CE (circa 145-170 AD). The text was already known to Clement of Alexandria by the late second century and was explicitly cited by Origen in the early third century, providing a firm upper bound for its date.

The earliest surviving manuscript is **Papyrus Bodmer V** (P.Bodm. V), a late-third or early-fourth century papyrus discovered in 1952 as part of the Bodmer Papyri collection in Egypt. This manuscript bears the unusual double title "Birth of Mary. Revelation of James," a designation found nowhere else in the tradition. The second part of this title -- "Revelation of James" -- is almost certainly a scribal error or later addition.

Beyond Papyrus Bodmer V, the manuscript tradition for this text is extraordinarily rich: over 100 Greek manuscripts survive, spanning from late antiquity through the medieval period. The text was translated into Syriac, Ethiopic, Sahidic Coptic, Georgian, Old Church Slavonic, Armenian, and Arabic. A Latin translation is also presumed to have circulated, given that the text was known to the compilers of the Gelasian Decree (circa 500 AD).

The name "Protoevangelium" (Proto-Gospel) was coined in 1552 by the French scholar Guillaume Postel, who published the first printed edition in Basel, Switzerland. Postel believed -- incorrectly -- that the work predated the canonical Gospels, hence the prefix "proto-" (first). Despite being a misnomer, the title stuck and remains the text's most common scholarly designation.

## Historical Context

The Protoevangelium of James emerges from a period of intense theological creativity in early Christianity, when communities were actively expanding upon the sparse details found in the canonical infancy narratives of Matthew and Luke. The canonical Gospels say almost nothing about Mary's own origins. Matthew begins with Joseph's perspective and a genealogy tracing Jesus's lineage through David; Luke offers the Annunciation and the Magnificat but nothing about Mary's childhood. The Protoevangelium fills this silence with an entire biography of Mary, from her miraculous conception to the birth of Jesus.

The text draws heavily on Old Testament patterns of barrenness and divine intervention -- the stories of Sarah and Abraham, Hannah and Samuel -- and reshapes them around the figure of Mary. Her parents, Joachim and Anna, are cast as a righteous couple suffering the social disgrace of childlessness, echoing the Hannah narrative from 1 Samuel almost beat for beat.

This literary project reflects the earliest stirrings of what would become Marian devotion. By the mid-second century, some Christian communities were already beginning to venerate Mary not simply as the mother of Jesus, but as a uniquely holy figure in her own right. The Protoevangelium is the earliest surviving document to articulate this veneration systematically, presenting Mary as sacred from before her birth, set apart for God's purposes, and perpetually virgin. It represents the foundation stone of Mariology -- the theological study of Mary that would become central to both Catholic and Orthodox Christianity.

The text also engages with contemporary theological controversies. Its vivid, physical descriptions of Mary's pregnancy and the midwife Salome's examination of her after the birth appear designed to counter Docetism -- the belief that Jesus only appeared to have a physical body. By insisting on both the reality of the birth and the preservation of Mary's virginity during it, the author stakes out a distinctive middle position: the incarnation was real, but the birth was miraculous.

## Canonical Status

The Protoevangelium of James was never included in the New Testament canon, and its exclusion was made explicit on multiple occasions by Western church authorities. Pope Innocent I condemned it in 405 AD, and the Gelasian Decree (circa 500 AD) listed it among texts "which are to be avoided by catholics."

Jerome, the great translator of the Latin Vulgate, was particularly hostile to the text. His opposition significantly diminished its circulation and influence in the Western, Latin-speaking church for centuries. Jerome preferred to explain the "brothers of Jesus" mentioned in the canonical Gospels as cousins rather than accepting the Protoevangelium's solution -- that they were Joseph's children from a prior marriage.

In the Christian East, however, the reception was dramatically different. The Eastern Orthodox Church embraced the Protoevangelium's narratives enthusiastically. Its explanation that the adelphoi (brothers) of Jesus were Joseph's sons from a previous marriage became and remains the standard Orthodox position. The text was read during Byzantine liturgical services on Marian feast days, and its stories became deeply woven into Orthodox devotional life and iconographic tradition.

The result is one of the great paradoxes of Christian literary history: a text formally rejected by institutional authorities in both East and West nonetheless shaped the faith, art, and worship of virtually all Christians for nearly two thousand years. Its stories are embedded so deeply in the tradition that most believers who know them have no idea they originate from a non-canonical source.

## Summary

The Protoevangelium of James unfolds in 25 chapters, divided roughly into three acts.

### Act I: The Birth and Childhood of Mary (Chapters 1-8)

The story opens with **Joachim**, a wealthy and devout Israelite, who is publicly humiliated at the Temple when a man named Rubim turns him away from offering sacrifice, declaring that childless men are unworthy. Devastated, Joachim retreats into the wilderness to fast and pray for forty days and forty nights.

Meanwhile, his wife **Anna** mourns doubly -- both her barrenness and her husband's absence. In a poignant scene, she puts on her wedding garments, goes into her garden, sits beneath a laurel tree, and pours out a lament to God, noting that even the birds and beasts and earth itself are fruitful, while she alone remains barren.

An angel appears to Anna and promises her a child who "will be spoken of throughout the whole world." The same angel visits Joachim in the wilderness with the same promise. The couple reunites at the Golden Gate of Jerusalem, and Anna conceives. When the child is born, Anna asks the midwife, "What is it?" and upon hearing "A girl!" exclaims, "My soul exalts this day!"

The child is named **Mariam** (Mary). Anna keeps her in a sanctuary within the household, allowing nothing profane to touch her. At six months, Mary takes her first steps -- seven of them -- and Anna declares that she will not walk on common ground again until she is presented at the Temple.

### Act II: Mary in the Temple and Her Betrothal (Chapters 7-16)

At the age of three, Mary is brought to the Temple in Jerusalem in a solemn procession. The priest receives her, blesses her, and places her on the third step of the altar, where "the Lord God put grace upon her and she danced with her feet, and the whole house of Israel loved her." Mary lives in the Temple, "nurtured like a dove and received food from the hand of an angel."

When Mary reaches twelve, the priests grow anxious: her menstruation will soon make the Temple ritually impure. The high priest prays for guidance, and an angel instructs him to assemble all the widowers of Israel, each bringing a rod. God will provide a sign to reveal who should become Mary's guardian. The rods are collected, and when **Joseph** -- described as an elderly widower with sons from a previous marriage -- receives his rod back, a dove flies out of it and lands on his head. Joseph is reluctant. "I have children, and I am an old man, and she is a young girl," he protests, fearing he will be laughed at. But the priest warns him not to resist God's will, and Joseph takes Mary into his home.

Mary is then assigned, along with other Temple virgins, to weave a new veil for the Temple. By lot, she receives the true purple and scarlet threads -- colors of royalty and sacrifice. While spinning the scarlet thread, the angel Gabriel appears to her with the Annunciation: "You will conceive from his word." Mary, confused, asks, "Shall I conceive as every woman conceives?" The angel replies, "Not so, Mary... the power of the Lord will overshadow you."

Mary visits her kinswoman Elizabeth, who is pregnant with John the Baptist. Upon returning home, Mary is now visibly pregnant, and Joseph is horrified. A priest discovers the pregnancy and hauls both Joseph and Mary before the Temple authorities. Both are subjected to the **Trial of Bitter Waters** -- the ancient Israelite ordeal for suspected adultery described in Numbers 5 -- and both emerge unharmed, vindicating their innocence.

### Act III: The Nativity and Its Aftermath (Chapters 17-25)

The decree of Augustus sends Joseph and Mary toward Bethlehem. Along the way, Mary laughs and then weeps, and tells Joseph she sees two peoples before her -- one weeping and one rejoicing (a vision interpreted as Israel and the Gentiles). Labor begins before they reach the town, and Joseph settles Mary in a **cave** -- not the stable or inn of the canonical narratives.

Joseph rushes out to find a midwife. What follows is one of the text's most remarkable passages: as Joseph walks, he describes time itself standing still. Birds freeze in mid-flight, workers pause with their hands raised, sheep stop where they stand, a shepherd's hand hangs motionless in the air, and the waters of a stream cease flowing. Then, just as suddenly, everything resumes. This moment of cosmic suspension marks the birth of Jesus.

Joseph returns with a midwife, but a bright cloud overshadows the cave, then transforms into an unbearable light. When the light recedes, the infant Jesus appears at Mary's breast. The midwife departs in awe, declaring, "A virgin has given birth -- a thing which nature does not allow!" She encounters **Salome**, who refuses to believe without physical proof. Salome examines Mary and finds her virginity intact, but her hand is consumed by fire as punishment for her doubt. Repentant, she prays for forgiveness, and an angel instructs her to touch the infant Christ -- upon doing so, her hand is healed.

The narrative then follows elements familiar from Matthew's Gospel: the Magi arrive, guided by a star; Herod interrogates them about the newborn king; and when the Magi do not return, Herod orders the **Massacre of the Innocents**. Elizabeth flees with the infant John the Baptist, and when soldiers pursue her, a mountain miraculously opens to shelter them. Herod, enraged at losing John, sends soldiers to the Temple to interrogate John's father, the priest **Zechariah**. When Zechariah refuses to reveal his son's location, he is murdered at the altar. The other priests discover his blood congealed beside the altar and hear a voice declaring, "Zechariah has been murdered, and his blood will not be wiped away until his avenger comes."

The text closes with the author identifying himself: "I, James, wrote this history in Jerusalem, when there arose a tumult when Herod died."

## Key Themes

### Mary's Perpetual Virginity

The Protoevangelium is the earliest surviving text to assert Mary's perpetual virginity in its threefold sense: *ante partum* (before birth -- the virgin conception), *in partu* (during birth -- the miraculous delivery that preserved her physical virginity), and *post partum* (after birth -- she never had sexual relations). The Salome episode functions as a dramatic, narrative "proof" of the *virginitas in partu*, while the characterization of Joseph as an elderly widower eliminates any expectation of a normal marital relationship.

### The Sacred Childhood

Mary is presented as sacred from the moment of her conception. Anna creates a "sanctuary" in her bedroom; Mary's feet do not touch common ground; she is fed by angels in the Temple. This theme of radical purity and separation foreshadows the later Catholic doctrine of the **Immaculate Conception** -- that Mary was conceived without original sin -- though the Protoevangelium does not articulate the doctrine in those terms.

### The Midwife Tradition

The episode with Salome introduces the figure of the doubting witness who is punished and then healed through contact with the Christ Child. This narrative pattern -- doubt, physical punishment, repentance, miraculous healing -- deliberately echoes the story of Thomas in John 20 and serves to validate the extraordinary claims about the birth. The midwife tradition also grounds the miraculous event in bodily, physical reality, countering Docetist claims that the incarnation was only an illusion.

### Davidic Lineage and Temple Symbolism

Mary is identified as belonging to the tribe of David, and her assignment to weave the Temple veil with purple and scarlet thread -- the colors of royalty and of the curtain that separates the Holy of Holies -- symbolically links her to both the Davidic kingship and the Temple itself. The veil she weaves is the same veil that the canonical Gospels report was torn at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:51), creating a profound symbolic arc from Mary's hands to the cross.

### The Trial of Bitter Waters

By subjecting both Mary and Joseph to the ordeal of Numbers 5:11-31 and having them pass unscathed, the text addresses the scandal of an unmarried pregnant girl within the framework of Jewish law. God himself vindicates their innocence through a recognized legal mechanism, lending divine authority to the virgin birth narrative.

### Cosmic Nativity

The scene in which time stands still -- birds frozen, water halted, the entire created order holding its breath -- elevates the nativity from a local, human event to a cosmic one. The birth of Jesus is presented as a rupture in the fabric of reality itself, a moment when heaven breaks into the ordinary world.

## Influence & Legacy

Few non-canonical texts have left a deeper mark on Christianity than the Protoevangelium of James. Its influence is so pervasive that many Christians accept its stories as biblical without ever realizing their true source.

### Marian Doctrine

The Protoevangelium laid the groundwork for virtually every major Marian doctrine that developed in subsequent centuries. Its portrait of Mary as perpetually virgin became dogmatic teaching in both the Catholic and Orthodox traditions. Its depiction of her sacred childhood contributed to the later development of the doctrines of the Immaculate Conception (defined as Catholic dogma in 1854) and Mary's sinlessness. The names of Mary's parents -- **Joachim and Anna** (Anne) -- come exclusively from this text and are now venerated as saints in both traditions, with their own feast days.

### Liturgical Feasts

At least four major Marian feasts in the Christian liturgical calendar derive directly from the Protoevangelium's narratives:

- **The Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary** (September 8) -- celebrating Mary's birth to Joachim and Anna
- **The Presentation of Mary in the Temple** (November 21) -- commemorating the three-year-old Mary's entry into the Temple
- **The Feast of Saints Joachim and Anne** (July 26 in the Western calendar) -- honoring Mary's parents
- **The Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary** (December 8/9) -- rooted in Anna's miraculous conception

In the Byzantine liturgical tradition, the Protoevangelium was read aloud during services on these feast days, making it functionally scriptural in worship even as it remained formally non-canonical.

### Christian Art

The Protoevangelium has been one of the most fertile sources for Christian visual art across nearly two millennia. Scenes originating from this text became staples of Christian iconography and painting:

- **The Meeting at the Golden Gate** -- Joachim and Anna's reunion, one of the most depicted scenes in medieval Western art (notably by Giotto in the Arena Chapel, Padua)
- **The Presentation of Mary in the Temple** -- the young Mary ascending the Temple steps, a beloved subject in both Eastern icons and Western painting (Titian's monumental version in the Accademia, Venice)
- **The Annunciation at the Well / with the Purple Thread** -- Eastern iconographic traditions show Mary spinning or drawing water when Gabriel appears, details drawn from the Protoevangelium rather than Luke
- **The Nativity in the Cave** -- the cave setting for Jesus's birth, standard in Eastern Christian iconography, comes from this text, not the canonical Gospels
- **The Midwife and Salome** -- these figures appear in early nativity scenes in catacomb paintings and on sarcophagi

The ox and donkey that appear in virtually every nativity scene across all Christian traditions also reflect the broader apocryphal infancy tradition that the Protoevangelium helped establish.

### Theological Legacy

Beyond specific doctrines, the Protoevangelium established the basic narrative template for how Christians imagine the "backstory" of the New Testament. The idea that Mary had a miraculous childhood, that Joseph was much older than Mary, that the birth took place in a cave, that midwives were present -- all of these deeply embedded cultural assumptions trace back to this single second-century text. It is, in a real sense, the origin of Christmas as Christians have imagined it for most of history.
