# Narrative / Storytelling Style

This is a novelistic adaptation of Wisdom of Solomon Chapter 1, reimagined as vivid, immersive prose. Solomon addresses the rulers of the world directly, and the scene is brought to life with atmospheric detail and character interiority. Verse references are preserved in brackets for cross-referencing against the KJV source text.

---

The throne room had emptied hours ago. The last of the foreign delegations had filed out through the cedar doors, their sandals whispering across the polished stone, their voices trailing off into the corridors of the palace. But Solomon had not moved. He sat forward on the great throne, his fingers steepled beneath his chin, his gaze fixed on something none of his attendants could see.

The oil lamps guttered low. Shadows pooled in the corners of the hall and climbed the pillars like living things. Outside, Jerusalem slept beneath a field of stars so vast and close they seemed to press against the earth, and the night air carried the scent of cedar smoke and jasmine through the open colonnades.

Solomon closed his eyes. He had spent the day listening to kings and governors, men who ruled provinces and commanded armies, men who believed their authority was their own. He had watched them posture and bargain, had seen the casual cruelty behind their diplomacy, the hunger behind their smiles. And something in him had broken open -- not with anger, but with an urgency he could no longer contain.

He rose from the throne.

"Bring me parchment," he said to the scribe who dozed in the shadows. "And light the lamps again. All of them."

When the room blazed with light once more, Solomon began to speak, and the scribe's reed moved across the parchment like a thing possessed.

---

"Listen to me," Solomon said, and his voice carried a weight that made the scribe look up. "I am speaking now to every man and woman who holds the power of judgment over others -- every ruler, every magistrate, every sovereign who sits where I sit." He paused, letting the silence gather. "Love righteousness. That is where it begins. Not policy. Not strategy. Not the shrewd calculus of power. Love what is right the way you love water when you are dying of thirst." [1]

He stepped down from the dais and began to pace, his robes trailing behind him. "Think of the Lord," he continued, "but think of Him rightly -- with a heart that is good and uncomplicated. Seek Him with simplicity. Not with the mind of a merchant calculating advantage, but with the openness of a child who has not yet learned to be cynical." [1]

He stopped at one of the great windows and looked out into the night. The stars burned above the Kidron Valley, indifferent and eternal.

"He will be found," Solomon said quietly, almost to himself. "That is the extraordinary thing. He does not hide from those who come to Him honestly. But you cannot test Him. You cannot approach Him the way you would approach a rival king, probing for weakness, laying traps. Come to Him without suspicion, without distrust, and He will show Himself to you as plainly as the dawn." [2]

He turned back to the scribe. "Write this carefully. Crooked thoughts -- scheming, duplicity, the habit of seeing every interaction as a contest to be won -- these things create a distance from God that no ritual can bridge. And when a man tries to put God's power to the test, treating it as a tool to be evaluated, that power turns back on him like a mirror, and what he sees there is his own foolishness laid bare." [3]

Solomon's expression darkened. He had known men like this -- brilliant men, powerful men, men whose souls had curdled with ambition until there was no room left for anything clean. "Wisdom will not enter a soul that has given itself over to malice," he said. "You cannot invite her in while keeping your sins like pets in the back rooms of your heart. She will not dwell in a body that has made itself a house for wrongdoing. The two cannot coexist. It is like trying to hold fire and water in the same hand." [4]

He sat down on the edge of the dais, suddenly weary, and when he spoke again his voice was gentler, as if he were explaining something to someone he loved. "The holy spirit of discipline -- and I call her holy because she is set apart, she is unlike anything else in creation -- she will flee from deceit the way a wild bird flees from a cage. She removes herself from minds cluttered with foolishness, from thoughts that have no foundation in understanding. And when unrighteousness walks through the door, she walks out. She will not stay. She cannot." [5]

Solomon leaned forward, his hands on his knees. "Understand this about wisdom: she is a loving spirit. There is nothing cold or indifferent about her. But precisely because she loves, she will not excuse blasphemy. She will not smile and look away when a man speaks against what is sacred. Because God Himself is watching -- not from a great distance, not abstractly, but intimately. He is a witness to the deepest currents of a man's heart, to the secret motions of his innermost being. He sees every thought before it becomes a word. He hears every word before it leaves the tongue." [6]

The scribe's hand trembled slightly, and Solomon noticed. He softened his voice but not his meaning.

"The Spirit of the Lord fills the world," he said. "Not a corner of it, not a kingdom of it -- the whole of it. That which holds all things together, that vast and patient intelligence that sustains the stars in their courses and the blood in your veins, it has knowledge of every voice. Every whisper. Every muttered curse spoken in the dark when a man believes himself alone." [7]

He stood again and walked to the center of the room, where the lamplight was strongest, and his shadow stretched in every direction like a sundial at noon. "So let no one imagine that unrighteous speech goes unheard. Let no one comfort himself with the thought that his words vanish into silence. They do not. And when justice comes -- and it will come, as certainly as morning follows night -- it will not pass him by. It will find him. It will know his name." [8]

"There will be an accounting," Solomon continued, and now there was something almost sorrowful in his voice. "Every secret counsel of the ungodly, every whispered conspiracy, every plan hatched in the belief that God was not listening -- all of it will be laid open. The sound of those words, which the speaker thought died in the air, will reach the Lord, and they will serve as testimony. His own words will condemn him. His own voice will be the evidence." [9]

He paused and looked directly at the scribe, as if the man were a stand-in for all the rulers of the earth. "The ear of jealousy hears everything. And I do not mean jealousy as you understand it among men -- petty, insecure, grasping. I mean the fierce and holy possessiveness of a God who made you and will not share you with the lie you have chosen instead of Him. That ear misses nothing. Even the noise of murmuring -- the low, corrosive grumbling that men do when they resent God's ways but lack the courage to say so openly -- even that is heard." [10]

Solomon's voice dropped to barely above a whisper, but in the silence of the throne room it carried to every wall. "So guard your tongue. Beware of murmuring -- it profits no one and poisons everything it touches. Refrain from backbiting, from the cowardly art of speaking against others when they cannot answer. Because there is no word so secret that it will come to nothing. Every syllable has weight. Every utterance has consequence. And the mouth that speaks lies -- the mouth that distorts truth to serve its own purposes -- that mouth is not merely reckless. It kills the soul. Not someone else's soul. Its own." [11]

He walked back to the window. The night had deepened, and the stars seemed closer still, as though the heavens were leaning in to listen.

"Do not seek death," Solomon said, and his voice was urgent now, almost pleading. "Do not wander into it through the accumulated errors of a careless life, as a man might wander into a swamp because he refused to watch the path. Do not pull destruction down upon yourselves with the work of your own hands, brick by brick, choice by choice, until the edifice of your ruin is complete and you stand inside it wondering how you got there." [12]

He turned from the window, and the lamplight caught his face, and the scribe saw something there -- a vast and tender grief.

"God did not make death," Solomon said. "Write that down. Underline it. Let it burn into the minds of everyone who reads this. God did not make death. He takes no pleasure in the destruction of the living. None. The thought of it grieves Him." [13]

"He created all things so that they might exist -- truly exist, fully and abundantly, with the vitality that He Himself possesses. The generations of the world were made whole and healthy. There was no poison woven into the fabric of creation, no hidden venom in the design. The kingdom of death had no dominion over the earth. It was not part of the plan." [14]

Solomon's voice grew quiet, almost reverent. "Righteousness is immortal," he said. And he let the words hang in the air like incense smoke, like a truth too large to need elaboration. [15]

Then his expression hardened.

"But the ungodly," he said, "with their works and with their words, they summoned death to themselves. They called out to it. They beckoned it closer." He shook his head slowly. "They thought it was their friend. Can you imagine? They looked at death -- at the annihilation of everything God intended for them -- and they thought they saw an ally. They thought they saw freedom from consequence, release from accountability, an escape from the demands of righteousness. And so they consumed themselves to nothing. They entered into a covenant with death, a pact, a partnership." [16]

Solomon fell silent. The lamps flickered. Somewhere in the palace, a door closed softly.

"They are worthy of that partnership," he said at last, and his voice was heavy with the weight of it. "Not because God wished it. Not because it was inevitable. But because they chose it, deliberately and with open eyes, and in the end a man becomes worthy of the thing he will not stop choosing." [16]

The scribe set down his reed. His hand ached. The parchment was full.

Solomon stood at the window for a long time after that, watching the stars wheel slowly over Jerusalem, saying nothing, thinking of all the rulers of the earth who would never hear these words -- and of the few who might.
