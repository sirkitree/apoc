# The Wisdom of Solomon

**A first-century Hellenistic Jewish meditation on divine wisdom, righteous suffering, and the destiny of the soul -- written in the voice of King Solomon but shaped by the encounter between Hebrew faith and Greek philosophy in ancient Alexandria.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original Language:** Greek. Unlike most Old Testament books, the Wisdom of Solomon was not translated from Hebrew or Aramaic -- it was composed directly in Koine Greek, the common literary language of the Hellenistic world. Its vocabulary, rhetorical style, and philosophical terminology all point to a Greek-speaking author thoroughly at home in the literary culture of his day.

**Date of Composition:** Most scholars place the book between approximately 100 BCE and 40 CE, with a strong consensus favoring the late first century BCE. The French scholar C. Larcher argued for a more precise window of 31--10 BCE, during the early Roman imperial period. The text shows no awareness of the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple (70 CE), and its engagement with Egyptian religion suggests a date when Alexandrian Judaism was still a vibrant, confident community.

**Place of Composition:** Alexandria, Egypt -- the great cosmopolitan capital of the Ptolemaic and then Roman world, home to the largest Jewish community in the Diaspora and one of the most important centers of learning in antiquity. The city's famous library, its thriving philosophical schools, and its complex interfaith tensions all left their mark on this text.

**Key Manuscripts:**

- **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century CE) -- Generally considered the most accurate witness to the text. Housed in the Vatican Library.
- **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century CE) -- Discovered at St. Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai in the 19th century.
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- An important early witness, now in the British Library.
- **Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus** (5th century CE) -- A palimpsest in Paris, partially preserving the text.
- **Codex Venetus** (8th--9th century CE) -- Along with the cursive manuscript 68, it preserves a textual tradition considered especially close to the original.

The book is transmitted as part of the Septuagint (the Greek Old Testament) and appears in all major Septuagint manuscripts.

## Historical Context

**The World of Alexandrian Judaism:** By the first century BCE, the Jewish community in Alexandria had lived in Egypt for over two centuries. They spoke Greek, read their scriptures in Greek translation, and participated in the intellectual life of one of the ancient world's most sophisticated cities. Yet they also faced real pressures: the allure of pagan culture, the philosophical arguments of Epicureans and Stoics, and occasional outbursts of anti-Jewish hostility.

**Community of Origin:** The author was almost certainly an educated Alexandrian Jew -- someone who had studied both the Torah and Greek rhetoric, who knew his Plato and his Prophets. Though the text is written in the first person as King Solomon, this was understood as a literary convention (pseudepigraphy) even in antiquity. No ancient reader was genuinely fooled. The "Solomonic" voice gave the author authority to speak on wisdom, kingship, and justice.

**Purpose:** The Wisdom of Solomon served multiple audiences simultaneously:

- **For fellow Jews**, especially young people tempted by the pleasures and philosophies of the pagan world, it demonstrated that their ancestral faith was intellectually robust -- that Judaism could hold its own against Greek philosophy and, in fact, surpass it.
- **For skeptics and materialists** (the text specifically engages Epicurean arguments about death and pleasure), it offered a passionate defense of divine justice and the immortality of the righteous soul.
- **For rulers and the powerful**, it presented wisdom as the indispensable quality of just governance -- a theme with deep roots in both Hebrew and Greek political thought.

## Canonical Status

The Wisdom of Solomon occupies a fascinating position in the history of the biblical canon -- accepted by some traditions, respected but set aside by others, and ignored by still others:

- **Roman Catholic Church:** Fully canonical. The book was affirmed at the Council of Rome (382 CE), the Synod of Hippo (393 CE), the Councils of Carthage (397 and 419 CE), the Council of Florence (1442), and definitively at the Council of Trent (1546). It is classified as a "deuterocanonical" book.
- **Eastern Orthodox Churches:** Canonical. The Synod of Jerusalem (1672) explicitly declared the Wisdom of Solomon "a genuine part of Scripture."
- **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church:** Included in the broader Ethiopian biblical canon as a deuterocanonical work.
- **Anglican Communion:** Included in the Apocrypha section of the Bible. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1563) state that such books may be read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not used to establish doctrine -- though individual Anglicans vary widely in the weight they give these texts.
- **Lutheran Churches:** Similarly classified as useful apocryphal reading, not canonical scripture.
- **Most Protestant traditions:** Considered part of the biblical Apocrypha. Not included in the canon, though historically printed in Protestant Bibles (including the original 1611 King James Version) between the Old and New Testaments.
- **Judaism:** Not part of the Jewish canon. Despite its deeply Jewish content, the book was composed in Greek and after the effective closure of the Hebrew canon. It is not found in the Tanakh or in rabbinic tradition.

## Summary

The Wisdom of Solomon contains 19 chapters and is traditionally divided into three major sections, each with its own tone, subject matter, and rhetorical strategy.

### Part I: The Book of Eschatology -- Righteousness, Death, and Immortality (Chapters 1--5)

The book opens with a direct address to "the rulers of the earth," urging them to love righteousness and seek God with sincerity of heart. Wisdom, the author declares, will not enter a deceitful soul or dwell in a body enslaved to sin.

The text then gives voice to the wicked -- those who reason that life is short, death is final, and therefore pleasure is the only rational pursuit. In one of the most memorable passages in all of Jewish literature, the ungodly declare: "Let us lie in wait for the righteous man, because he is inconvenient to us and opposes our actions" (2:12). They resolve to test the righteous person with insult and torture, to see whether God will really protect him.

The author's answer is stunning and theologically revolutionary: the righteous who suffer and die are not abandoned by God. "The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them. In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died... but they are at peace" (3:1--3). Suffering is a form of divine testing, like gold refined in a furnace. The wicked, by contrast, will face a terrible judgment. In the end, the righteous will "stand with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed them" (5:1).

This section represents one of the clearest expressions of belief in the immortality of the soul in pre-Christian Jewish literature.

### Part II: The Book of Wisdom -- The Nature and Pursuit of Sophia (Chapters 6--9)

The second section shifts from eschatology to philosophy. Speaking as Solomon, the author describes Wisdom (Sophia) as a radiant, immortal, all-pervading spirit -- "a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty" (7:25). She is "a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness" (7:26).

Solomon recounts how he loved Wisdom above wealth, health, beauty, and even light itself. He describes her twenty-one attributes (7:22--23) -- she is intelligent, holy, unique, subtle, mobile, clear, unpolluted, distinct, invulnerable, loving the good, keen, irresistible, beneficent, humane, steadfast, sure, free from anxiety, all-powerful, overseeing all, and penetrating through all spirits.

Wisdom is not merely an abstract concept here: she is personified as a divine figure, almost a hypostasis of God himself, who sits beside God's throne and was present at the creation of the world. Solomon sought her as a bride and prayed to God for her coming.

Chapter 9 contains Solomon's great prayer for wisdom -- one of the most beautiful prayers in the biblical tradition. He acknowledges human frailty ("a perishable body weighs down the soul, and this earthy tent burdens the anxious mind," 9:15) and asks God to send Wisdom from the holy heavens to labor at his side.

### Part III: The Book of History -- Wisdom's Role in the Story of Israel (Chapters 10--19)

The final and longest section of the book retells the history of Israel from Adam through the Exodus, showing how Wisdom guided and delivered the righteous at every stage. Adam, Noah, Abraham, Lot, Jacob, Joseph, and Moses all appear -- though often without being named, referred to simply as "the righteous one."

The retelling of the Exodus occupies the bulk of this section (chapters 11--19) and takes an unusual form: the author constructs a series of seven elaborate contrasts (synkriseis) between God's treatment of the Israelites and his treatment of the Egyptians. Where the Egyptians were punished through the Nile turned to blood, the Israelites received water from a rock. Where the Egyptians were plagued by frogs and insects, the Israelites received quail from heaven. The same elements of creation that brought destruction to the oppressors brought salvation to the oppressed.

Embedded within this historical review is a remarkable digression on idolatry (chapters 13--15), which analyzes the origins and consequences of idol worship. The author identifies three forms: nature worship (forgivable, if foolish), the worship of handmade images (absurd and self-defeating), and the worship of animals (the worst of all -- a pointed critique of Egyptian religion). This passage deeply influenced Paul's argument in Romans 1:18--32.

The book closes with a triumphant declaration of God's faithfulness: "For in everything, O Lord, you have exalted and glorified your people, and you have not neglected to help them at all times and in all places" (19:22).

## Key Themes

### The Immortality of the Soul
The Wisdom of Solomon offers one of the earliest and most fully developed Jewish arguments for life after death -- not as bodily resurrection (the dominant form in texts like Daniel), but as the survival and reward of the righteous soul. This reflects engagement with Platonic philosophy, yet the author transforms it: immortality is not an inherent property of the soul but a gift of God to the righteous.

### Personified Wisdom (Sophia)
Lady Wisdom dominates the center of the book. She is more than a literary personification -- she functions as a quasi-divine mediator between God and the world, present at creation, active in history, and available to those who seek her. The Sophia tradition here became enormously influential in Christian theology, particularly in the development of Logos Christology.

### Divine Justice and the Problem of Suffering
The book wrestles with why the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper -- the same question that drives Job and Ecclesiastes. Its answer is distinctive: apparent injustice is temporary, and the final judgment will reveal the true state of affairs. This represents a significant development in Jewish theodicy.

### The Synthesis of Hebrew and Greek Thought
The author moves freely between biblical narrative and Platonic philosophy, between prophetic rhetoric and Greek literary forms. He draws on Stoic concepts (the world-pervading spirit), Platonic ideas (the pre-existence of the soul, the body as burden), and Middle Platonic theology (emanation, reflection) -- yet always subordinates them to a thoroughly Jewish monotheism.

### Anti-Idolatry and Natural Theology
The digression on idolatry (chapters 13--15) represents one of the most sophisticated ancient analyses of the psychology and sociology of idol worship. It also contains a remarkable argument from natural theology: those who cannot perceive the Creator from the beauty of creation are "not to be excused" -- language that echoes directly in Paul's letter to the Romans.

### The Four Cardinal Virtues
In 8:7, the author lists temperance, prudence, justice, and fortitude -- the classical Greek cardinal virtues -- as the fruits of Wisdom. This is the only place in the Jewish scriptural tradition where the Platonic four virtues appear explicitly, and it signals the depth of the author's engagement with Greek ethical philosophy.

### God as Creator from Formless Matter
Rather than creation ex nihilo (from nothing), the author speaks of God's "all-powerful hand" creating the world "out of formless matter" (11:17), a concept closer to Plato's Timaeus than to the later Christian doctrine of creation from nothing.

## Influence & Legacy

### On the New Testament
The Wisdom of Solomon left deep marks on early Christian thought, even though many of its readers did not realize the source of the ideas they were absorbing:

- **Paul's Letter to the Romans:** The argument of Romans 1:18--32 -- that humanity's failure to recognize God in creation leads to idolatry, which leads to moral collapse -- closely parallels Wisdom 13--15 in both structure and vocabulary. Scholars have traced extensive verbal and thematic echoes.
- **The Letter to the Hebrews:** The description of Christ as "the reflection of God's glory and the exact imprint of God's very being" (Hebrews 1:3) draws directly on the language used of Wisdom in 7:25--26, where Sophia is called "a reflection of eternal light" and "an image of his goodness." The rare Greek word apaugasma ("radiance" or "reflection") appears in both passages.
- **The Gospel of John:** The Prologue's portrayal of the pre-existent Logos who was with God from the beginning and through whom all things were made builds on the Sophia tradition that the Wisdom of Solomon helped to shape.
- **Colossians 1:15--20:** The description of Christ as "the image of the invisible God" and the agent of creation echoes Wisdom's description of Sophia.

### On Early Christian Theology
The language of the Wisdom of Solomon became foundational for Christological debate. When early Church Fathers like Origen needed vocabulary to describe the eternal relationship between the Father and the Son, they turned to terms first used of Sophia -- emanation, radiance, reflection, image. The Wisdom of Solomon thus helped provide the conceptual and linguistic framework for the doctrine of the Trinity.

### On Philosophy and Interfaith Dialogue
As one of the most successful ancient syntheses of Hebrew theology and Greek philosophy, the Wisdom of Solomon stands as an early model of interfaith intellectual engagement. It demonstrated that rigorous philosophical thinking and deep religious commitment were not mutually exclusive -- a conviction that would become central to both Jewish and Christian intellectual traditions.

### On Literature and Art
The personification of Wisdom as a luminous female figure inspired centuries of artistic and literary representation, from Byzantine icons of Hagia Sophia (Holy Wisdom) to medieval depictions of Lady Philosophy. The great cathedral of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (532--537 CE) takes its name from this tradition. In Western art, Solomon's encounter with Wisdom paralleled his encounter with the Queen of Sheba as subjects for painters and sculptors.

### On Liturgical Tradition
The book has been used extensively in Catholic and Orthodox liturgy. Passages from the Wisdom of Solomon appear in readings for feasts of martyrs (drawing on chapters 3--5, with their vision of righteous suffering rewarded) and in celebrations honoring the Virgin Mary and the saints.
