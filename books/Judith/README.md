# The Book of Judith

**A gripping tale of faith, cunning, and courage in which a pious widow saves her besieged city by beheading an enemy general.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original Language:** Most scholars agree the Book of Judith was originally composed in Hebrew (or possibly Aramaic). The surviving Greek text is filled with Hebraisms in both language and phrasing, strongly indicating translation from a Semitic original. However, no ancient Hebrew or Aramaic manuscript has ever been discovered, despite Jerome's claim in the late 4th century that he translated the Vulgate text from an Aramaic copy.

**Key Manuscripts:**

- **Septuagint (LXX):** The oldest surviving complete texts are preserved in the great uncial codices of the Septuagint, including Codex Vaticanus (B) and Codex Alexandrinus (A), both dating to the 4th-5th centuries CE. Codex Sinaiticus also originally contained Judith. These represent two slightly different Greek recensions of the text.
- **The oldest extant fragment** is a 3rd-century CE potsherd (ostracon) inscribed with Judith 15:1-7.
- **Codex 58** preserves a somewhat corrected and "improved" recension, while Codices 19 and 108 offer a closely related variant text.
- **Jerome's Vulgate** (late 4th century) was translated from an Aramaic manuscript and is notably shorter than the Greek, as Jerome omitted passages he found unclear in his source text.
- **Additional ancient translations** include Old Latin, Syriac (Peshitta), Sahidic (Coptic), and Ethiopic versions, as well as several late medieval Hebrew retellings.

**Date of Composition:** Late 2nd century to early 1st century BCE, most likely during or shortly after the Maccabean period (c. 150-100 BCE).

**Place of Composition:** Palestine. The author demonstrates intimate knowledge of Palestinian geography, Jewish law, and the religious concerns of the period. Based on the text's punctilious regard for Torah observance, the author likely belonged to the Hasidean (pious) party, the forerunners of the Pharisees.

## Historical Context

**Historical Setting of the Narrative:** The book opens with a dramatic anachronism: "It was the twelfth year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar, who ruled over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh" (1:1). The real Nebuchadnezzar was king of Babylon, not Assyria, and Nineveh had already been destroyed before his reign. The text deliberately telescopes multiple historical periods -- the Assyrian threat to Israel, the Babylonian exile, and the Seleucid oppression -- into a single, composite enemy. Most scholars believe these anachronisms are intentional, signaling to the original audience that the story is a parable or historical fiction rather than a chronicle of real events.

Various scholars have proposed that the fictional "Nebuchadnezzar" may be a cipher for Ashurbanipal, Artaxerxes III, Antiochus IV Epiphanes, or other historical rulers. The most popular identification connects the story's background to the Seleucid persecution of the Jews under Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167-164 BCE), which also sparked the Maccabean revolt.

**Community of Origin:** The author was almost certainly a Palestinian Jew writing during or shortly after the Maccabean revolt. Jewish tradition connects Judith with Hanukkah; she appears in liturgical poems (piyyutim) composed for the festival as early as the medieval period. The book's emphasis on Torah observance, dietary laws, prayer, and trust in God over military might reflects the values of the Hasidean movement.

**Purpose:** The Book of Judith was written as an encouragement to Jewish communities facing foreign oppression. Its central message is that God delivers the faithful -- not through vast armies or political alliances, but through the unexpected agency of the weak and marginalized. A single, devout widow accomplishes what no army could. The story serves as both theological argument (trust God, not human power) and resistance literature (the empire can be defeated).

## Canonical Status

The Book of Judith holds different places across the world's religious traditions:

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Roman Catholic** | Deuterocanonical (fully canonical). Accepted at the councils of Rome (382), Hippo (393), Carthage (397), and Florence (1442). Dogmatically defined as canonical at the Council of Trent (1546). |
| **Eastern Orthodox** | Accepted as inspired scripture, confirmed at the Synod of Jerusalem (1672). |
| **Oriental Orthodox** | Included in the Old Testament canon. |
| **Church of the East** | Included in the Old Testament. |
| **Protestant** | Classified as Apocrypha. Martin Luther included it as the first of eight apocryphal writings placed between the Old and New Testaments in his Bible, viewing it as an allegory. Most Protestant traditions regard it as edifying but not authoritative. |
| **Jewish** | Not included in the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). Although widely read in antiquity, it was excluded from the rabbinic canon formalized around the late 1st century CE. However, Jewish tradition preserved it in connection with Hanukkah celebrations. |

## Summary

The Book of Judith is composed of 16 chapters and divides naturally into two halves, each structured with careful literary symmetry.

### Part One: The Assyrian Threat (Chapters 1-7)

The story opens in the court of Nebuchadnezzar, "king of the Assyrians," who has recently defeated the Median king Arphaxad. Flush with victory, Nebuchadnezzar demands that the western nations acknowledge his supremacy. When they refuse, he dispatches his commanding general, Holofernes, with an enormous army of 120,000 infantry and 12,000 cavalry to punish the defiant nations.

Holofernes cuts a devastating path westward, destroying shrines and temples, demanding that all peoples worship Nebuchadnezzar as a god. The nations along the Mediterranean coast capitulate one by one. Only Israel refuses to submit.

The Israelites, recently returned from exile and having rededicated the Temple, prepare for war. They fortify the mountain passes, stockpile food, and pray fervently. When Holofernes learns of this small nation's defiance, he is incredulous. Achior, an Ammonite leader serving in the Assyrian coalition, warns Holofernes about Israel's God: if the people have not sinned, their God will protect them and no army can prevail. Holofernes mocks Achior and expels him, sending him to the Israelite town of Bethulia to share in its coming destruction. The people of Bethulia take Achior in and protect him.

Rather than assault Bethulia's fortified position directly, Holofernes follows the advice of the Edomites and Moabites in his coalition: he seizes control of the town's water supply outside its walls. After 34 days of siege, with cisterns running dry and people fainting in the streets, the townspeople demand surrender. Their leader Uzziah bargains for five more days, vowing that if God does not deliver them by then, he will capitulate.

### Part Two: Judith's Deliverance (Chapters 8-16)

It is only now, at the story's midpoint, that the protagonist appears. Judith ("Jewish woman" -- the name itself is symbolic) is introduced as a wealthy, beautiful, and deeply pious widow. Her husband Manasseh died of heatstroke during the barley harvest, and she has lived in mourning for three years and four months, fasting every day except Sabbaths and festivals, and scrupulously observing the Law.

When Judith hears that Uzziah has given God a five-day deadline, she is outraged. She summons the elders and rebukes them: who are they to put God to the test? God cannot be threatened with ultimatums like a human being. She announces that she has a plan, but refuses to reveal it, asking only that the elders open the city gate for her that night.

Judith prays at length, invoking the story of her ancestor Simeon's vengeance for the rape of Dinah (Genesis 34), and asking God to use her hand to crush the arrogance of the Assyrians. She then removes her widow's garments, bathes and anoints herself, puts on her finest clothing and jewelry, and packs a bag of ritually pure food. Accompanied only by her maid, she walks out through the city gate and into the Assyrian camp.

The Assyrian soldiers are stunned by her beauty. Judith tells Holofernes that she has fled Bethulia because God is about to punish her people for breaking dietary laws, and she will reveal the precise moment when God's protection is withdrawn so that Holofernes can attack without resistance. She claims that each night she must leave the camp to pray and receive God's revelation. Holofernes is delighted by her beauty and intelligence, and grants her free passage in and out of the camp.

For three days Judith follows this routine, leaving each night to pray at a spring outside the camp, living on her own kosher provisions. On the fourth day, Holofernes's steward Bagoas invites her to a private banquet. Judith accepts, dressing in her most alluring finery. She eats only the food her maid has prepared. Holofernes, overwhelmed by desire, drinks himself into a stupor -- "more wine than he had ever drunk on a single day in his life."

When all the servants withdraw and Judith is alone with the unconscious general, she prays twice for strength. Then, taking Holofernes's own sword from the bedpost, she strikes his neck twice and severs his head. She wraps the head in the bed canopy and gives it to her maid, who places it in their food bag. The two women leave the camp as if going out to pray, as was now their established custom -- but this time they keep walking, back up the mountain to Bethulia.

Judith displays the head of Holofernes before the astonished townspeople and proclaims that God has struck down the enemy by her hand, and that no defilement or shame came upon her. Uzziah declares her "blessed by the Most High God above all other women on earth." When Achior the Ammonite sees the head of the general who had expelled him, he converts to Judaism and is circumcised.

At dawn, the Israelites hang the head from their walls and charge down the mountain. The Assyrians rush to Holofernes's tent to rouse their commander and discover his headless corpse. Panic sweeps through the camp, and the leaderless army scatters. The Israelites pursue and rout them. The plunder is enormous; Judith herself is awarded Holofernes's personal effects -- his tent, silver, furniture, and all his possessions.

The high priest Joakim comes from Jerusalem to honor Judith. She leads the women of Israel in a triumphant hymn of thanksgiving (Chapter 16), a song that echoes the victory songs of Miriam and Deborah. Judith dedicates Holofernes's possessions to the Temple. She returns to her estate, frees her maid, and lives the rest of her days as a widow despite many proposals of marriage. She dies at the age of 105. The text concludes: "No one ever again spread terror among the Israelites during the lifetime of Judith, or for a long time after her death."

## Key Themes

### Faith and Divine Sovereignty
At its heart, Judith is a story about trust in God versus trust in human power. Nebuchadnezzar demands to be worshipped as a god; the story systematically dismantles that claim. The true God acts through the most unlikely instrument -- a fasting widow with a sword -- to overthrow the mightiest army in the world. Judith's repeated insistence that the elders must not "put God to the test" establishes a theology of patient faith.

### The Reversal of Power
The book revels in dramatic inversions. The weak defeat the strong. A woman conquers a general. Beauty becomes a weapon. A food bag carries a severed head. The one who boasted "your God shall be my God" becomes a headless corpse. These reversals are not mere plot twists; they embody a theological claim that God works through the lowly and overturns the proud.

### Gender, Agency, and the Body
Judith is one of the most striking female protagonists in ancient literature. She operates with complete autonomy: she summons the male elders (not the other way around), rebukes them, devises and executes the plan alone, and never accepts a subordinate role. Her beauty and sexuality are tools she deploys strategically and on her own terms. The text is careful to note that "no defilement" came upon her -- she maintained control throughout. Scholars have extensively debated whether Judith is a feminist figure, noting that while she wields extraordinary agency, the narrative is still embedded in patriarchal assumptions (her piety, chastity, and widowhood are essential to her characterization).

### Resistance and Anti-Imperialism
Written during or shortly after the Maccabean struggle against the Seleucid Empire, the Book of Judith speaks directly to communities under foreign domination. Holofernes's demand that all nations worship Nebuchadnezzar as a god mirrors the Seleucid requirement to worship Greek deities. The story insists that imperial power, no matter how overwhelming, cannot prevail against a people whose God is sovereign over history.

### Deception and Ethics
Judith lies. She lies elaborately, persuasively, and repeatedly to Holofernes. She uses her beauty to manipulate, and she kills a man in his sleep. The text presents no moral discomfort about this; Judith is praised by townspeople, elders, and the high priest himself. The ethical framework of the book subordinates conventional morality to the higher imperative of communal survival and divine purpose. This has made Judith a fascinating case study in the ethics of resistance, deception in wartime, and the tension between personal morality and collective liberation.

### Torah Observance and Jewish Identity
Throughout her time in the enemy camp, Judith eats only her own kosher food, prays at the appointed times, and maintains ritual purity. Her faithfulness to the Law is not incidental -- it is the mechanism of the plot. Her nightly departures to "pray" establish the pattern that allows her escape. Jewish identity, defined by practice and observance, is portrayed as both spiritually powerful and practically effective.

## Influence & Legacy

### Visual Art
Few biblical stories have inspired as much art as Judith's beheading of Holofernes. The scene became one of the most frequently depicted subjects in Western art from the Renaissance through the modern era:

- **Donatello** (c. 1457-1464) created a landmark bronze sculpture of Judith and Holofernes, originally placed in the Medici garden alongside his famous David. In the political turmoil of Florence, the sculpture became a symbol of republican resistance against tyranny.
- **Sandro Botticelli** painted *The Return of Judith to Bethulia* (c. 1469-70), depicting her as a graceful, goddess-like figure processing homeward with the head.
- **Caravaggio** (c. 1598-99) created one of the most visceral depictions in *Judith Beheading Holofernes*, capturing the exact moment of the sword strike with unflinching Baroque realism.
- **Artemisia Gentileschi** painted the subject at least twice (Florence, c. 1620; Naples, c. 1623-25), producing what many consider the most powerful versions. Unlike Caravaggio's delicate Judith paired with a passive attendant, Gentileschi depicts two strong young women working together with rolled-up sleeves and focused determination. As one of the first women admitted to the Academy of Design in Florence, Gentileschi's Judith paintings have become icons of female artistic and personal power.
- **Gustav Klimt** (1901) reinterpreted Judith as a gilded femme fatale in *Judith I*, drawing on Friedrich Hebbel's 1840 dramatic reinterpretation and infusing the scene with fin-de-siecle eroticism.
- Countless other artists -- including Michelangelo, Rembrandt, Titian, Paolo Veronese, Cranach, Rubens, and Allori -- depicted scenes from the Judith narrative.

### Music and Opera
The dramatic arc of Judith's story proved irresistible to composers:

- **Antonio Vivaldi** composed *Juditha triumphans* (1716), his only surviving oratorio, with a Latin libretto celebrating Venice's victory over the Turks by analogy with Judith's triumph.
- **Alessandro Scarlatti**, **Thomas Arne** (whose *Judith* premiered at Drury Lane in 1761), and **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart** all composed works based on the story.
- Judith appears in liturgical music and hymns across multiple traditions, from medieval Jewish piyyutim for Hanukkah to 16th-century Latin church hymns.

### Literature and Drama
- **Anglo-Saxon Judith** (10th century): A fragmentary Old English epic poem retelling the story with Germanic heroic conventions, preserved in the same manuscript as *Beowulf*.
- **Friedrich Hebbel** wrote a landmark drama *Judith* (1840) that reimagined the heroine's psychology, suggesting a more complex, ambivalent relationship with Holofernes.
- The figure of Judith influenced a long tradition of "woman warrior" and "femme fatale" characters in Western literature.
- Jewish tradition preserved Judith as a Hanukkah heroine, connecting her story with the themes of divine deliverance and resistance to foreign oppression that define the festival.

### Theology and Biblical Studies
The Book of Judith has become a central text in feminist biblical scholarship, sparking extensive debate about female agency, the use of sexuality as a tool of liberation, and the tension between patriarchal narrative frameworks and subversive content. The book's ethical complexities -- deception, violence, the ends justifying the means -- continue to generate rich theological discussion about the morality of resistance.
