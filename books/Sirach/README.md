# The Book of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus)

**The longest surviving wisdom book from antiquity -- a Jerusalem sage's passionate guide to living well, fearing God, and finding meaning in a changing world.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

### Original Language

The Book of Sirach was composed in **Biblical Hebrew** around **196-175 BCE** in **Jerusalem** by a sage named **Yeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira** (commonly called "Ben Sira"). He was a professional scribe and teacher who ran an academy (Hebrew: *bet midrash*) in Jerusalem, deeply steeped in the Torah, the prophetic writings, and the broader Jewish wisdom tradition.

Around **132 BCE**, Ben Sira's grandson emigrated to Alexandria, Egypt, where he translated the work into **Koine Greek** and added a remarkable prologue -- one of the earliest surviving translator's prefaces in all of literature. The grandson's prologue is itself a fascinating document: in it, he apologizes for imperfections in translation, noting that "what was originally expressed in Hebrew does not have exactly the same sense when translated into another language." Anyone who has wrestled with translation will recognize the sentiment.

### Key Manuscripts

For centuries, the Hebrew original was considered lost. Only the Greek (Septuagint) and Latin (Vulgate) translations survived in the Western tradition. Then, in a series of dramatic rediscoveries, the Hebrew text began to resurface:

- **Cairo Genizah (1896-1900)** -- Fragments from six medieval manuscripts (11th-12th century CE) were discovered in the storeroom (*genizah*) of the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Old Cairo by Solomon Schechter and others. Together, these fragments preserve approximately **two-thirds** of the Hebrew text and remain the most extensive Hebrew witnesses to the book.

- **Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran)** -- Small but important fragments of Sirach in Hebrew were found in Cave 2 at Qumran (designated 2Q18), dating to the 1st century BCE. These ancient copies confirmed that the medieval Cairo Genizah manuscripts were faithfully transmitting a genuinely old Hebrew text, not a late retranslation from Greek.

- **Masada Scroll (1964)** -- During Yigael Yadin's excavation of the Masada fortress, a scroll (Mas1H / MasSir) was discovered in casemate room 1109, preserving portions of Sirach chapters 39-44 in Hebrew. Because Masada fell to the Romans in **73 CE**, this manuscript provides a firm terminus ante quem -- the Hebrew text was circulating before the destruction of the Second Temple. This discovery was pivotal in establishing scholarly consensus that the Cairo Genizah manuscripts reflect a genuine Hebrew original rather than a medieval back-translation.

Together, these manuscript traditions -- Greek, Latin, Syriac, Cairo Genizah, Qumran, and Masada -- make Sirach one of the most richly attested books of the Second Temple period.

## Historical Context

### The World Ben Sira Inhabited

Ben Sira lived during a turbulent transition in the ancient Near East. The land of Israel had passed from Persian to Ptolemaic (Egyptian) control and, during Ben Sira's own lifetime, was seized by the Seleucid Empire under Antiochus III following the Battle of Panium (200 BCE). Greek culture -- its language, philosophy, gymnasium culture, and social ideals -- was increasingly penetrating Jewish life.

Ben Sira was no reactionary isolationist. He had traveled widely (34:11-12), dined with the powerful (31:12-32:13), and was clearly aware of Greek literary and philosophical conventions. Yet he was deeply concerned that the allure of Hellenism would erode the distinctive identity and faith of his people. His book is, in one sense, a sustained argument that **Jewish wisdom is not inferior to Greek philosophy** -- that the Torah, rightly understood, contains everything a young person needs to navigate life with integrity and purpose.

### Community of Origin

Ben Sira wrote from the heart of Jerusalem's educated elite. He was connected to the Temple establishment (his extended praise of the high priest Simon II in chapter 50 is clearly written by an eyewitness) and familiar with the scribal and priestly classes. His school likely trained young men for careers in government, Temple service, and commerce. His teachings reflect the concerns of an urban, literate class grappling with foreign influence while trying to preserve ancestral traditions.

### Purpose

Ben Sira's purpose was both practical and theological. He wanted to demonstrate that Israel's God-given wisdom tradition offered a complete guide to the good life -- from table manners to theodicy, from dealing with difficult friends to contemplating the wonders of creation. He sought to equip a new generation to be faithful Jews in an increasingly cosmopolitan world.

## Canonical Status

The Book of Sirach occupies a unique and somewhat contested place across religious traditions:

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Judaism** | Not included in the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). Although widely read and quoted in the Talmud and rabbinic literature, it was ultimately excluded from the Jewish canon when the boundaries of scripture were formalized. The Talmud occasionally quotes it with the formula "as it is written," suggesting it once held near-scriptural authority. |
| **Roman Catholic Church** | **Deuterocanonical** (fully canonical). Affirmed at the Council of Trent (1546) as inspired scripture, though Catholic canonical recognition extends specifically to the Greek text. |
| **Eastern Orthodox Churches** | **Deuterocanonical** (fully canonical). Affirmed by the Synod of Jerusalem (1672). |
| **Oriental Orthodox Churches** | Canonical. Included in the Ethiopian, Coptic, and Armenian biblical canons. |
| **Anglican Communion** | Included among the "Apocrypha" -- read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not used to establish doctrine (Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles). |
| **Lutheran Churches** | Similarly included in the Apocrypha section; valued for edification but not considered on par with canonical scripture. |
| **Other Protestant traditions** | Generally excluded from the Bible, though historical awareness of it varies widely. |

The title **"Ecclesiasticus"** (Latin for "the Church Book") was given by the Latin Church Fathers, beginning with Cyprian in the 3rd century, because the book was so frequently read aloud in churches. In the early church, it served as a kind of official catechism for those preparing for baptism.

## Summary

The Book of Sirach spans 51 chapters of wisdom poetry, ethical instruction, theological reflection, and historical narrative. While it does not follow a rigidly systematic outline, scholars generally recognize the following major sections:

### The Translator's Prologue

Ben Sira's grandson provides a brief but invaluable preface explaining when and why he translated the book. He notes the importance of the "Law, the Prophets, and the other books" -- one of the earliest references to the threefold division of the Hebrew scriptures -- and asks readers to be forgiving of any shortcomings in translation.

### Part I: Wisdom and Moral Instruction (Chapters 1-23)

The book opens with a magnificent hymn declaring that **"all wisdom comes from the Lord and with him it remains forever"** (1:1). The fear of the Lord is immediately established as the foundation, crown, and fullness of wisdom -- a theme that reverberates throughout the entire work.

From this theological grounding, Ben Sira launches into an extraordinarily wide-ranging collection of teachings on practical ethics:

- **Trusting God in trials** (2:1-18) -- "My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing."
- **Honoring parents** (3:1-16) -- Among the most tender passages in all wisdom literature.
- **Humility and pride** (3:17-29; 10:6-18)
- **Almsgiving and care for the poor** (3:30-4:10; 29:8-13)
- **The pursuit of wisdom** (4:11-19; 6:18-37) -- Wisdom is personified as a woman who tests, disciplines, and ultimately rewards those who seek her.
- **Friendship** (6:5-17; 22:19-26) -- Incisive observations on true versus false friends, including the famous warning that "a faithful friend is a sturdy shelter."
- **Speech and silence** (5:9-6:1; 19:4-17; 23:7-15) -- Ben Sira returns again and again to the power and danger of the tongue.
- **Wealth and poverty** (13:1-14:19) -- Realistic, sometimes biting observations on how money shapes relationships.
- **Free will and moral responsibility** (15:11-20) -- A striking passage insisting that God does not cause humans to sin: "It was he who created humankind in the beginning, and he left them in the power of their own free choice."
- **Self-discipline and moderation** (18:30-19:3; 23:1-6)

### Part II: Wisdom's Excellence and Social Life (Chapters 24-42:14)

Chapter 24 stands as the theological centerpiece of the book. In a majestic speech reminiscent of Proverbs 8, **personified Wisdom speaks in her own voice**, recounting how she came forth from the mouth of the Most High, searched for a dwelling place among all peoples, and was ultimately commanded by the Creator to pitch her tent in Israel. Ben Sira then makes his boldest theological claim: this cosmic Wisdom is identified with **"the book of the covenant of the Most High God, the Law that Moses commanded"** (24:23). Torah and Wisdom are one.

The remaining chapters in this section cover an astonishing range of topics:

- **Marriage, good and bad wives** (25:13-26:27; 36:26-31) -- These passages reflect the patriarchal assumptions of the ancient world, and some are uncomfortable reading for modern audiences, though the portrait of an ideal marriage partner is warm and appreciative.
- **Commerce and business ethics** (26:29-27:3; 42:1-5)
- **Proper behavior at banquets** (31:12-32:13) -- Entertainingly specific advice about eating, drinking, and hosting.
- **The role of physicians and medicine** (38:1-15) -- A remarkable passage that affirms both prayer and professional medical care: "Honor physicians for their services, for the Lord created them."
- **Mourning and grief** (38:16-23) -- Counsels a measured grief that honors the dead without destroying the living.
- **Manual labor and intellectual work** (38:24-39:11) -- An honest (and somewhat elitist) discussion of various occupations, concluding that the scribe's vocation is highest because it serves the community's wisdom.
- **Theodicy and divine justice** (39:12-35) -- Ben Sira wrestles with the problem of evil, ultimately affirming that "the works of the Lord are all good, and he will supply every need in its time."
- **Human suffering and mortality** (40:1-41:13)
- **Shame and honor** (41:14-42:8)

### Part III: The Glory of God in Creation (42:15-43:33)

In a breathtaking shift from ethical instruction, Ben Sira turns his gaze to the natural world. This hymn to creation praises the sun, moon, stars, rainbow, snow, ice, wind, rain, and the depths of the sea. The passage builds to an overwhelming conclusion: **"We could say more but could never say enough; let the final word be: 'He is the all'"** (43:27). It is one of the great nature poems of the ancient world.

### Part IV: The Praise of the Ancestors (44:1-50:24)

"Let us now praise famous men, and our ancestors in their generations" (44:1) -- one of the most celebrated opening lines in all of ancient literature, later borrowed by James Agee for his landmark 1941 book about Alabama sharecroppers.

This extended hymn traces the heroes of Israel's history from the very beginning:

- **Enoch**, who walked with God
- **Noah**, who preserved life through the flood
- **Abraham**, father of many nations, tested and found faithful
- **Isaac** and **Jacob**
- **Moses**, beloved by God and humanity alike
- **Aaron**, anointed for the priesthood
- **Phinehas**, zealous in covenant loyalty
- **Joshua** and **Caleb**, warriors of the conquest
- **The Judges** (as a group), **Samuel**, **Nathan**
- **David**, who established worship and song
- **Solomon**, whose wisdom and folly are both acknowledged
- **Elijah**, taken up in a whirlwind, and **Elisha**, his successor
- **Hezekiah** and **Isaiah**, king and prophet together
- **Josiah**, the great reformer
- **Jeremiah**, **Ezekiel**, and the **Twelve Prophets**
- **Zerubbabel**, **Joshua (Jeshua)** the high priest, and **Nehemiah**, who rebuilt Jerusalem

The sequence climaxes with a lavish portrait of **Simon II, son of Onias**, the high priest whom Ben Sira apparently knew personally. Simon is described in vivid, almost cinematic detail as he emerges from the Temple on the Day of Atonement -- "like the morning star among the clouds, like the full moon at the festal season" (50:6). This eyewitness description of Second Temple worship is one of the most precious passages in all of Jewish literature.

### Epilogue (Chapter 51)

The book closes with two personal compositions: a **prayer of thanksgiving** (51:1-12) in which Ben Sira recounts God's deliverance from personal danger, and an **autobiographical poem** (51:13-30) describing his lifelong pursuit of wisdom. The final verses serve as an invitation: "Draw near to me, you who are uneducated, and lodge in the house of instruction" (51:23). The teacher's door remains open.

## Key Themes

### The Fear of the Lord as the Root of Wisdom

This is the book's foundational conviction. Ben Sira uses the phrase "fear of the Lord" more than any other biblical author, and he returns to it obsessively -- as beginning, crown, fullness, and root of wisdom. This "fear" is not terror but reverent awe, an orientation of the entire self toward the God who created and sustains all things.

### The Identification of Wisdom with Torah

Ben Sira makes the bold theological move of identifying cosmic Wisdom -- the principle by which God created the world -- with the concrete, historical Law of Moses. This fusion of creation theology and covenant theology is one of his most significant intellectual contributions, bridging the wisdom tradition (Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes) with the legal and narrative traditions of Israel.

### The Dignity of the Everyday

Few ancient writers pay as much attention to the texture of daily life. Ben Sira has something to say about table manners, borrowing money, raising children, visiting the sick, choosing friends, controlling one's temper, tipping musicians at a banquet, and the proper length of a eulogy. His vision of wisdom is emphatically this-worldly: the good life is lived not in philosophical abstraction but in the messy particulars of human community.

### Free Will and Moral Responsibility

In a passage that anticipates centuries of theological debate, Ben Sira insists that human beings possess genuine free choice (15:11-20). God does not cause sin. Fire and water are set before you; stretch out your hand for whichever you choose. This emphasis on human agency distinguishes Sirach from more deterministic strands of Jewish thought and resonates powerfully with later Christian and Jewish ethical teaching.

### Creation as Revelation

The great nature hymn of chapters 42-43 reflects Ben Sira's conviction that the natural world is not merely a backdrop for human drama but a primary revelation of God's glory. Sun, stars, storms, and sea all bear witness to the Creator. This sacramental vision of nature influenced later Jewish and Christian mystical traditions.

### The Continuity of Sacred History

The Praise of the Ancestors (chapters 44-50) is more than a catalog of heroes. It is a theological argument that God's faithfulness runs like an unbroken thread through Israel's entire history, from creation to the present day. By ending with the living high priest Simon II, Ben Sira collapses the distance between past and present: the same God who called Abraham now meets the worshiper in the Temple.

### Wealth, Poverty, and Social Justice

Ben Sira is realistic about the power of money and unflinching about the obligation to care for the poor. He recognizes that wealth brings temptation and that poverty brings suffering. His advice navigates between asceticism and materialism, urging generosity, honest commerce, and trust in divine provision.

## Influence & Legacy

### On the New Testament

Although the New Testament never directly quotes Sirach by name, its fingerprints are everywhere:

- The **Epistle of James** is the most heavily influenced New Testament book, sharing with Sirach its emphasis on the dangers of speech, the testing of faith through trials, the relationship between wisdom and action, and the insistence that God does not tempt anyone to sin.
- The **Magnificat** (Luke 1:46-55) echoes Sirach's language about God scattering the proud and lifting up the lowly.
- The **Sermon on the Mount** shares thematic parallels with Ben Sira's ethical teachings.
- The "Praise of the Ancestors" in Sirach 44-50 provided a model for the great "faith hall of fame" in **Hebrews 11**.

### On Jewish Tradition

Though excluded from the Jewish canon, Sirach was deeply influential:

- The **Babylonian Talmud** quotes it multiple times, sometimes with the introductory formula used for scripture.
- Recent scholarship suggests that Sirach may have provided vocabulary and structural frameworks for the **Amidah**, one of the central prayers of Jewish liturgy.
- A medieval Jewish poet used Sirach 50 as the basis for a poem in the **Yom Kippur** service (Mahzor).
- Ben Sira's fusion of wisdom and Torah anticipated the approach of later **rabbinic Judaism**, which similarly sought to derive ethical wisdom from the study of the Law.

### On Christian Worship and Theology

- The Latin title "Ecclesiasticus" ("Church Book") reflects its enormous popularity in early Christian liturgy. It was used extensively in the catechumenate -- the process of preparing converts for baptism.
- **Cyprian**, **Jerome**, **Clement of Alexandria**, and **Augustine** all quoted Sirach.
- Sirach 44:1-14 ("Let us now praise famous men") was the traditional reading for **All Saints' Day** in the Anglican liturgical tradition and remains widely used in memorial and funeral services.
- Ben Sira's identification of Wisdom with Torah influenced early Christian reflections on Christ as the incarnate Wisdom of God (see John 1, Colossians 1, and the Wisdom Christology of the patristic period).

### On Literature and Culture

- **James Agee** and photographer **Walker Evans** borrowed the title "Let Us Now Praise Famous Men" (from Sirach 44:1) for their landmark 1941 documentary book about impoverished Alabama tenant farmers -- an ironic inversion of Ben Sira's celebration of the powerful and celebrated.
- The book's practical, this-worldly wisdom has made it a perennial resource for ethical reflection, quoted in contexts ranging from commencement speeches to political philosophy.
