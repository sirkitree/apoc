# The Book of Baruch (including the Letter of Jeremiah)

A composite work of prayer, poetry, and prophetic exhortation written in the voice of Jeremiah's faithful scribe, calling exiled Israel to repentance, wisdom, and hope.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**Original Language:** The earliest surviving texts of Baruch are in Greek, preserved in the Septuagint (LXX). However, the linguistic character of chapters 1--3:8 strongly suggests translation from a Hebrew (or possibly Aramaic) original. Anomalous Greek readings in these sections appear to reflect mistranslations of a Semitic source text. The later poetic sections (3:9--5:9) may have been composed in Hebrew as well, though the evidence is less definitive. No Hebrew manuscript of Baruch has ever been found, and both Origen (3rd century) and Jerome (5th century) noted the absence of a Hebrew text in their day.

The **Letter of Jeremiah** (chapter 6 in the Vulgate tradition) circulated as a separate composition in Greek. A small but significant Greek papyrus fragment of the Letter of Jeremiah (designated **7Q2**) was discovered in Cave 7 at Qumran among the Dead Sea Scrolls, paleographically dated to approximately **100 BCE**. This is the oldest physical witness to any part of the Baruch corpus and confirms that the Letter was in circulation during the late Second Temple period.

**Key Manuscripts:**

- **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century CE) -- contains both Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah as separate books, placed in the order: Jeremiah, Baruch, Lamentations, Letter of Jeremiah.
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century CE) -- similarly preserves both texts among the prophetic books.
- **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century CE) -- another major witness to the Greek text.
- **7Q2** (c. 100 BCE) -- Greek fragment of the Letter of Jeremiah from Qumran Cave 7, the earliest extant manuscript evidence.

**Date of Composition:** Baruch is pseudepigraphic -- attributed to Baruch ben Neriah, the scribe of the prophet Jeremiah, but composed much later. Most scholars date the various sections of the book to the **2nd or 1st century BCE**, with the final editorial compilation likely occurring in the last two centuries before the Common Era. The Letter of Jeremiah may be somewhat older, possibly composed around **300 BCE** by a Jew living in Babylonia, given the intensity of its polemic against idolatry.

**Place of Composition:** The setting described is Babylon during the exile, but the actual place of writing is debated. Candidates include Jerusalem, Babylon, and Alexandria. The Letter of Jeremiah was likely written in a diaspora context where the temptation of idol worship was a lived reality.

## Historical Context

**Historical Setting:** The book presents itself as being written in **Babylon in the fifth year after the destruction of Jerusalem** by the Chaldeans (586 BCE). Baruch reads his work aloud before King Jeconiah (Jehoiachin) and the exiled community, who respond with weeping, fasting, and prayer. They collect money and send the book to Jerusalem along with funds to support the Temple's ongoing liturgical life -- a poignant detail suggesting continuity of worship even after catastrophe.

**Community of Origin:** Baruch speaks to and from a community grappling with the theological crisis of exile: Why did God allow the destruction of Jerusalem? The answer the book provides follows the Deuteronomic pattern -- Israel sinned, God punished justly, repentance opens the door to restoration. The text addresses both the exiles in Babylon and the remnant community in Jerusalem, serving as a bridge between dispersed and homeland Jews.

**Purpose:** The book has several interlocking purposes:

1. **Theological explanation** -- to make sense of national catastrophe through the lens of covenantal disobedience and divine justice.
2. **Liturgical function** -- the text explicitly instructs that it be read aloud "in the house of the Lord upon the day of the feast and on the days of the solemn assembly" (1:14), suggesting it was designed for communal worship.
3. **Exhortation to wisdom** -- to call Israel back to the Torah as the source of true wisdom and life.
4. **Consolation** -- to offer hope that God will restore the fortunes of the exiled people.
5. **Warning against idolatry** -- particularly in the Letter of Jeremiah, to inoculate diaspora Jews against the seductive pageantry of Babylonian religion.

## Canonical Status

The canonical standing of Baruch varies significantly across religious traditions:

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Roman Catholic** | Canonical (deuterocanonical). Affirmed by the Council of Florence (1442) and the Council of Trent (1546), which declared "Jeremias with Baruch" as part of the Old Testament canon. Baruch 3:9-37 is read during the Holy Saturday liturgy. |
| **Eastern Orthodox** | Canonical. A selection from Baruch is read as one of the eight Paroemia (Old Testament readings) during the Vesperal Divine Liturgy on Christmas Eve. |
| **Oriental Orthodox** | Canonical. Accepted as part of the broader Old Testament. |
| **Anglican / Lutheran** | Not canonical, but considered edifying. Included in the "Apocrypha" section of Bibles in these traditions, recommended for reading but not for establishing doctrine. |
| **Protestant (Reformed)** | Not canonical. Classified as apocrypha. Not typically included in Protestant Bibles. |
| **Jewish** | Not canonical. Never accepted into the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). Although used by Alexandrian Jews and transmitted through the Septuagint, the Palestinian Jewish community did not recognize it as scripture. |

**A note on the Letter of Jeremiah:** In the Septuagint and Orthodox Bibles, the Letter of Jeremiah is typically counted as a separate book. In the Vulgate and Catholic tradition, it is appended to Baruch as chapter 6. This collection follows the Vulgate convention.

## Summary

### Part I: Introduction and Historical Setting (1:1--14)

The book opens by identifying its author as **Baruch son of Neriah**, tracing his lineage back five generations. He writes in Babylon "in the fifth year, in the seventh day of the month" -- that is, five years after the Chaldeans captured and burned Jerusalem. Baruch reads the book publicly before King Jeconiah and the entire exiled community gathered by the river Sud. The people respond with weeping, fasting, and prayer. They take up a collection and send the book and the money to Jerusalem, to Joakim the high priest and the remaining community, with instructions to purchase offerings for the Temple altar. Remarkably, they also instruct the Jerusalem community to "pray for the life of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and for the life of Baltasar his son" -- a striking call to pray for the welfare of their captors, echoing Jeremiah 29:7.

### Part II: Confession of Sins (1:15--2:10)

The confession begins with a sweeping acknowledgment: "To the Lord our God belongs righteousness, but to us confusion of face." The people trace the roots of their suffering back to the exodus from Egypt, cataloguing a long history of disobedience. They confess that every warning sent through the prophets was ignored. The result is the curses foretold by Moses -- famine so severe that parents would eat the flesh of their own children (2:3), subjection to surrounding nations, and the people becoming "a byword" among their neighbors. This section echoes Daniel 9 so closely that scholars debate which text influenced the other.

### Part III: Prayer for Mercy (2:11--3:8)

The tone shifts from confession to supplication. The community appeals not to their own merit but to God's reputation: "For your own sake... look down upon us." They recall God's covenant promises, including the pledge to bring the people back to the land and to give them "a heart that obeys and ears that listen" (2:31). The prayer reaches its emotional peak with a plea that God consider the dead in Sheol, who can no longer give glory to God, and instead look to the living who are "bowed down, walking feebly, with failing eyes and famished souls" (2:18). God is asked to act for the sake of his own name and for the honor of the Temple.

### Part IV: Poem on Wisdom (3:9--4:4)

This is the literary and theological crown of the book. It opens with a magnificent call: **"Hear, O Israel, the commandments of life; give ear, and learn wisdom!"** The poet asks why Israel finds itself in exile, in the land of enemies, growing old in a foreign country -- and answers: "You have forsaken the fountain of wisdom."

The poem then embarks on a grand survey of who has found wisdom. The rulers of nations? No -- they have perished. The merchants of Teman, the storytellers, the seekers of understanding? None of them discovered her. Even the legendary giants of old, those "famous men of old, great in stature, expert in war" (3:26), were not chosen by God and perished for lack of wisdom. The passage climaxes in a breathtaking theological statement: **God alone knows wisdom**. He gave her to Israel in the form of the Torah: "She is the book of the commandments of God, the law that endures forever. All who hold her fast will live, and those who forsake her will die" (4:1).

The verse that most captivated early Christian readers is 3:37-38: "He found the whole way to knowledge, and gave her to his servant Jacob... **Afterward she appeared on earth and lived among mortals.**" Church Fathers such as Irenaeus, Cyprian, and John of Damascus read this as a prophecy of the Incarnation -- Wisdom made flesh in Christ. Thomas Aquinas quoted this passage in the *Summa Theologiae* to affirm that "the Son of God assumed human nature."

### Part V: Consolation of Jerusalem (4:5--5:9)

The final section is a dramatic sequence of poems in which Jerusalem itself becomes a speaking character. It opens with four odes, each beginning with the exhortation **"Take courage!"** (4:5, 21, 27, 30).

First, Baruch addresses the exiled people directly: "Take courage, my people, you who perpetuate Israel's name!" He reassures them that God did not hand them over to destruction but to discipline. Then **Jerusalem speaks as a grieving mother**, a widow who has watched her children be led away: "I was left desolate because of the sins of my children." She tells her neighbors to witness her sorrow, but also confesses that she herself saw God's wrath come upon her people because of their sins.

The mood then turns to hope. Jerusalem calls to her children to cry out to God, who will deliver them. The final poem (4:36--5:9) envisions a glorious restoration: "Look toward the east, O Jerusalem, and see the joy that is coming to you from God!" The scattered children will be gathered from east and west. Jerusalem is told to take off her garments of mourning and put on the beauty of God's glory forever. Mountains will be made low and valleys filled, so that Israel may walk safely in God's glory. This imagery closely parallels the Psalms of Solomon 11 and anticipates the language of Isaiah 40 and 60.

### Part VI: The Letter of Jeremiah (Chapter 6)

The Letter of Jeremiah is cast as a message sent by the prophet Jeremiah to the Jews about to be taken captive to Babylon, warning them that they will remain there "a long time, up to seven generations" before God brings them home. Its single sustained argument: **the gods of Babylon are not gods at all**.

The letter consists of approximately **ten distinct proofs** that idols are powerless human creations, each concluding with a refrain such as: *"From this you will know that they are not gods; so do not fear them."*

The arguments are vivid and often bitterly satirical:

- Idols are made of wood, overlaid with gold and silver, yet they cannot save themselves from rust and corrosion (6:7-14).
- They hold scepters like judges but cannot punish wrongdoers; they carry daggers and axes but cannot defend themselves from war or thieves (6:15-22).
- Their faces are blackened by the smoke of the temple, while bats, swallows, and cats crawl over their bodies (6:23-28).
- Women bring offerings to them, and the ritually unclean serve as their priests, yet the idols cannot set up a deposed king or give rain or shine (6:29-39).
- Chaldean priests themselves dishonor their gods, stealing gold from the idols to spend on prostitutes (6:40-44).
- They are made by carpenters and goldsmiths and are worth no more than the stone and wood they are made of (6:45-51).
- They cannot establish a king, give gifts, avenge wrong, or rescue the oppressed -- they are as useless as crows between heaven and earth (6:52-64).
- The letter concludes with the devastating comparison: **"Like a scarecrow in a cucumber patch, guarding nothing, are their gods of wood, overlaid with gold and silver"** (6:70). A righteous person with no idols at all is better off than all of these gods.

The polemic draws on the tradition of Isaiah 44 and Jeremiah 10:1-16, where the absurdity of idol manufacture is a major prophetic theme. Its emotional intensity suggests that for its original audience, the pull of Babylonian religion was real and dangerous.

## Key Themes

### Exile as Divine Discipline
The book interprets the Babylonian exile not as the failure of God but as the just consequence of Israel's persistent disobedience. This follows the Deuteronomic theological framework: sin leads to punishment, repentance leads to restoration. The exile is painful but purposeful.

### Corporate Confession and Repentance
The long prayer of confession (1:15--3:8) is communal, not individual. The community takes collective responsibility for the sins of their ancestors and themselves, modeling a theology of solidarity across generations.

### Wisdom and Torah
The great poem of 3:9--4:4 identifies Wisdom -- the transcendent, cosmic principle that orders all creation -- with the Torah, the concrete commandments given to Israel. This is a bold theological move: it claims that the universal is found in the particular, that the wisdom which eludes all nations has been given specifically to Israel in the form of God's Law.

### Personified Wisdom
Following the tradition of Proverbs 8, Job 28, Sirach 24, and the Wisdom of Solomon, Baruch personifies Wisdom as a figure who "appeared on earth and lived among mortals." This personification became theologically significant for Christians who read it as anticipating the Incarnation.

### Jerusalem as Mother
In the consolation poems (4:5--5:9), Jerusalem is personified as a bereaved mother and a widow, mourning her exiled children. This image -- deeply rooted in Lamentations and Isaiah -- gives voice to communal grief while also providing the ground for hope, as the mother is promised that her children will return.

### The Futility of Idolatry
The Letter of Jeremiah is a sustained, almost comedic demolition of idol worship. Its method is empirical: look at what the gods can and cannot do. They cannot move, speak, see, save, judge, or protect. The letter is less philosophical argument than prophetic ridicule, continuing the tradition of Second Isaiah's mockery of idol-makers.

### Hope and Restoration
Despite the weight of sin and exile, the book ends with radiant hope. Mountains are leveled, valleys filled, Jerusalem is clothed in glory, and the scattered children return. God's final word is not judgment but mercy.

## Influence & Legacy

### Early Christian Theology
The verse that most profoundly shaped Christian theology was Baruch 3:37-38: "Afterward she appeared on earth and lived among mortals." The Church Fathers -- including **Irenaeus**, **Cyprian**, **Clement of Alexandria**, **Hilary of Poitiers**, **John of Damascus**, and **Gregory of Nazianzus** -- read this as a prophetic announcement of the Incarnation. **Thomas Aquinas** quoted Baruch 3:37 (numbered 3:38 in the Vulgate) in the *Summa Theologiae* as proof that "the Son of God assumed human nature."

### Liturgical Use
Baruch has a lasting presence in Catholic and Orthodox worship:
- **Catholic tradition:** Baruch 3:9-37 is read during the **Easter Vigil (Holy Saturday)** liturgy, connecting the wisdom poem to the great themes of creation, redemption, and resurrection.
- **Orthodox tradition:** A passage from Baruch is read as one of the eight Paroemia (Old Testament readings) during the **Vesperal Divine Liturgy on Christmas Eve**, linking the book to the celebration of Christ's birth.

### Wisdom Literature Tradition
Baruch's identification of Wisdom with Torah stands as a key link in the chain of Jewish wisdom literature, connecting the more universal speculations of Proverbs and Job to the Torah-centered piety of Sirach and later rabbinic thought. This equation -- that true wisdom is obedience to God's revealed Law -- became foundational for rabbinic Judaism.

### Anti-Idolatry Tradition
The Letter of Jeremiah's vivid polemic against idol worship stands alongside Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10, Wisdom of Solomon 13-15, and the later rabbinic mockery of paganism as part of a sustained tradition of satirical religious critique. Its imagery -- bats on the heads of gods, cats walking across divine laps, scarecrows in cucumber patches -- remained potent rhetorical ammunition for centuries.

### Related Pseudepigraphal Literature
The figure of Baruch inspired an extensive body of later pseudepigraphal writings:
- **2 Baruch** (Syriac Apocalypse of Baruch) -- an apocalyptic text reflecting on the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE.
- **3 Baruch** (Greek Apocalypse of Baruch) -- a visionary journey through the heavens.
- **4 Baruch** (*Paraleipomena Jeremiou*) -- a narrative about Baruch and Jeremiah after the fall of Jerusalem.

These works testify to the enduring power of Baruch as a literary and theological figure in the imagination of both Jews and Christians in the centuries around the turn of the Common Era.
