# The Book of Jubilees

**An ancient Jewish retelling of Genesis and Exodus, revealed by the Angel of the Presence to Moses on Mount Sinai, dividing all of sacred history into periods of forty-nine years -- the jubilees.**

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Book of Jubilees was composed in Hebrew, most likely between **160 and 150 BCE**, during a turbulent period of Second Temple Judaism. The original Hebrew text was lost for centuries, but three key witnesses preserve the work:

- **Ethiopic (Ge'ez):** The only complete surviving text. Twenty-seven Ge'ez manuscripts exist, the earliest dating to the 15th and 16th centuries. Because the Ethiopian Orthodox Church accepted Jubilees as canonical scripture, Ethiopic scribes carefully preserved and copied it across the centuries. The Ge'ez version was translated from a now-lost Greek intermediary, which was itself translated from the Hebrew original. Most modern English editions rely on the Ge'ez text.

- **Dead Sea Scrolls (Hebrew fragments):** Between 1947 and 1956, fragments from approximately **fifteen manuscripts** of Jubilees were recovered from five caves at Qumran (Caves 1, 2, 3, 4, and 11). All were written in Biblical Hebrew. These fragments contain words or partial verses from roughly 215 of the book's 1,307 verses. The sheer number of copies found at Qumran -- more than for most biblical books aside from Psalms, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Exodus, and Genesis -- testifies to how important this text was to the community that collected the Dead Sea Scrolls. Comparing the Hebrew fragments to the Ethiopic version has confirmed that the ancient translators worked with remarkable accuracy.

- **Latin and Greek fragments:** A partial Latin translation survives, and scattered Greek quotations appear in the writings of early Church Fathers such as Epiphanius and Justin Martyr. These confirm details of the text's transmission history but are too fragmentary to serve as a basis for translation.

The work is also known by several other names: **"The Little Genesis"** (Leptogenesis), because it covers the same ground as Genesis with additional detail; and in Ge'ez, **Mets'hafe Kufale** ("Book of Division"), referring to its systematic division of history into jubilee periods.

## Historical Context

Jubilees emerged during one of the most contested periods in Jewish history. The mid-second century BCE saw the aftermath of the Maccabean revolt against the Seleucid Empire, fierce debates over the proper way to observe the Torah, and deep anxiety about Hellenistic cultural influence on Jewish life and worship.

The author -- almost certainly a priestly figure, possibly connected to the community that would later settle at Qumran -- was writing in a world where fundamental questions were being fought over: Which calendar should govern the Temple festivals? How strictly should Israel separate itself from Gentile nations? Were the patriarchs already observing the commandments before Sinai?

Jubilees addresses all of these questions by rewriting the narrative of Genesis and the opening of Exodus. It does not simply repeat the biblical text; it **interprets, expands, and reshapes** the stories to argue for specific theological and legal positions. Where Genesis is sometimes ambiguous or silent, Jubilees fills in the gaps with precise dates, ethical rulings, and angelic explanations.

The relationship to Genesis and Exodus is that of a commentary woven into the fabric of narrative itself. Scholars call this genre **"rewritten scripture"** -- a retelling that walks alongside the original, sometimes echoing it word for word, sometimes departing radically. Jubilees does not claim to replace the Torah. Rather, it claims to be the hidden, fuller version of what was revealed to Moses, dictated from the "heavenly tablets" that record all of history.

## Canonical Status

The canonical status of Jubilees varies dramatically across religious traditions:

- **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church:** Jubilees is **fully canonical** scripture, part of the broader 81-book Ethiopian biblical canon (sometimes counted as 81, sometimes as 46 OT + 35 NT). It sits alongside 1 Enoch and the three books of Meqabyan as texts unique to the Ethiopian canon. The Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church likewise regards it as scripture.

- **Beta Israel (Ethiopian Judaism):** The Jewish community historically rooted in Ethiopia also considers Jubilees canonical, a striking exception within Judaism. For Beta Israel, the Ge'ez text carried scriptural authority, and the book's emphasis on separateness, purity, and sabbath observance resonated deeply with their practice.

- **Qumran Community (Dead Sea Scrolls):** While we cannot say the Qumran sect had a formal "canon" in the later sense, the evidence is overwhelming that Jubilees held an **authoritative, quasi-scriptural status** among them. The Damascus Document, one of the sect's key legal texts, explicitly cites Jubilees as an authority for proper calendar reckoning. The large number of manuscript copies confirms that it was read, studied, and copied as a foundational text.

- **Rabbinic Judaism, Catholic, Protestant, and Eastern Orthodox traditions:** Jubilees is **not canonical** in any of these traditions. Rabbinic sources show no direct engagement with the text, and the Western churches never included it in their biblical canons. It is classified as Old Testament pseudepigrapha in modern scholarship.

## Summary

The Book of Jubilees (50 chapters, 1,341 verses) presents itself as a private revelation: while Moses spends forty days on Mount Sinai, the **Angel of the Presence** dictates to him the true history of the world, reading from the heavenly tablets. This framing device allows the author to present his interpretation of history as divinely authorized.

The narrative covers the span from **Creation to the arrival at Sinai**, a period the author calculates as exactly **fifty jubilees** (50 x 49 = 2,450 years, minus the 40 years of wilderness wandering, yielding 2,410 years). Each major event is dated precisely within this jubilee framework.

**Chapters 1-2:** God instructs Moses on Sinai, warning of Israel's future apostasy and promising ultimate restoration. The creation account is retold with an emphasis on the sabbath and the angelic hierarchy created on the first day.

**Chapters 3-4:** Adam and Eve in Eden, the fall, and the story of Cain and Abel. Jubilees adds specific halakhic details -- for instance, that Adam and Eve observed purity laws even in the Garden.

**Chapters 5-10:** The generations before the Flood, the corruption caused by the Watchers (fallen angels), the Flood itself, and Noah's covenant. Here Jubilees introduces **Mastema** and his demons in a crucial scene: after the Flood, most evil spirits are bound, but Mastema negotiates with God to keep one-tenth of them free to test and tempt humanity.

**Chapters 11-23:** The Abraham cycle, told with extensive additions. Abraham's youth (including his rejection of idolatry as a child), the covenant, the binding of Isaac (here attributed to Mastema's provocation, not God's testing), and Abraham's death. These chapters are especially rich in halakhic argument, with the patriarchs observing festivals, tithes, and purity laws centuries before Sinai.

**Chapters 24-45:** The stories of Isaac, Jacob, Esau, and the twelve sons of Jacob. The rivalry between Jacob and Esau is intensified, with a full-scale war between their families. Joseph's story is retold with particular attention to his moral purity.

**Chapters 46-50:** Israel in Egypt, the birth of Moses, and the Exodus up to the arrival at Sinai, closing the frame by returning to the moment of revelation where the book began.

Throughout, the narrative is punctuated by laws, calendar instructions, and warnings against following the ways of the nations -- especially against adopting a lunar calendar.

## Key Themes

### The 364-Day Solar Calendar
Perhaps the most distinctive and passionately argued theme in Jubilees. The author insists that the true calendar ordained by God has **364 days per year** -- exactly 52 weeks. Because 364 is divisible by 7, every date falls on the same day of the week every year. Festivals never shift. The sabbath is never violated by a conflicting holy day. The author condemns those who follow the lunar calendar as walking "in the feasts of the Gentiles" and warns that calendar error will lead to cosmic disorder. This same 364-day calendar appears in 1 Enoch and was clearly championed by the Qumran community.

### Expanded Angelic Lore
Jubilees presents a richly populated supernatural world. Angels are created on the first day alongside the heavens and the earth. They are organized into ranks -- angels of the presence, angels of sanctification, angels governing natural phenomena. The Angel of the Presence who narrates the book holds a place of special intimacy with God. This angelology provided a foundation for later Jewish and Christian speculation about heavenly hierarchies.

### Mastema: The Adversary
Where the Hebrew Bible uses "Satan" sparingly and often ambiguously, Jubilees gives the adversary a name -- **Mastema** (from the Hebrew root meaning "hostility") -- and a defined role. Mastema is the chief of demons, the spirits of the giants destroyed in the Flood. He serves as a prosecutor and tempter with God's permission, a figure who explains why evil persists in a world governed by a just God. He instigates the binding of Isaac, sends the plagues on Egypt alongside God's angels, and generally accounts for the darker episodes of biblical history that the author wished to distance from God's direct action.

### Halakhic Concerns
Jubilees is deeply interested in Jewish law (halakhah), but its method is striking: rather than deriving law from the text of the Torah, it **embeds law within narrative**. The patriarchs are portrayed as already observing the commandments -- keeping the sabbath, celebrating the Festival of Weeks (Shavuot), offering tithes, following purity regulations -- long before the giving of the Law at Sinai. This retroactive observance serves to argue that these laws are eternal and cosmic, written on the heavenly tablets from the beginning of creation, not merely historical legislation.

### Covenant Theology and Separateness
Jubilees insists on the absolute uniqueness of Israel's covenant with God. The chosen people must remain strictly separate from the nations. Intermarriage is condemned in the strongest terms (the story of Dinah and Shechem is expanded into a categorical prohibition). Circumcision is presented as a cosmic ordinance observed even by the angels. The book's vision of covenant is both exclusive and total -- every aspect of life, from diet to calendar to marriage, falls under its scope.

## Influence & Legacy

### The Qumran Community
The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls transformed our understanding of Jubilees. The Qumran community -- widely identified with the Essenes or a related priestly separatist movement -- treated Jubilees as a foundational authority. Their sectarian calendar matches the 364-day solar calendar of Jubilees. The Damascus Document cites Jubilees by name. The community's emphasis on angelic worship, strict purity, and separation from the Jerusalem Temple establishment all find precedent in Jubilees. Some scholars argue that the author of Jubilees may have been an early member or precursor of the movement that eventually settled at Qumran.

### Ethiopian Judaism (Beta Israel)
For the Beta Israel community, Jubilees was not a curiosity but a living scripture. Its emphasis on sabbath observance, dietary law, purity, and separation from Gentile practice aligned closely with Beta Israel religious life. The survival of Jubilees in Ethiopic is itself a testament to the Ethiopian religious communities -- both Jewish and Christian -- that valued and preserved the text when it was lost everywhere else.

### Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity
The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church's acceptance of Jubilees as canonical scripture means it has shaped Ethiopian Christian theology, liturgy, and biblical interpretation in ways that have no parallel in Western Christianity. Jubilees' chronological framework, its angelic lore, and its retelling of the patriarchal narratives are woven into the fabric of Ethiopian religious culture.

### Calendar Debates in Ancient Judaism
Jubilees is one of our most important witnesses to the **calendar controversies** that divided Second Temple Judaism. The tension between solar and lunar calendars was not an abstract astronomical question -- it determined when to observe the most sacred festivals. Jubilees' passionate advocacy for the 364-day calendar, combined with the evidence from Qumran and 1 Enoch, reveals a sustained tradition of dissent against the lunisolar calendar that eventually became normative in Rabbinic Judaism.

### Broader Scholarly Significance
Jubilees is indispensable for understanding the diversity of Second Temple Jewish thought. It illuminates how ancient Jews read, interpreted, and rewrote their own scriptures. It provides evidence for the development of angelology, demonology, and apocalyptic thought. And it demonstrates that the boundary between "biblical" and "non-biblical" was far more fluid in antiquity than later canonical lists suggest.
