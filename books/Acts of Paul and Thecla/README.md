# Acts of Paul and Thecla

A second-century apocryphal narrative about a young woman who abandons her engagement, survives miraculous ordeals, baptizes herself, and becomes a traveling apostle -- the most celebrated female saint in early Christianity after Mary.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Acts of Paul and Thecla (often abbreviated *APTh*) was composed around 160-180 CE, most likely in Asia Minor (modern Turkey). It originally formed a section of the larger *Acts of Paul*, but circulated independently from an early date and was far more popular than the parent work.

The earliest external witness is **Tertullian of Carthage** (c. 190 CE), who reported in *On Baptism* 17 that the text was written by "a presbyter of Asia" who was subsequently defrocked after confessing he had composed it "out of love for Paul." Tertullian was hostile to the work precisely because some Christians -- particularly women -- were citing Thecla's example to justify women teaching and administering baptism in the church.

### Manuscript Tradition

The text survives in a remarkable number of languages and copies, testifying to its enormous popularity:

- **Greek** -- Multiple manuscripts, including the 12th-century Codex Oxoniensis and several Vatican codices from the 13th-14th centuries.
- **Coptic** -- The **Heidelberg Papyrus** (6th century), discovered in 1894, proved that the Acts of Thecla, 3 Corinthians, and the Martyrdom of Paul all belonged to the larger Acts of Paul.
- **Bodmer Papyrus XLI** (4th century) -- One of the earliest surviving copies of any portion of the text.
- **Syriac, Armenian, Latin, Ethiopic** -- Versions in all of these languages survive, sometimes differing significantly from the Greek tradition.

The sheer breadth of this manuscript tradition -- spanning from Egypt to Ethiopia to Western Europe -- is unusual for a non-canonical text and speaks to how deeply Thecla's story resonated across the ancient Christian world.

## Historical Context

The Acts of Paul and Thecla emerged during a volatile period in early Christianity. The biblical canon had not yet been defined. Communities were wrestling with fundamental questions: Who could teach? Who could baptize? What role should women play? Was celibacy superior to marriage?

### Women in Early Christianity

The text reflects -- and likely fueled -- real debates about women's authority. Paul's canonical letters contain contradictory signals: Romans 16 names women as deacons and apostles, while 1 Corinthians 14:34 (possibly a later interpolation) commands women to "be silent in the churches." The Acts of Paul and Thecla sides firmly with the more egalitarian Paul, presenting a woman who preaches, baptizes, and travels as an independent missionary.

### The Celibacy Movement

Second-century Christianity saw a powerful ascetic current. The *APTh* channels this by making celibacy the heart of Paul's preaching. His "Beatitudes of the Chaste" in the opening chapters reframe the gospel almost entirely in terms of sexual renunciation: "Blessed are they who have kept the flesh chaste, for they shall become the temple of God." For Thecla, accepting this message means rejecting her fiance, her mother, and her entire social world -- a radical act in a Roman society where a woman's primary duty was to marry and produce children for the empire.

### Martyrdom and the Arena

The text also reflects the lived reality of Christian persecution. Scenes of public execution in the amphitheater -- fire, wild beasts, bulls -- draw on the same world that produced the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* and the *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*. But where most martyr accounts end in death, Thecla survives every ordeal, making her story function more as a romance of divine rescue than a traditional martyrology.

## Canonical Status

The Acts of Paul and Thecla was never included in the New Testament canon. Tertullian's hostility, and the text's implicit challenge to emerging gender hierarchies in the church, likely contributed to its exclusion from authoritative lists.

Yet its popularity was extraordinary. The text was widely read, copied, and beloved across the ancient Christian world for centuries:

- **Methodius of Olympus** (d. 311) praised Thecla as the ideal virgin in his *Symposium*.
- **Gregory of Nazianzus** (329-390) and **Gregory of Nyssa** (335-395) both cited her as a model of ascetic virtue.
- **Ambrose of Milan** recommended her story to Christian virgins.
- **Jerome** and **Augustine** both knew the text, though they were more cautious about it.

In the Eastern church especially, the *APTh* occupied a space somewhere between scripture and popular devotion -- not canonical, but deeply authoritative in practice. A fifth-century expansion called the *Life and Miracles of Thecla* was composed at her shrine in Seleucia, demonstrating that her cult continued to generate new literature for centuries.

## Summary

### Part One: Iconium

The story opens in **Iconium** (modern Konya, Turkey), where Paul arrives at the home of Onesiphorus. The text preserves one of the earliest physical descriptions of Paul in Christian literature: "a man of small stature, with a bald head and crooked legs, in a good state of body, with eyebrows meeting and nose somewhat hooked." Despite this ungainly appearance, he radiates grace.

Paul begins preaching a message centered on chastity and resurrection. A young noblewoman named **Thecla**, betrothed to a prominent citizen named **Thamyris**, listens from a window of a neighboring house. She is so captivated that she sits motionless for three days and nights, refusing food and ignoring all pleas from her mother **Theocleia** and her fiance.

Thamyris, enraged at losing his bride, rallies the city against Paul. He and other prominent men haul Paul before the governor, who has him flogged and expelled from the city. Thecla bribes the guards to visit Paul in prison, where she sits at his feet and "kissed his bonds."

When Thecla's devotion is discovered, the governor sentences Paul to scourging and expulsion and condemns Thecla to **death by burning** -- her own mother crying out for her execution, declaring she should burn "in the midst of the theater" as a warning to other women who had listened to Paul's teaching. Stripped naked and placed on the pyre, Thecla makes the sign of the cross. At that moment, **God sends a violent hailstorm and rainstorm** that extinguishes the flames, shatters the theater, and allows her to escape unharmed.

### Part Two: Antioch

Thecla finds Paul on the road and follows him to **Pisidian Antioch**. There, a powerful local official named **Alexander** becomes infatuated with her and attempts to seize her in public, offering Paul money for her. Paul shamefully denies knowing her. Thecla **fights Alexander off**, tearing his cloak and knocking the crown from his head, humiliating him before the city.

For this public affront, Thecla is arrested and sentenced to the **wild beasts** in the amphitheater. A prominent local woman named **Queen Tryphaena** takes Thecla into her home before the execution, and the women of the city rally to her cause, crying out against the unjust sentence.

In the arena, the crowd is astonished as:

- A **fierce lioness** sent against Thecla instead lies down at her feet, licks her feet, and defends her against other animals.
- Thecla sees a pit of water filled with **man-eating seals** and, declaring "In the name of Jesus Christ, I baptize myself on the last day!", throws herself in. A flash of **lightning** kills the seals, and a cloud of fire surrounds her, shielding her nakedness from the crowd.
- More beasts are released. The women in the stands throw flowers and perfumes into the arena, and the animals fall asleep.
- Thecla is tied between **wild bulls** with hot irons, but the flames burn through the ropes and free her.

Tryphaena faints at the spectacle. The governor, terrified that he will answer to the emperor for harming a kinswoman of Caesar, releases Thecla. Alexander himself begs for her freedom. The women of the city shout: **"There is one God, the God of Thecla!"**

### Part Three: Commission and Later Life

Thecla finds Paul again in **Myra**, now dressed in a man's cloak with her hair cut short. She tells him: "I have received the washing, O Paul; for He who worked with you for the Gospel has worked with me also for my baptism."

Paul **commissions her**: "Go and teach the word of God." Thecla returns to **Iconium**, where she finds Thamyris dead and converts her mother Theocleia (in some versions, Theocleia remains unconverted). She then travels to **Seleucia**, where she "enlightened many by the word of God" and, according to later traditions, lived as a hermit in a cave for seventy-two years before her death.

## Key Themes

### Female Apostleship
Thecla is not a passive saint. She preaches, baptizes herself, travels independently, wears men's clothing, and receives a direct apostolic commission from Paul. Later tradition honored her as an **"apostle and protomartyr among women"** and **"equal-to-the-apostles."** Her story was cited by real women to claim authority in early Christian communities -- exactly the practice Tertullian was trying to suppress.

### Celibacy as Liberation
In the Roman world, a woman's body belonged to her father, then her husband, then the state (which needed her to produce soldiers and citizens). Thecla's embrace of celibacy is not merely pious self-denial -- it is a radical act of autonomy. By refusing Thamyris, she severs herself from patriarchal control and claims her body for God alone.

### Self-Baptism
Thecla's self-baptism in the pool of seals is one of the most theologically provocative moments in early Christian literature. It implies that the sacrament does not require male priestly mediation -- that a woman's faith and the Holy Spirit are sufficient. This is precisely what made the text dangerous to church authorities and why Tertullian warned against it.

### Persecution and Divine Rescue
Thecla faces execution four times (fire, beasts, seals, bulls) and survives each through miraculous intervention. The pattern echoes Daniel in the lions' den and the three youths in the fiery furnace, casting Thecla as an heir to biblical heroes of faith.

### Female Solidarity
One of the text's most striking features is the community of women who support Thecla. Queen Tryphaena shelters her. The women of Antioch protest her sentence. Women in the arena throw spices to stupefy the beasts. Even a lioness fights to defend her. The story imagines a world in which women collectively resist unjust male authority.

### Gender Transgression
Thecla cuts her hair, wears men's clothing, and moves through the world with a freedom normally reserved for men. The text treats this not as scandal but as holy transformation. Later hagiography would develop this motif extensively -- stories of women saints disguised as monks trace a direct line back to Thecla.

## Influence & Legacy

### Veneration of Saint Thecla
Thecla became the **most venerated female saint in early Christianity after the Virgin Mary**. Her cult was centered at **Seleucia** (modern Silifke, on Turkey's southeastern coast), where a major shrine and pilgrimage site developed. Archaeological remains of a rock church dedicated to Thecla survive there today.

She is honored across multiple traditions:
- **Roman Catholic**: Feast day September 23
- **Eastern Orthodox**: Feast day September 24; honored as "Equal-to-the-Apostles"
- **Oriental Orthodox and Eastern Catholic** churches also celebrate her

Churches dedicated to her include the **Cathedral of Santa Tecla** in Tarragona, Spain, where her silver-encased hand is carried in a three-day festival procession, and the **Monastery of Saint Thecla** (Ma'loula) in Syria, one of the few places where Western Aramaic is still spoken.

### Impact on Early Christian Women
Thecla was not merely an object of devotion -- she was a model for action. Historical women who explicitly followed her example include:

- **Eugenia of Rome**, who adopted Thecla's practice of cross-dressing to live as a monk.
- **Euphrosyne**, who lived disguised as a male monk for thirty-eight years.
- **Melania the Elder** (4th century), praised as a "second Thecla" for her extensive travels and ascetic life.

The text gave women a scriptural-adjacent precedent for preaching, traveling, and living independently -- which is exactly why church fathers like Tertullian worked so hard to discredit it.

### Art and Culture
Thecla appears in early Christian art from the fourth century onward. Frescoes in the catacombs of Rome depict her alongside canonical saints. Carved ivory panels, illuminated manuscripts, and church mosaics preserve her image across the Mediterranean world. The fifth-century *Life and Miracles of Thecla*, composed at Seleucia, expanded her legend with accounts of miraculous healings and divine appearances, establishing her shrine as a major healing center rivaling those of more established saints.

### Enduring Scholarly Interest
Modern scholars have made the *APTh* a key text in the study of gender and early Christianity. Debates continue over whether it represents authentic women's voices or male constructions of idealized femininity, whether it reflects real communities of celibate women or theological speculation, and how it functioned in the complex negotiations over authority in the second-century church.
