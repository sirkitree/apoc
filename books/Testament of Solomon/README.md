# Testament of Solomon

*A pseudepigraphal text in which King Solomon interrogates demons one by one, learns their secrets, and puts them to work building the Temple in Jerusalem.*

---

## Source & Manuscripts

The Testament of Solomon survives almost entirely in Greek. No Hebrew or Aramaic original has been identified, and the author's native language appears to have been Koine Greek. Apart from a single Syriac manuscript (Bib. Nat. Fonds Syriaque 194) and a single Arabic manuscript (Vat. ar. 448), both unpublished, every known witness to the text is Greek.

The manuscripts themselves date from the 15th through 17th centuries, but the material they preserve is far older. Scholars have identified multiple recensions -- distinct textual families that diverged as scribes copied, expanded, and rearranged the work over centuries.

**F. C. Conybeare** produced the first modern English translation in 1898, published in the *Jewish Quarterly Review*. This remained the standard for decades and is the version most widely available today. In 1922, **Chester Charlton McCown** published a critical Greek edition drawing on manuscripts from Mount Athos, Bologna, Holkham Hall, Jerusalem, London, Milan, Paris, and Vienna -- establishing the scholarly standard that organized the text into 26 chapters (130 sections). **Dennis C. Duling** later contributed a fresh English translation with commentary for James Charlesworth's *Old Testament Pseudepigrapha* (1983), along with an important 1988 retrospective essay reassessing the work's origins.

**Date of Composition:** The dating question is genuinely vexed. McCown argued for an early 3rd-century CE compilation that incorporated 1st-century Jewish material. Duling proposed a 3rd-century origin, likely from Alexandria, though he also entertained dates as late as the 5th or 6th century. The current scholarly consensus treats the Testament as a composite work whose primary strata date to the 1st through 3rd centuries CE, with later Christian interpolations and editorial layers added through the early medieval period. The text's blend of Jewish demonology, Hellenistic astrology, and Christian theology reflects this long accumulation.

## Historical Context

The Testament of Solomon sits at the intersection of several traditions that were very much alive in the late antique world:

**Jewish Solomonic Legend.** By the Second Temple period, Solomon had become far more than a wise king in Jewish imagination. He was a master of spirits, a healer, and an exorcist. Josephus (*Antiquities* 8.2.5) describes Solomon composing incantations for healing and exorcism, and claims to have witnessed a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar driving out demons using techniques attributed to Solomon. The Testament builds directly on this tradition, casting Solomon as the supreme authority over the demonic realm.

**Early Christian Demonology.** The text shows clear Christian editorial hands. Key passages reference the crucifixion, the virgin birth, and the name "Emmanouel" as having power over demons. A three-headed dragon spirit is thwarted by a reference to "the place of the skull" (Golgotha). These layers reveal how early Christians adopted and adapted Jewish demonological frameworks, reading Solomon's power over demons as a prefigurement of Christ's authority over evil.

**Hellenistic Magic and Astrology.** The Testament is saturated with Greco-Roman magical thinking. The 36 decans -- 10-degree portions of the zodiac -- appear as demons with animal heads (dogs, asses, oxen, birds), each responsible for specific diseases. The seven spirits connected to the Pleiades reflect Greek astronomical traditions. Names and practices drawn from Egyptian, Arabic, and broader Mediterranean magical traditions appear throughout, suggesting an author (or authors) deeply embedded in the cosmopolitan magical culture of the Eastern Mediterranean.

**Temple Construction Legends.** The organizing conceit -- that Solomon built the Temple using conscripted demons -- serves as a narrative frame for what is essentially a demonological encyclopedia. This legend had enormous staying power; variants appear in rabbinic literature, Islamic tradition (where *jinn* build Solomon's palace), and Ethiopian Christian texts.

## Canonical Status

The Testament of Solomon is **not considered canonical by any Jewish or Christian tradition**. It does not appear in the Hebrew Bible, the Septuagint, the Catholic deuterocanon, the Eastern Orthodox expanded canon, or any Protestant biblical canon. It is classified as an Old Testament pseudepigraphon -- a work attributed to a biblical figure but not accepted as scripture.

That said, it was not entirely ignored by ancient Christians. The *Dialogue of Timothy and Aquila* (c. 400 CE) cited it as an authority, suggesting some circles took it seriously. Its primary audience, however, was likely practitioners and students of the magical arts rather than theologians.

## Summary

The Testament is narrated in first person by Solomon himself, presented as a kind of deathbed memoir.

**The Ring of Power.** The story begins during the construction of the Temple in Jerusalem. A demon named **Ornias** has been tormenting a young laborer -- stealing half his wages and food, and sucking his thumb until the boy wastes away. Solomon prays for help, and the archangel **Michael** descends with a magical ring bearing the seal of God (the pentalpha or five-pointed star). With this ring, Solomon can summon, bind, and command any demon.

**The Parade of Demons.** What follows is the heart of the text: a long series of interrogations in which Solomon summons demons one by one and questions each about its name, nature, dwelling place, powers, zodiacal associations, and -- crucially -- which angel or divine name can thwart it. The demons are compelled to answer truthfully. Major figures include:

- **Ornias** -- the initial tormenting spirit, subdued by the archangel Ouriel
- **Beelzeboul** -- the prince of demons, who rules those bound in Tartarus and trembles before the name "Eloi"
- **Onoskelis** -- a seductive female demon with the legs of a mule
- **Asmodeus** -- familiar from the Book of Tobit, who murders and corrupts; thwarted by the angel Raphael and the smoke of burning fish liver and gall
- **The Seven Sisters** -- spirits connected to the Pleiades, elements of the *kosmokrator* (cosmic ruler of darkness)
- **Lix Tetrax** -- a wind demon thwarted by the archangel Azael
- **The Headless Murder Demon** -- all limbs, no head, who attacks infants; stopped only by lightning flashes
- **Obyzouth** -- a female demon who strangles newborns, with echoes of Lilith traditions; countered by the angel Raphael writing her name
- **Kunopegos** -- half horse, half fish, who causes seasickness and shipwrecks
- **Enepsigos** -- a shape-shifting female demon with "countless names," associated with Kronos
- **The Three-Headed Dragon** -- thwarted by references to the crucifixion at Golgotha

**The 36 Decans.** In a remarkable catalogue, the demons of the 36 decans of the zodiac appear as a group. Each has a distorted name, an animal-headed human form, and claims responsibility for specific ailments -- headaches, ear infections, sore throats, insomnia, bowel troubles. Each also provides the magical formula or angelic name by which it can be banished. This section reads almost like a medical-magical reference manual.

**The Temple Built by Demons.** Solomon puts his captive demons to work. They quarry stone, carry water, and perform superhuman labor to construct the Temple. The narrative transforms what might have been a simple exorcism manual into a grand mythological account of sacred architecture raised by the forced labor of evil itself.

**Solomon's Fall.** The text ends with Solomon's downfall. He falls in love with a Shunammite (or Jebusite) woman and, at the urging of the demon Error (one of the Seven Sisters), sacrifices to the pagan gods Raphan and Moloch. His glory departs. The ring loses its power. Solomon writes his testament as a warning, urging readers to guard against demonic temptation.

## Key Themes

**Demonology as Systematic Knowledge.** The Testament treats demons not as vague forces of evil but as specific, nameable entities with defined powers, weaknesses, and jurisdictions. Knowing a demon's name and nature is the key to defeating it -- a principle central to magical practice across many cultures.

**Angelic Hierarchy as Divine Counterweight.** For every demon, there is a thwarting angel. The text maps a cosmic order in which the demonic and angelic realms mirror each other -- Raphael counters Asmodeus, Ouriel subdues Ornias, Michael grants the ring of authority. This dualistic architecture profoundly influenced later angelology and demonology.

**Wisdom as Power Over Evil.** Solomon's authority comes not from military strength but from divinely granted knowledge. The ring is a tool, but the real weapon is the interrogation itself -- the systematic extraction of information. Wisdom, in this text, is literally demon-defeating.

**Astrology and the Body.** The 36 decans section connects celestial mechanics to human illness, placing the Testament squarely within the ancient tradition of iatromathematics (medical astrology). Each body part and ailment corresponds to a cosmic force, suggesting that healing requires understanding the heavens.

**The Fragility of Holiness.** Solomon, the greatest exorcist who ever lived, ultimately falls to the very forces he once commanded. The ending is a meditation on human weakness -- even supreme wisdom cannot protect against the corruptions of desire. This tragic arc gives the text unexpected moral depth.

## Influence & Legacy

The Testament of Solomon is the **foundation stone of the entire Solomonic magical tradition** -- one of the most enduring currents in Western esotericism.

**The Magical Treatise of Solomon (Hygromanteia).** This Byzantine-era Greek text, likely composed between the 6th and 13th centuries, directly incorporates and expands material from the Testament. It served as a crucial bridge between late antique magical practice and the Renaissance grimoire tradition.

**The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis).** The most famous of the medieval Solomonic grimoires, circulating from at least the 14th century, draws on traditions of Solomonic authority over spirits that originate in the Testament. It elaborates rituals, seals, pentacles, and invocations for summoning and commanding spirits.

**The Lesser Key of Solomon (Lemegeton).** This 17th-century compilation, whose first book is the celebrated *Ars Goetia* with its catalogue of 72 demons, is explicitly based on the Solomonic ring tradition. The structure -- summoning spirits, learning their names and natures, binding them to service -- directly echoes the Testament's interrogation scenes.

**Aleister Crowley and Modern Occultism.** Crowley and S. L. MacGregor Mathers published an influential edition of the *Goetia* in 1904, reinterpreting the demons as aspects of the unconscious mind. This psychological reading extended the Testament's legacy into 20th-century occultism, Thelema, and the broader Western magical revival.

**Rabbinic and Islamic Traditions.** Jewish midrashic literature preserves parallel stories of Solomon's mastery over demons (*Gittin* 68a-b in the Babylonian Talmud). In Islamic tradition, the Quran (Sura 34:12-14) and later commentaries describe Solomon commanding jinn -- a tradition that likely shares common roots with the Testament.

**Popular Culture.** The imagery of Solomon's ring, the binding of named demons, and the idea of a hidden catalogue of spirits have permeated fantasy literature, role-playing games, anime, video games, and horror fiction. Works as diverse as the manga/anime *Magi: The Labyrinth of Magic*, the *Shin Megami Tensei* video game franchise, and numerous entries in the urban fantasy genre draw -- directly or indirectly -- on the template the Testament established.
