# 1 Enoch (The Book of Enoch)

An ancient Jewish apocalyptic text attributed to the great-grandfather of Noah, offering a sweeping vision of fallen angels, cosmic journeys, divine judgment, and the origins of evil that profoundly shaped early Christianity and Second Temple Judaism.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

1 Enoch has one of the most fascinating textual histories of any ancient document. The complete text survives only in **Ethiopic (Ge'ez)**, preserved across roughly forty manuscripts held in Ethiopian monasteries and libraries. The Ethiopic version was translated from Greek, which was itself translated from the original **Aramaic**.

### Aramaic Dead Sea Scrolls Fragments

The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran transformed our understanding of 1 Enoch. **Qumran Cave 4** yielded fragments of **eleven Aramaic manuscripts** covering approximately one-fifth of the Ethiopic text. These fragments confirmed that the book was originally composed in Aramaic and demonstrated both the fidelity and the divergences of the Ethiopic translation from its Semitic originals. Notably, fragments from every section of 1 Enoch were found at Qumran **except** the Book of Parables (chapters 37-71), fueling ongoing scholarly debate about that section's date and origin. Nine additional Aramaic manuscripts of the related *Book of Giants* -- a text not included in 1 Enoch -- were also recovered.

### Greek Fragments

Several Greek fragments survive as well, including portions discovered at Akhmim in Egypt (the "Gizeh Greek fragments") and quotations preserved in the works of early Church Fathers such as Clement of Alexandria and Origen. A Greek fragment of chapters 1-32 was published from a codex found in a Christian grave in Akhmim, Egypt, in 1886-1887.

### Rediscovery and Translation History

The modern West rediscovered 1 Enoch through the Scottish explorer **James Bruce**, who traveled to Ethiopia in **1773** and procured three Ge'ez manuscripts. He deposited one at the French National Library in Paris, one at Oxford's Bodleian Library, and kept the third for himself (later also donated to the Bodleian). **Richard Laurence** produced the first English translation from the Ethiopic in **1821**. August Dillmann published a critical German translation in 1851.

The landmark English scholarly edition came from **R.H. Charles** (1855-1931), the great Irish biblical scholar. Charles published his Ethiopic critical text and English translation in **1893**, followed by a revised edition in 1906 and a further revision in **1912** as part of his monumental *Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha of the Old Testament* (1913). Charles's translation remains widely read and influential, though modern scholars have refined many of his readings in light of the Qumran evidence.

### Date of Composition

1 Enoch is a **composite text**, assembled from five originally independent works composed over several centuries:

| Section | Approximate Date |
|---|---|
| Astronomical Book (chs. 72-82) | 3rd century BCE (possibly the oldest section) |
| Book of Watchers (chs. 1-36) | 3rd-2nd century BCE |
| Book of Dream Visions (chs. 83-90) | c. 165-160 BCE (Maccabean period) |
| Epistle of Enoch (chs. 91-107) | 2nd-1st century BCE |
| Book of Parables (chs. 37-71) | Late 1st century BCE - 1st century CE |

The collection spans roughly **350 BCE to 50 CE**, making it a living document that evolved across some of the most turbulent and creative centuries in Jewish history.

## Historical Context

### Second Temple Judaism

1 Enoch emerged during the **Second Temple period** (516 BCE - 70 CE), one of the most intellectually fertile eras in Jewish history. After the Babylonian exile, Jewish communities grappled with fundamental questions: Why does evil persist if God is just? What happens after death? When will God intervene decisively in history? The various sections of 1 Enoch represent different communities' answers to these questions across several centuries.

### Apocalypticism

1 Enoch stands as one of the foundational texts of **Jewish apocalypticism** -- a worldview characterized by the revelation of divine secrets, the expectation of a dramatic cosmic transformation, the activity of angels and demons in human affairs, and a deterministic view of history moving toward a divinely ordained climax. While the biblical book of Daniel is the best-known canonical apocalypse, 1 Enoch is arguably the genre's most important and influential early exemplar.

### The Enochic Tradition

The figure of Enoch -- described in Genesis 5:24 with the tantalizing phrase "Enoch walked with God; then he was no more, because God took him" -- became the anchor for an entire tradition of revealed wisdom. The **Enochic tradition** represents a stream of Judaism that may have been distinct from (and sometimes in tension with) the Mosaic Torah tradition. Where the Torah traces authority through Moses at Sinai, the Enochic writings claim access to primordial wisdom revealed before the Flood. This tradition emphasized astronomy, angelology, the solar calendar, and cosmic geography, and it found a home among groups like the **Qumran community** (often identified with the Essenes), who treasured these texts.

## Canonical Status

### Ethiopian Orthodox Canon

1 Enoch holds the unique distinction of being considered **fully canonical Scripture** by the **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** and the **Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church**. In Ethiopia, the text has been continuously read, copied, and studied as sacred literature for over 1,500 years. It is part of the broader Ethiopian biblical canon, which also includes other texts like Jubilees that most Christian traditions do not accept.

### Quoted in the New Testament

The **Epistle of Jude** (verses 14-15) directly quotes 1 Enoch 1:9, attributing the words to "Enoch, the seventh from Adam." This is the only explicit quotation of a pseudepigraphal text in the New Testament presented as prophecy. The passage reads: *"See, the Lord is coming with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all."*

### Early Church Debates

Several early Church Fathers held 1 Enoch in high regard. **Tertullian** (c. 160-220 CE) argued for its inspired status. **Clement of Alexandria**, **Irenaeus**, **Justin Martyr**, **Origen**, **Cyprian**, and **Hippolytus** all quoted from or referenced the text. However, by the **fourth century**, as the Christian biblical canon solidified, 1 Enoch fell out of favor. **Athanasius**, **Jerome**, and **Augustine** excluded it from their canonical lists. The **Council of Laodicea** (c. 363-364 CE) did not include it among accepted books. Over time, the text was essentially lost to Western Christianity until Bruce's rediscovery in 1773, surviving only in the Ethiopian tradition.

## Summary of the Five Major Sections

### 1. The Book of Watchers (Chapters 1-36)

The most famous section of 1 Enoch opens with a theophany -- a vision of God's coming in judgment -- before turning to its central narrative: the fall of the **Watchers**, a group of angelic beings who abandon heaven to take human wives.

Led by **Semyaza** (also spelled Shemihazah), two hundred angels descend to **Mount Hermon** and swear an oath to carry out their transgression together. Their union with human women produces the **Nephilim**, a race of destructive giants who devour the earth's resources and turn violently against humanity. Meanwhile, another leader, **Azazel**, teaches humans the arts of warfare (swords, shields, breastplates) and vanity (cosmetics, jewelry, dyeing), corrupting civilization from within.

The cries of the devastated earth reach heaven. God dispatches archangels to enact judgment: **Raphael** binds Azazel and casts him into a pit of darkness; **Michael** pronounces doom on Semyaza and the Watchers, imprisoning them until the final judgment; and **Gabriel** incites the Nephilim to destroy one another.

The section concludes with Enoch's extraordinary **cosmic journeys**, guided by angels to the edges of the earth. He witnesses the prison of fallen stars, the mountain of the dead awaiting judgment, the fragrant tree of life reserved for the righteous, the gates through which winds blow, and a range of seven mountains in the northwest crowned by **the throne of God**. These passages constitute some of the earliest detailed descriptions of the geography of heaven and hell in Jewish literature.

### 2. The Book of Parables / Similitudes (Chapters 37-71)

The most theologically significant section for Christian readers, the Parables present three extended discourses revealing the secrets of heaven and the coming judgment.

The central figure is the **"Son of Man"** -- also called the **"Righteous One,"** the **"Chosen One,"** and the **"Anointed One" (Messiah)** -- a pre-existent heavenly being who sits on the **"throne of glory"** to judge the living and the dead. Kings and the mighty of the earth will fall before him; the righteous will dwell with him in eternal light. This is the first known use of "Son of Man" as a definite messianic title in Jewish literature, and its connection to Jesus's frequent self-designation in the Gospels has been debated by scholars for over a century.

The Parables also describe the hierarchy and names of the angels, reveal astronomical and meteorological secrets, and envision the final overthrow of evil. In a startling climax, **Enoch himself is identified with the Son of Man** (chapter 71), suggesting a tradition of human exaltation to divine status.

Because no fragments of the Parables were found at Qumran, some scholars date this section later than the rest (possibly 1st century CE), and a few have argued for Christian influence, though most now consider it a Jewish composition.

### 3. The Astronomical Book / Book of the Luminaries (Chapters 72-82)

Possibly the oldest section of 1 Enoch, the Astronomical Book is a detailed treatise on the movements of the sun, moon, and stars as revealed to Enoch by the angel **Uriel**.

The text describes a **solar calendar of 364 days** (52 weeks exactly), divided into four seasons of thirteen weeks each. This calendar -- which ensures that festivals always fall on the same day of the week -- stood in direct opposition to the **lunar calendar** used by the Jerusalem Temple establishment. The same solar calendar appears in the Book of Jubilees and was adopted by the Qumran community, suggesting that calendrical disputes were a major source of sectarian division in Second Temple Judaism.

Enoch observes the sun and moon passing through **twelve gates** in the heavens, learns the laws governing their rising and setting, and receives instruction about the winds, weather, and the relationship between celestial and terrestrial phenomena. Though its astronomical models are pre-scientific, the section reveals a genuine intellectual fascination with understanding the cosmos through systematic observation and divine revelation.

### 4. The Book of Dream Visions (Chapters 83-90)

This section contains two visionary dreams that Enoch experiences before his marriage.

The **first dream** (chapters 83-84) is a brief but terrifying vision of the earth's destruction by the Flood, prompting Enoch to pray for the preservation of a righteous remnant.

The **second dream**, known as the **Animal Apocalypse** (chapters 85-90), is a remarkable allegorical retelling of all human history from Adam to the messianic age. Humans are represented as animals: **Adam is a white bull**, Eve a heifer, Cain a black bull, Abel a red bull. Israel becomes a flock of **sheep**, surrounded by predatory wolves, lions, and other beasts representing the nations. The fallen Watchers appear as stars that mingle with cattle. The narrative traces the patriarchs, the Exodus, the monarchy, the exile, and the Second Temple period with increasing detail.

The allegory reaches its climax in the **Maccabean period** (the likely time of composition, c. 165-160 BCE): a **"great horn"** grows on one of the lambs -- almost certainly **Judas Maccabeus** -- who rallies the sheep against their oppressors. The vision concludes with God's final judgment, the building of a **new Jerusalem**, and the transformation of all creatures into **white cattle**, symbolizing the restoration of the original Adamic purity and the unity of all peoples.

### 5. The Epistle of Enoch (Chapters 91-108)

The final section frames itself as Enoch's farewell address to his children and to future generations of the righteous.

Its centerpiece is the **Apocalypse of Weeks** (found in chapters 93 and 91:12-17, likely displaced from their original order), which divides all of history into **ten "weeks"** (periods). The first seven weeks cover history from Enoch to the author's present; the final three describe the eschatological future, culminating in the destruction of the old heaven, the appearance of a **"new heaven,"** and an eternity of goodness and righteousness.

The Epistle also contains a powerful series of **"woe oracles"** against the wealthy and powerful who oppress the righteous -- language remarkably similar in form and spirit to the beatitudes and woes of Jesus in the Gospels (Luke 6:20-26). It affirms the reality of **post-mortem reward and punishment**, assuring the righteous that their suffering is temporary and that the souls of the dead are preserved for judgment.

The closing chapters (106-107) include a fragment about **Noah's miraculous birth** -- born with a body white as snow and hair white as wool, with eyes that lit up the room -- which his father Lamech initially feared was the offspring of the Watchers.

## Key Themes

### Fallen Angels and the Watchers

The Watcher myth is 1 Enoch's most distinctive and influential contribution. Rather than locating the origin of human sin in Adam and Eve's disobedience (as in later Christian theology), 1 Enoch presents a **supernatural origin of evil**: angelic beings who transgress cosmic boundaries by abandoning heaven, taking human wives, and teaching forbidden knowledge. This represents a profoundly different theodicy -- evil comes not from human free will alone but from a cosmic rebellion that corrupts creation itself.

### The Son of Man

The Parables introduce a pre-existent, heavenly messianic figure who judges the nations and vindicates the righteous. This "Son of Man" draws on imagery from **Daniel 7:13** ("one like a son of man" approaching the Ancient of Days) but develops it into a full messianic portrait. Whether Jesus drew directly on this tradition, or whether both Jesus and the Parables drew on a common pool of ideas, the parallels are striking and consequential.

### Eschatology and Final Judgment

Every section of 1 Enoch is saturated with the expectation of a **definitive divine judgment** -- on the fallen angels, on human sinners, and on the corrupt structures of the world. The text envisions resurrection, the separation of the righteous and the wicked, a transformed earth, and an eternal age of peace. These expectations formed the seedbed from which early Christian eschatology grew.

### Cosmic Geography

Enoch's guided tours of the universe provide some of the earliest detailed maps of the **unseen world** in Jewish literature: the chambers of the winds, the storehouses of snow and hail, the prison of fallen stars, the mountain of God, the tree of life, the abodes of the dead divided into compartments for the righteous and the wicked. This cosmic geography profoundly influenced later Jewish mysticism (Merkabah and Hekhalot literature) and Christian visions of heaven and hell.

### The Origins of Evil

1 Enoch offers multiple, layered explanations for why the world is broken. The Watchers' rebellion introduces violence and forbidden technology; Azazel's teachings corrupt civilization; the Nephilim's spirits become **demons** that plague humanity even after their physical destruction in the Flood. This demonology -- spirits of dead giants roaming the earth as evil spirits -- became the dominant framework for understanding demonic activity in the New Testament period.

## Influence & Legacy

### Influence on the New Testament

One scholar has observed that "the influence of 1 Enoch on the New Testament has been greater than that of all the other apocryphal and pseudepigraphal books taken together." Key points of influence include:

- **Jude 14-15** directly quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 as prophecy, and **Jude 6** alludes to the imprisoned Watchers.
- **2 Peter 2:4** references angels who sinned being cast into chains of darkness, echoing the Watcher tradition.
- **Jesus's use of "Son of Man"** as his primary self-designation in the Gospels resonates deeply with the Parables' portrait of a pre-existent heavenly judge, though the precise relationship remains debated.
- **Revelation** shares extensive imagery with 1 Enoch: the throne of God, the book of life, the lake of fire, the final judgment, the new heaven and new earth, and the white-robed righteous.
- The **Synoptic eschatological discourses** (Mark 13, Matthew 24-25, Luke 21) reflect apocalyptic frameworks developed in texts like 1 Enoch.
- The New Testament's robust **demonology** -- with evil spirits active on earth, subject to the authority of the Messiah -- builds on the Enochic understanding of demons as spirits of the Nephilim.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls Community

The Qumran community treasured 1 Enoch (with the possible exception of the Parables). The presence of eleven Aramaic manuscripts makes it one of the most frequently attested non-biblical texts at Qumran. The community adopted the **364-day solar calendar** described in the Astronomical Book, and their theology of cosmic warfare between the forces of light and darkness owes much to the Enochic tradition. Scholars have argued that the Enochic writings were among the foundational texts that shaped the Qumran community's identity, theology, and practice for centuries.

### Ethiopian Christianity

In the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, 1 Enoch is not a curiosity but **living Scripture**, read in liturgical contexts, studied by priests and scholars, and woven into the fabric of Ethiopian Christian theology and piety. Ethiopia's continuous preservation of the complete text -- when it was lost everywhere else -- represents one of the most remarkable stories of manuscript survival in human history. Ethiopian Christianity's unique character owes something to its engagement with texts like 1 Enoch and Jubilees that other Christian traditions set aside.

### Jewish Mysticism

The throne visions and cosmic journeys of 1 Enoch are recognized as precursors to the **Merkabah** (chariot/throne) mystical tradition and the later **Hekhalot** (heavenly palaces) literature. Scholar Gershom Scholem noted that the main subjects of later Jewish mysticism already occupy a central position in 1 Enoch.

### Medieval and Islamic Traditions

The figure of Enoch persisted across cultures: in Jewish tradition as **Metatron** (the transformed Enoch who becomes the highest angel, as elaborated in 3 Enoch); in Islamic tradition as the prophet **Idris**, mentioned in the Quran (19:56-57, 21:85) and identified with Enoch by most classical commentators. Medieval Jewish, Christian, and Islamic writers continued to engage with the Enochic tradition in various ways.

### Modern Popular Culture

1 Enoch has experienced a dramatic revival of popular interest in recent decades. Its vivid narratives of fallen angels, forbidden knowledge, giant offspring, and cosmic warfare have influenced contemporary fiction, film, television, and video games. The text has attracted attention from audiences ranging from biblical scholars to conspiracy theorists, and it regularly appears on bestseller lists in new translations and popular editions. Its themes of supernatural beings intervening in human affairs, hidden knowledge, and apocalyptic transformation continue to resonate with modern imaginations.

### A note on verse order

Verses in these files are **not always in numerical order**. Charles transposed verses he judged to be displaced in the Ethiopic, and this edition follows his arrangement rather than renumbering it. Chapters 60, 90 and 106 run out of sequence — 106, for instance, prints 106:17 between 106:14 and 106:15. Anything iterating over these files should read verse numbers rather than assume them; `books.json` records the affected chapters per file under `verseOrder`.

The markdown edition previously held here, `All_Books_of_Enoch.md`, was an unproofread OCR dump of an Internet Archive scan -- library circulation stamps, page furniture and broken characters interleaved with the text -- and it ran 1 Enoch, 2 Enoch and 3 Enoch together in a single file. It has been replaced by the files above. 2 Enoch now has its own clean markdown edition in [`books/2 Enoch/`](../2%20Enoch/). The old file remains in the repository's git history.
