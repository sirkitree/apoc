# 2 Esdras

Seven visions given to Ezra in Babylon after the fall of Jerusalem, in which a man argues with an archangel about why God permits the wicked to prosper -- and does not get a comfortable answer. The most searching theodicy in the whole apocryphal literature.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

**A composite book.** 2 Esdras as it stands in the King James Apocrypha is three works bound together, written at different times by different hands:

| Part | Chapters | Common name | Language of composition | Date |
|------|----------|-------------|------------------------|------|
| **5 Ezra** | 1--2 | Christian preface | Greek | 2nd century CE |
| **4 Ezra** | 3--14 | The Jewish apocalypse -- the core | Hebrew (lost), via Greek (lost) | c. 100 CE |
| **6 Ezra** | 15--16 | Christian appendix | Greek | 3rd century CE |

The core -- chapters 3--14, the seven visions, universally called **4 Ezra** in scholarship -- is a Jewish work written in the generation after the Roman destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. The Christian chapters were added at each end later, and are absent from the Eastern versions. Chapters 1--2 and 15--16 sometimes circulate separately in Latin manuscripts, which is part of how their independence was established.

**Original Language:** The core was almost certainly composed in **Hebrew**, translated into **Greek**, and from Greek into the versions that survive. Neither the Hebrew nor the Greek is extant -- an unusual situation for a text of this importance, and it means every reading depends on daughter translations.

**Key Versions and Manuscripts:**

- **Latin** -- the fullest and most important witness, and the basis of the King James translation. Codex Sangermanensis (822 CE) and Codex Ambianensis are principal manuscripts.
- **The "missing fragment."** A large section of chapter 7 (verses 36--105 in modern numbering) was cut out of an ancestor of most Latin manuscripts -- apparently because it denies that the living can pray effectively for the dead, a doctrine the medieval church held. A leaf had literally been excised from the codex behind the tradition. **Robert L. Bensly** recovered the passage in 1875 from Codex Ambianensis at Amiens, and it was published in 1875 and 1895. The King James Apocrypha, translated in 1611, **does not contain it**; the KJV text of chapter 7 jumps from verse 35 to what it numbers verse 36 (modern 7:106). Readers comparing the KJV with a modern edition should expect chapter 7's verse numbers to diverge sharply for this reason.
- **Syriac, Ethiopic, Georgian, Armenian, Arabic (two independent versions), Coptic** -- a remarkably wide translation history, and collectively essential for reconstructing the lost Greek.

**Date of Composition:** The core apocalypse is dated to about **100 CE**, near the end of the reign of Domitian, on the strength of the Eagle Vision. The eagle with three heads and twelve wings is Rome; the three heads are usually identified as **Vespasian, Titus and Domitian**, which places the writing shortly after the Flavians.

**Place of Composition:** Palestine is most likely, though the fictional setting is Babylon -- a transparent code, standard after 70 CE, for Rome.

## Historical Context

**Historical Setting:** The book is set "in the thirtieth year after the ruin of the city," with Ezra in Babylon lamenting the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. Nobody was meant to be fooled. The real subject is **70 CE**: the Roman siege, the burning of the Second Temple, the end of the sacrificial system, and the question that Jews of that generation could not avoid -- how a God who punished Israel by the hand of Babylon for its sins could now punish it by the hand of a Rome incomparably more wicked than Israel had ever been.

**The problem the book is trying to solve:** 4 Ezra is unusual, and arguably unique in the apocalyptic literature, in the seriousness with which it lets the objection stand. Ezra is not a straw man. He argues that God made humanity with an "evil heart" (*cor malignum*) and then held it responsible for what that heart does; that if the Law could not be kept, giving it was cruelty; that it would have been better for humanity never to have been created; and that a God who saves a handful and destroys the multitude is not obviously just. The angel **Uriel** answers him -- sometimes crushingly, sometimes evasively -- and the argument goes on for four full visions before anything resembling consolation arrives.

**Relation to other post-70 literature:** 4 Ezra belongs with **2 Baruch** (the Syriac Apocalypse), with which it shares so much material and outlook that literary dependence in one direction or the other is generally assumed. Both wrestle with the same catastrophe. 4 Ezra is by far the darker of the two.

## Canonical Status

| Tradition | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo** | Canonical, as *Ezra Sutuel*. The only tradition that receives it fully. |
| **Roman Catholic** | Not canonical. Excluded at Trent (1546), but printed in an appendix to the Clementine Vulgate as **4 Esdras**, together with 3 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasseh, so that it would not be lost. |
| **Eastern Orthodox** | Not in the Greek canon (there is no Greek text). It is included in the Slavonic Bible as **3 Esdras** and in the Russian Synodal Bible. |
| **Anglican / Lutheran** | Not canonical, but included in the Apocrypha. Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles names "The Fourth Book of Esdras" among the books read "for example of life and instruction of manners." |
| **Protestant (Reformed)** | Not canonical. Apocrypha. |
| **Jewish** | Not canonical, and not preserved in Jewish tradition -- the Hebrew original was lost entirely, and the book survived only because Christians copied it. |

Despite never being canonical in the West, 2 Esdras had an outsized liturgical afterlife: see **Influence & Legacy** below.

## Summary

### The Christian Preface: Chapters 1--2 (5 Ezra)

God, through Ezra, indicts Israel for its long history of ingratitude -- rehearsing the exodus, the wilderness, the prophets -- and announces that he will give "your houses to a people that shall come." The mother who bore them is bereft; another people will inherit. The tone then turns from indictment to promise, addressed to that coming people: *"Be ready to the reward of the kingdom, for the everlasting light shall shine upon you for evermore."* Ezra sees on Mount Zion a great multitude that cannot be numbered, singing, and a young man of tall stature setting crowns on their heads: these are they who have "confessed the Son of God in the world." The passage is straightforwardly Christian, and it is the clearest sign that these chapters are a later frame.

### The Seven Visions: Chapters 3--14 (4 Ezra)

**First Vision (3:1--5:20) -- Why is Babylon spared?**
Ezra, troubled in Babylon, lays the case before God. He traces the evil heart from Adam forward: God gave the Law, but did not remove the evil root, so the Law bore no fruit. Then the question -- *are the deeds of Babylon better than those of Zion?* The angel **Uriel** answers with three riddles: weigh me the weight of fire, measure me the blast of wind, call back a day that is past. Ezra cannot. Uriel's point is that a man cannot understand what he lives among, let alone the ways of the Most High. But Ezra presses: he asked about *this* world, not the next. Uriel concedes that the age is hastening to its end and gives signs.

**Second Vision (5:21--6:34) -- Why one people?**
Ezra fasts seven days and asks why God chose one vine, one lily, one river, one people -- and then handed that people to the many. Uriel replies with the parable of the forest and the sea, each planning to invade the other's territory and each stopped by its own boundary, and again turns Ezra's question back on him: *you cannot love the creation more than its creator.* Signs of the end follow, and the promise that "the beginning is manifest by the hand of man, and the end by mine own hand."

**Third Vision (6:35--9:25) -- The few and the many.**
The longest and most anguished exchange. Ezra asks: if the world was made for Israel's sake, why does Israel not possess it? Uriel answers with the doctrine of the two ages, and the image of the narrow entrance -- a city with a broad and glorious interior reached only by a path with fire on one side and deep water on the other. Ezra's reply is the heart of the book: it would have been better for the earth never to have produced Adam, or having produced him, to have restrained him from sinning.

This vision contains, in modern editions, the great excised passage (**7:36--105**): the vision of the pit of torment and the paradise of delight set opposite each other on the day of judgement; the description of the seven ways of torment for the wicked souls and the seven orders of joy for the righteous; and Ezra's plea that the righteous might intercede for the ungodly, which Uriel refuses -- *"the day of doom shall be the end of this time... then shall no man be able to save him that is destroyed, nor to oppress him that hath gotten the victory."* This passage is **absent from the King James text**, which was made before its recovery.

Ezra pleads for mercy for the many. Uriel's answer is that God rejoices over the few who are saved as a farmer rejoices over the seed that comes up, and does not grieve over the multitude that perish any more than over seed that fails. Ezra is not satisfied.

**Fourth Vision (9:26--10:59) -- The mourning woman.**
The turn. Ezra goes into a field and meets a woman weeping for her only son, who died on his wedding night after thirty years of barrenness and prayer. Ezra, still full of Zion's grief, rebukes her sharply: *your sorrow is one son; ours is a whole city and a whole people.* And then, mid-rebuke, the woman's face flashes like lightning, she cries out, and she is transformed into a city with vast foundations. Uriel explains: she was Zion. The barren thirty years were the years before Solomon's offerings; the son who died was Jerusalem. Ezra has spent three visions arguing that his grief is unanswerable, and has just been made to hear his own argument from the other side.

**Fifth Vision (11:1--12:51) -- The eagle.**
An eagle with twelve wings and three heads rises from the sea and reigns over the earth. Wings rise and vanish; the heads devour; the middle head is the greatest. A lion comes out of the wood and rebukes the eagle in a human voice, and the eagle is burned. Uriel identifies the eagle as the fourth kingdom of Daniel's vision -- **Rome** -- interpreted differently here than Daniel had it, and the lion as the Messiah of the line of David, who will judge and destroy the empire and deliver the remnant in mercy.

**Sixth Vision (13:1--58) -- The man from the sea.**
A man rises out of the heart of the sea and flies with the clouds. A multitude gathers to make war on him; he carves out a great mountain, stands on it, and destroys them not with weapon or spear but with a stream of fire from his mouth. Then he calls to himself another, peaceable multitude: the lost ten tribes, who had gone away into a far country called Arzareth to keep the Law in a place where no man dwelt.

**Seventh Vision (14:1--48) -- The restoration of the scriptures.**
A voice from a bush -- the same bush -- calls Ezra. He is told he will be taken up, and asks who will teach the people, since the Law is burned. He is given a cup of fiery liquid, and for forty days he dictates to five scribes who write in characters they do not know. **Ninety-four books** are written. Ezra is told to publish the **twenty-four** openly, "that the worthy and unworthy may read it," and to deliver the remaining **seventy** only to the wise, "for in them is the spring of understanding, the fountain of wisdom, and the stream of knowledge." Then Ezra is taken up.

The twenty-four are the books of the Hebrew Bible. The seventy are the esoteric literature -- and this passage is the classic self-justification of the entire apocalyptic tradition, explaining in advance why books like this one are not in the public canon.

### The Christian Appendix: Chapters 15--16 (6 Ezra)

Prophecies of war, famine and disaster against Babylon, Egypt, Assyria and Syria, in the manner of the Old Testament oracles against the nations, closing with an exhortation to the elect to endure: *"Be ye ready to strive... hear my words and receive them, and doubt not concerning the things whereof I speak."*

## Key Themes

### The Evil Heart
*Cor malignum* -- the "evil heart" or "grain of evil seed" sown in Adam -- is 4 Ezra's diagnosis of the human condition, and it is close to a doctrine of original sin. The book's version is bleaker than most: the evil heart was not removed when the Law was given, so the Law could convict but not cure. *"O thou Adam, what hast thou done? For though it was thou that sinned, thou art not fallen alone, but we all that come of thee."*

### Theodicy That Does Not Resolve
The distinguishing feature of 4 Ezra is that Uriel never actually answers Ezra's question, and the book seems to know it. What changes is not the argument but Ezra himself, and the change is worked by a vision -- the mourning woman -- rather than by a proof. Whether this is a failure of theodicy or a deliberate statement about the limits of theodicy is the central interpretive question about the book.

### The Two Ages
God has made not one world but two, and the sorrows of this one are the birth pangs of the next. This apocalyptic two-age scheme is the framework that allows Uriel to answer "why do the wicked prosper" with "wait." Ezra keeps refusing to be satisfied with it, which is precisely what makes the book interesting.

### The Few and the Many
*"There be many more of them which perish, than of them which shall be saved: like as a wave is greater than a drop."* The narrowness of salvation is stated more starkly here than almost anywhere in Jewish or Christian literature, and Ezra's repeated protests against it are the moral centre of the argument.

### Hidden Books and Public Scripture
The vision of the ninety-four books gives the apocalyptic tradition a charter: the public scriptures are true but partial, and the deeper knowledge is reserved. It is also the earliest clear statement of a **twenty-four book** Hebrew canon, which makes chapter 14 a significant witness for the history of the Jewish canon quite apart from its literary interest.

## Influence & Legacy

### *Requiem aeternam*
2 Esdras 2:34--35 -- *"Expecting the shepherd, he shall give you everlasting rest... **Requiem aeternitatis dabit vobis**"* -- is the source of the opening of the Roman Catholic Requiem Mass: **"Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine, et lux perpetua luceat eis"** ("Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them"). The line is also the source of the *Lux aeterna*. A book that no Western church accepted as canonical thus supplied the most-set, most-sung words in the entire Western liturgical repertoire -- through Mozart, Verdi, Fauré, Duruflé, Britten and the rest.

### Columbus and the Proportions of the Earth
2 Esdras 6:42 states that on the third day God gathered the waters into a seventh part of the earth and dried up six parts. **Christopher Columbus** cited this verse in his *Book of Prophecies* and in his letters to Ferdinand and Isabella as scriptural authority that the ocean was narrow and the land extensive -- support for the belief that Asia could be reached by sailing west. Cardinal Pierre d'Ailly's *Imago Mundi*, Columbus's heavily annotated source, had made the same use of the verse.

### Dante, Milton, and the Vision Tradition
The pit of torment and the paradise of delight, the seven orders of the righteous souls, and the intercession that comes too late all fed the medieval literature of the afterlife, directly or through intermediaries, and belong to the background of the *Divine Comedy*.

### The Lost Tribes
The sixth vision's account of the ten tribes departing to **Arzareth** to keep the Law "in a further country, where never mankind dwelt" became the foundational text for centuries of speculation about the lost tribes of Israel -- including, in the seventeenth century, the theory that the indigenous peoples of the Americas were their descendants.

### Modern Scholarship
Bensly's recovery of the missing fragment in 1875 is a celebrated episode in textual criticism, and the discovery of a deliberate excision -- doctrinally motivated censorship, visible as a cut leaf -- made it something more than a technical footnote. 4 Ezra is now a central document for the study of Judaism after 70 CE, of early Jewish thought about sin and the Messiah, and of the background to Paul's arguments in Romans about Adam, the Law and the divided self.
