# The Life of Adam and Eve / Apocalypse of Moses

A Jewish pseudepigraphal text that reimagines what happened to Adam and Eve after their expulsion from Eden -- their grief, their penitence, the origin of Satan's hatred for humanity, and the promise that death would not have the final word.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

No single "original" manuscript of this text survives. What we have instead is a family of related versions in multiple ancient languages, each preserving overlapping but distinct portions of the narrative. Scholars believe all of them descend from a lost original, likely composed in Hebrew or Aramaic.

The primary witnesses are:

- **Greek** -- Known as the *Apocalypsis Mosis* ("Apocalypse of Moses"), this version survives in approximately 27 manuscripts. It is the most widely studied recension and contains Eve's lengthy first-person account of the Fall, which is absent from the Latin.
- **Latin** -- Called the *Vita Adae et Evae* ("Life of Adam and Eve"), this is the most abundantly attested version with over 100 surviving manuscripts. It includes the penitence narrative (Adam standing in the Jordan, Eve in the Tigris) that does not appear in the Greek.
- **Armenian** -- Often titled the *Penitence of Adam*, this version uniquely preserves both the river-penitence episodes (shared with the Latin) and Eve's account of the Fall (shared with the Greek), making it especially valuable for reconstruction.
- **Georgian** -- Known as the *Book of Adam*, closely related to the Armenian version and sharing a common ancestor text (*Vorlage*). It provides important corroboration for readings found in the Armenian.
- **Slavonic** -- Largely follows the Greek Apocalypse of Moses but includes a section (chapters 28-39) found in the Latin but absent from the Greek, serving as a bridge between the two major traditions.
- **Coptic** -- Fragmentary witnesses that attest to the text's circulation in Egypt.

### Date of Composition

Most scholars date the original composition to **roughly 100 BCE - 70 CE**, placing it firmly within the Second Temple period. The key evidence for this dating is that the text appears to reference the Jerusalem Temple as still standing, and it lacks the theological vocabulary of later rabbinic literature. Leonhard Rost argued that the author may have lived in Palestine toward the end of the first century BCE. More recent scholars such as Jan Dochhorn have proposed a late first- or early second-century CE date based on parallels with Philo of Alexandria. The surviving Greek and Latin versions as we have them were likely compiled between the second and fourth centuries CE.

## Historical Context

This text belongs to the rich literary world of **Second Temple Judaism** (roughly 515 BCE - 70 CE), a period that produced an enormous body of writings that expanded, retold, and interpreted the Hebrew scriptures. Works like 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs all emerged from this same impulse: the desire to fill in the gaps that the terse biblical narratives left open.

Genesis devotes only a few verses to the expulsion from Eden and then moves on. The Life of Adam and Eve asks the questions that any careful reader of Genesis might ask: What did Adam and Eve actually *do* after they left the Garden? How did they survive? Did they feel remorse? What happened when they died?

The text also belongs to a broader tradition of **Adam literature** -- a constellation of writings across Jewish, Christian, and later Islamic traditions that placed Adam at the center of cosmic history. Other works in this tradition include the *Conflict of Adam and Eve with Satan* (a later Ethiopic/Arabic text), the *Cave of Treasures* (a Syriac chronicle), and various rabbinic *midrashim* that elaborate on Adam's creation and fall. The Life of Adam and Eve is the earliest and most influential of these expansions.

Leonhard Rost suggested the author may have had **affinities with Essene circles**, based on the text's ascetic features, particularly the strict physical separation of Adam and Eve during their penances. This remains speculative, but it underscores how deeply the text is rooted in the religious concerns of late Second Temple Judaism: sin, repentance, angelic hierarchies, and the hope for future redemption.

---

## Canonical Status

The Life of Adam and Eve is **not considered canonical by any major religious tradition** -- Jewish, Catholic, Orthodox, or Protestant. It is classified as Old Testament pseudepigrapha: writings attributed to biblical figures but not included in any standard biblical canon.

That said, the text was enormously popular. The sheer number of surviving manuscripts -- well over 100 in Latin alone, plus dozens in Greek, Armenian, Georgian, Slavonic, and Coptic -- testifies to its wide circulation throughout late antiquity and the medieval period. It was read, copied, and transmitted by Christian scribes for centuries, even though it was never granted scriptural authority. Some scholars have detected Christian interpolations in the text (particularly references to the "Son of God" and the promise of anointing with the "oil of mercy"), which may explain why Christian communities found it compelling enough to preserve even without canonical status.

## Summary

The narrative picks up where Genesis 3 leaves off. Adam and Eve have been expelled from the Garden of Eden and now face a world they do not understand.

### Hunger and Penitence

Unable to find the food they once had in Paradise, Adam and Eve decide to perform rigorous penance in the hope that God will forgive them and provide for their needs. Adam stands immersed in the **Jordan River for forty days**; Eve stands in the **Tigris River for thirty-seven days** (Adam assigns her fewer days, saying she is weaker). The imagery is striking -- two human figures standing neck-deep in cold rivers, crying out to God -- and it establishes the text's central concern with **repentance as a bodily, suffering act**.

### Satan's Deception and Backstory

While Eve is performing her penance, **Satan appears to her disguised as an angel of light** and convinces her to leave the river early, claiming that God has already accepted their repentance. Adam recognizes the deception immediately and confronts Satan, demanding to know why he persecutes them.

Satan's response is one of the most remarkable passages in all of ancient literature. He explains that when God created Adam in His own image, the archangel Michael commanded all the angels to **bow down and worship Adam**. Satan refused: *"I will not worship him who is lower and posterior to me. I am prior to that creature. Before he was made, I had already been made. He ought to worship me."* For this refusal, Satan and his followers were cast out of heaven. Satan tells Adam directly: *"All my enmity, jealousy, and resentment is towards you, since on account of you I was expelled and alienated from my glory."*

### Eve's Account of the Fall

In the Greek version (Apocalypse of Moses), Eve provides her own detailed **first-person account of the temptation in Eden** -- a narrative voice entirely absent from Genesis. She describes how the serpent approached her, how she ate the forbidden fruit, and how she gave it to Adam. This section gives Eve a narrative agency and psychological depth that the biblical account does not.

### Seth's Journey to Paradise

When Adam falls gravely ill, he gathers his children -- the text says he fathered thirty sons and thirty daughters -- and recounts the story of the Fall. He sends his son **Seth**, along with Eve, back to the gates of Paradise to beg for the **oil of the Tree of Mercy** (or the Tree of Life) that might heal him.

On the way, Seth is attacked and bitten by a wild beast, which withdraws when Seth commands it. When they reach Paradise, the archangel **Michael** meets them and refuses their request. However, Michael delivers a promise: the oil will be given **at the end of time**, when all flesh will be raised, the delights of Paradise will be restored to the holy, and God will dwell among His people. Some versions specify that God's Son will come to anoint all believers with this oil -- a passage widely regarded as a Christian addition to the text.

### Adam's Death and Heavenly Funeral

Adam dies, and what follows is an extraordinary **cosmic funeral scene**. Eve and Seth behold a chariot of light descending from heaven. The seven heavens open. God stretches out His hand and receives Adam's body, which is **washed three times** and then handed to the archangel Michael to be carried to the **Third Heaven**, where it will rest until the day of resurrection. God speaks to Adam's body: *"From dust you are; to dust you go"* -- but follows this with a promise of future resurrection.

### Eve's Death

Eve, after witnessing these things, instructs her children to inscribe the story of their parents' lives on tablets of stone and clay so that it will survive whatever judgment God sends upon the world. Shortly afterward, Eve dies and is buried beside Adam. Angels descend to attend her burial as well.

## Key Themes

### The Origin of Evil
The text offers one of the earliest and most developed accounts of **how evil entered the cosmos**. Evil does not begin with the serpent in the Garden -- it begins in heaven, with Satan's pride. The Fall of humanity is reframed as a secondary consequence of a prior angelic rebellion.

### Satan's Fall: Refusal to Worship Adam
The motif of Satan being commanded to worship Adam -- and refusing out of pride, insisting on his own prior creation -- is the theological centerpiece of the text. It provides a *motive* for Satan's hostility that Genesis never explains. Why does the serpent tempt Eve? Because Satan has a personal grievance against humanity itself.

### Penitence and the Suffering Body
The long penance scenes in the rivers are not incidental. They reflect a theology in which **repentance is visceral, physical, and prolonged**. The body that sinned must be the body that suffers in atonement. This theme resonated powerfully with early Christian ascetic movements.

### Death and the Promise of Resurrection
Adam's death is not portrayed as final. The elaborate heavenly funeral, the preservation of his body in the Third Heaven, and the explicit promise of future resurrection transform death from an ending into a **threshold**. The text insists that God's mercy extends beyond the grave.

### The Oil of Mercy
Seth's quest for the oil of the Tree of Life -- and Michael's refusal to grant it *now* but promise to grant it *at the end of days* -- encapsulates the text's eschatological hope. Healing, restoration, and the reversal of the Fall are coming, but they belong to a future age. The present is a time for endurance and penitence.

### Eve's Voice
Particularly in the Greek version, Eve is given a sustained, first-person narrative that is remarkable for ancient literature. She is not merely the object of blame but a **narrator of her own experience** -- a complex figure who grieves, repents, and takes responsibility.

## Influence & Legacy

### Milton's Paradise Lost
John Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667) -- perhaps the most influential retelling of the Fall in Western literature -- draws heavily on traditions found in the Life of Adam and Eve. Satan's grand speeches of defiance, his jealousy of humanity, and the dramatization of the angelic rebellion all echo themes first articulated in this ancient text. Milton was a formidable polyglot who served as Cromwell's Secretary for Foreign Tongues and had access to a vast range of sources. The portrait of Satan as a proud, eloquent rebel who insists on his own dignity even in defeat owes a clear debt to the Adam and Eve literature tradition.

### Islamic Tradition and Iblis
The Quran's account of **Iblis** (the Islamic name for Satan) refusing to prostrate before Adam bears a striking resemblance to the Life of Adam and Eve. In Surah 2:34, God commands the angels to prostrate before Adam; all comply except Iblis, who refuses. In Surah 7:12, Iblis justifies himself: *"I am better than he. You created me from fire, and him You created from clay."* This argument -- that prior or superior creation confers the right to refuse obedience -- parallels Satan's speech in the Life of Adam and Eve almost exactly. The intermediary tradition may be the Syriac *Cave of Treasures*, which specifies the fire-versus-clay distinction. Scholars have long noted these parallels as evidence of shared Second Temple Jewish traditions that flowed into both Christian and Islamic thought.

### Christian Theology of the Fall
The text shaped how early Christians understood the mechanics of the Fall. The idea that **Satan's rebellion preceded and caused human sin** -- rather than the two being independent events -- became standard in Christian theology. Paul's reference to Satan disguising himself as "an angel of light" (2 Corinthians 11:14) has been connected by scholars to the Life of Adam and Eve's depiction of Satan deceiving Eve in exactly this form during her river penance. The text's emphasis on Adam as a figure of cosmic significance also fed into Pauline theology of Christ as the "second Adam" (Romans 5:12-21, 1 Corinthians 15:22).

### Medieval Adam Literature
The Life of Adam and Eve spawned a rich tradition of derivative works across multiple cultures: the Ethiopic and Arabic *Conflict of Adam and Eve with Satan*, the Syriac *Cave of Treasures*, various Irish and Anglo-Saxon Adam narratives, and medieval dramatic traditions (mystery plays) that staged the Fall and its aftermath. The text's narrative framework -- expulsion, penitence, Satan's backstory, death, and the promise of redemption -- became the standard template for retelling Adam's story throughout the medieval world.
