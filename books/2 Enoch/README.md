# 2 Enoch (The Book of the Secrets of Enoch)

A pseudepigraphal apocalypse in which the patriarch Enoch ascends through ten heavens, receives the secrets of creation from God face to face, and returns to earth to pass on his wisdom before being taken up forever.

---

## Source & Manuscripts

2 Enoch survives in its complete form only in Church Slavonic (Old Bulgarian), preserved across more than twenty manuscripts and fragments dating from the 14th to 18th centuries CE. In 2009, Coptic fragments were also identified, breaking the long-held assumption that it was a "Slavonic only" text. The key complete manuscripts include A, U, B, and R (which contain all 73 chapters), while others such as J (through chapter 71) and P, N, V, and B2 preserve only parts of the work.

### The Recension Problem

The manuscripts divide into two main families: a **longer recension** (represented by manuscripts R, J, and P) and a **shorter recension** (represented by U, A, B, V, N, B2, and L). Early editors assumed the longer version was original, but modern scholarship recognizes that both recensions preserve original material, and neither can be dismissed as simply an expansion or abbreviation of the other. Some scholars have even proposed three or four distinct recensions. The relationship between them remains one of the thorniest textual problems in pseudepigrapha studies.

### Original Language

Most scholars believe the Slavonic text was translated from one or more lost Greek versions, since certain wordplay and linguistic features only make sense in Greek. However, Semitic terms and Hebraisms -- particularly in the shorter recension -- suggest that a Hebrew or Aramaic source may lie behind the Greek. Direct translation from Hebrew into Slavonic is considered unlikely before the 15th century, making a Greek intermediary almost certain.

### Date of Composition

Dating 2 Enoch is notoriously difficult, with proposals ranging from the 1st century BCE to the 10th century CE. The most widely held scholarly view places the original composition in the **late 1st century CE, before the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE**. The text references ongoing Temple practices, sacrificial customs, and pilgrimage as current realities -- it never mourns the Temple's loss, which would be almost unthinkable for a post-70 Jewish author. The Slavonic translation itself likely dates to the medieval period, with Christian scribes introducing some modifications during transmission.

### Key Editions

The first English translation was produced by **W. R. Morfill**, edited by **R. H. Charles**, in 1896. The critical Slavonic edition by **A. Vaillant** appeared in 1952. The standard modern English translation is by **F. I. Andersen**, published in J. H. Charlesworth's *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, volume 1 (1983), pages 91-221. Andersen rejected the Morfill-Charles "eclectic text" in favor of a 16th-century Bulgarian manuscript from the Library of the Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg as his primary witness.

## Historical Context

### Relationship to 1 Enoch

Despite sharing a patriarch, 2 Enoch is a distinct work from 1 Enoch (the Ethiopic Book of Enoch). Where 1 Enoch is a composite text assembled over centuries (3rd century BCE to 1st century CE), 2 Enoch appears to be a more unified composition. It is deeply rooted in the Enochic tradition of Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic literature, but it reshapes those traditions in significant ways. The figure of Enoch in 2 Enoch is more elaborate and exalted than in 1 Enoch -- he undergoes a physical transformation into an angelic being, prefiguring the supreme angel Metatron who would appear centuries later in 3 Enoch (the Hebrew Book of Enoch) and in rabbinic Merkabah mysticism.

### Jewish or Christian?

The question of whether 2 Enoch is a Jewish or Christian text has generated substantial debate. Some scholars have attributed it to early Christians or to an unknown Jewish sect, and the Slavonic manuscripts certainly contain Christian interpolations introduced by later copyists. However, the scholarly consensus has increasingly shifted toward **Jewish authorship**, likely from **Hellenistic Jewish circles in Alexandria, Egypt**. The text's Adamic traditions parallel those found in Philo of Alexandria and the Sibylline Oracles, and its theological concerns align with Greek-speaking Jewish communities who synthesized biblical tradition with Hellenistic philosophical influences. Christian elements (such as possible Trinitarian allusions in chapter 19) are generally regarded as later additions during the text's transmission history.

---

## Canonical Status

2 Enoch is **not considered canonical scripture by any major Jewish or Christian tradition**. Unlike 1 Enoch, which is part of the biblical canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, 2 Enoch holds no canonical status anywhere. It is classified as Old Testament pseudepigrapha -- a work attributed to a biblical figure but written much later. It was preserved primarily by Slavic Orthodox Christian scribes who valued it as edifying literature rather than scripture. Its significance lies not in canonical authority but in the window it opens onto the rich, imaginative world of Second Temple Jewish thought.

## Summary

The text divides into three major sections:

### Part I: The Heavenly Ascent (Chapters 1-38)

At the age of 365, Enoch is visited by two enormous angels who carry him up through the heavens. Each heaven reveals new wonders:

- **First Heaven**: The realm of atmospheric phenomena -- storehouses of snow, rain, dew, and the waters above the firmament, all governed by angels.
- **Second Heaven**: A place of darkness where fallen angels hang in chains, awaiting final judgment. They beg Enoch to pray for them.
- **Third Heaven**: Split into two regions. The northern part is **Paradise**, an astonishingly beautiful garden prepared for the righteous -- those who feed the hungry, clothe the naked, lift up the fallen, and help orphans. The southern part is a place of terrible torment for the wicked.
- **Fourth Heaven**: The courses of the sun and moon are revealed, along with detailed astronomical descriptions and a 364-day calendar system.
- **Fifth Heaven**: Home of the **Watchers (Grigori)**, giant silent beings mourning their fallen brothers -- the angels led by Satanail who descended to Mount Hermon, took human wives, and defiled the earth (a tradition shared with 1 Enoch and Genesis 6).
- **Sixth Heaven**: Seven bands of radiant angels -- phoenixes, cherubim, and six-winged seraphim -- who govern the cosmos and lead all creation in worship.
- **Seventh Heaven**: Enoch glimpses the Lord on his throne from afar, surrounded by fiery armies of archangels and heavenly hosts.
- **Eighth Heaven**: Called Muzaloth, the realm of the zodiac constellations and the changer of seasons, set just below the upper firmament.
- **Ninth Heaven**: The heavenly houses of the zodiacal signs.
- **Tenth Heaven**: Enoch stands before **God's face** itself. The archangel Michael strips away his earthly garments, anoints him with holy oil, and clothes him in garments of glory. Enoch is **transformed**, becoming like one of the angels in appearance.

God then reveals to Enoch the **secrets of creation** -- an extraordinary cosmogony unlike anything in Genesis. Before the visible world existed, God brought forth a primordial being called **Adoil**, who carried a great light in his belly. At God's command, Adoil disintegrated and released all creation. Then God summoned **Arukhas**, a being of darkness, who became the foundation of the lower things. From these two primal forces -- light and darkness -- God fashioned the entire cosmos. The angel **Vereviel** (or Pravuil) is commanded to dictate 360 books to Enoch, containing everything that is knowable.

### Part II: Enoch's Instructions to His Sons (Chapters 39-67)

Returned briefly to earth, Enoch gathers his sons and delivers extended ethical and theological teachings. The moral vision is striking in its simplicity and universality:

- Love all living beings.
- Feed the hungry, clothe the naked, help the injured and orphans.
- Do not swear falsely or oppress the poor.
- Do not conceal your silver in the earth but use it to help the needy.
- Endure affliction with patience: longsuffering, meekness, faithfulness, truth, and hope.

These instructions include a series of **beatitudes** ("Blessed is he who...") that bear remarkable resemblance to the Sermon on the Mount in Matthew 5. Notably, the Law of Moses is never referenced -- the ethical framework is grounded in natural law and the order of creation rather than Torah observance. The sin of fornication, so prominent in other Second Temple literature, receives little attention.

### Part III: The Melchizedek Narrative (Chapters 68-73)

The final section shifts dramatically in style from ethical exhortation to prose narrative. Enoch is no longer the central figure. Instead, the story follows **Methusalem** (Methuselah), **Nir** (a priest and brother of Noah), and the miraculous birth of **Melchizedek**.

Nir's wife Sopanim, though elderly and barren, is found to be pregnant. She dies, and from her body a child is born already bearing the **badge of priesthood** on his chest. This is Melchizedek -- born miraculously, outside the normal course of nature. The archangel Gabriel takes the child to Paradise to preserve him through the coming Flood, establishing him as the head of a new priestly line.

This section reflects broader Second Temple fascination with the mysterious figure of Melchizedek from Genesis 14:18 and Psalm 110:4 ("You are a priest forever, in the order of Melchizedek") -- a tradition also developed extensively in the Epistle to the Hebrews (5:6-10; 7:1-17) and in a fragmentary Melchizedek text found among the Dead Sea Scrolls (11Q13). Because of stylistic differences, many scholars believe chapters 68-73 may derive from a separate source that was incorporated into 2 Enoch at an early stage. Some manuscripts omit this section entirely, possibly because the miraculous virgin-like birth of Melchizedek was considered scandalous by later Christian copyists.

## Key Themes

### Heavenly Ascent
2 Enoch is one of the most elaborate heavenly journey texts from antiquity. The ten-heaven cosmology (expanding the more common seven-heaven model) provides a detailed map of the universe as imagined by its author -- a cosmos teeming with angels, governed by precise astronomical mechanisms, and culminating in the overwhelming presence of God.

### Cosmology and Creation
The Adoil-Arukhas cosmogony is one of the most original features of 2 Enoch. It presents creation not as speech alone ("Let there be light") but as the disintegration and transformation of primordial beings. This has been compared to the Logos theology of the Gospel of John's prologue, and scholars like Andrei Orlov have explored the parallels between Adoil and Johannine light imagery.

### Transformation and Theosis
Enoch's anointing and transformation into an angelic being -- becoming "like one of the glorious ones" with no visible difference -- is a powerful image of human divinization. This theme connects 2 Enoch to broader currents in Second Temple Judaism that envisioned the exaltation of righteous humans to heavenly status.

### Ethical Teaching
The moral instructions are remarkably universal and humane, focused on compassion, generosity, and care for the vulnerable. The beatitude-like formulas and the emphasis on love for all living beings give this section a warmth that contrasts with the cosmic spectacle of the heavenly ascent.

### The Melchizedek Tradition
The miraculous birth narrative places 2 Enoch within a web of traditions about this enigmatic priest-king that stretches from Genesis through the Psalms, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Epistle to the Hebrews, and into later Jewish and Christian mysticism.

## Influence & Legacy

### Merkabah Mysticism
2 Enoch stands at a critical juncture between early Jewish apocalypticism and the later **Merkabah (chariot throne) mystical tradition** that flourished in rabbinic Judaism. Enoch's vision of God's throne, his angelic transformation, and his reception of divine secrets anticipate the central concerns of the Hekhalot literature (palace/temple mystical texts). The portrayal of Enoch as a supreme angelic figure directly prefigures the identification of Enoch with **Metatron**, the "Prince of the Presence," in 3 Enoch and later Kabbalistic tradition.

### Paul's "Third Heaven"
In 2 Corinthians 12:2-4, the apostle Paul describes being "caught up to the third heaven" and into "Paradise," where he heard "inexpressible things." This language maps precisely onto the cosmology of 2 Enoch, where the third heaven contains Paradise. Whether or not Paul knew this specific text, he was clearly drawing on the same tradition of heavenly ascent and multi-layered heavens that 2 Enoch represents. The parallel has led scholars to argue that Paul's mystical experience should be understood within the framework of early Jewish ascent traditions.

### Medieval Cosmology
The layered cosmological model of 2 Enoch -- with its concentric heavens, astronomical mechanisms, and angelic hierarchies -- corresponds closely to medieval Christian and Islamic conceptions of the universe. The text likely influenced the development of these cosmological frameworks, particularly in Slavic Christian communities where the manuscripts circulated. Dante's *Paradiso*, while not directly dependent on 2 Enoch, reflects a similar tradition of graded heavens leading to the ultimate vision of God.

### The Johannine Prologue
Andrei Orlov and other scholars have drawn attention to striking parallels between 2 Enoch's Adoil cosmogony and the Prologue of the Gospel of John ("In the beginning was the Word... and the light shines in the darkness"). Both texts associate a primordial luminous figure with the origins of all creation. Whether these represent direct literary dependence or a shared tradition remains debated.
